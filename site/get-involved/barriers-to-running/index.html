Barriers to running

Anyone who meets the age, citizenship and residency rules can run for Congress, but each state decides what it takes to get a name printed on the ballot. This page shows the filing fees, petition signatures and deadlines in every state, with the same rules shown for every party.

No fee for U.S. House18 states

In these states a party candidate pays nothing to file. Signatures or a party nomination are the barrier instead.

Highest fee for U.S. HouseFlorida, $10,440

Georgia is next at $5,220. Both are a share of the $174,000 yearly salary of a member of Congress.

Fees found in FEC filings285 payments

Campaigns in 24 states reported paying a filing, qualifying or ballot fee to a state election office or party.

An example: Florida

Florida sets the qualifying fee as a share of the yearly salary of the office. For Congress that salary is $174,000. A candidate running with a party pays three parts:

$5,2203% filing fee
$1,7401% election assessment
$3,4802% party assessment
$10,440Total, 6% of salary

A candidate with no party pays 4%, which is $6,960. A candidate who does not want to pay can instead turn in petition signatures from 1% of the registered voters in the area (2,564 for a U.S. House district in 2026), due weeks before qualifying.

The FEC records show the fee being paid. One example: Belinda Keiser for Congress reported paying $10,440 to the Florida Department of State on May 20, 2026, for a "qualifying fee". See the FEC filing ↗ · See it in the Money web

What the Constitution requires

The Constitution sets the only qualifications to serve in Congress. States cannot add more, but they do set the rules for getting on the ballot.

U.S. HouseAt least 25 years old

A U.S. citizen for at least 7 years, and living in the state when elected. Article I, Section 2 ↗

U.S. SenateAt least 30 years old

A U.S. citizen for at least 9 years, and living in the state when elected. Article I, Section 3 ↗

The Supreme Court held in 1995 that states cannot add their own qualifications for Congress. U.S. Term Limits v. Thornton ↗ Governor and state legislature qualifications are set by each state and are not covered here yet.

How this was checked

  • Congress, all 50 states. Each state's numbers were read on the state's own election office page, fee list or law where it could be opened, and compared with a second source (Ballotpedia). 33 states were checked on the official page, 5 partly, and 11 come from the second source only because the official page could not be read here. In 1 state (Louisiana) the sources disagree, and the card says how.
  • Fees paid. FEC records in our Money web where a House or Senate campaign paid a filing, qualifying or ballot fee to a state election office or party. Late fees, fines and annual fees are left out. The amount, date and wording are as the campaign reported them.
  • Governor and state legislature. From the second source only, except the Florida fees, which were read on Florida's official list. These rows have not been checked on each state's own page yet.
  • Not covered yet. Notary rules, residency rules for state offices, rules for write-in candidates in most states, and the District of Columbia.
  • Same for every party. Each card shows the rules for party candidates and for candidates without a party side by side. Rules change; the official links on each card show the current version.

Last checked November 27, 2026. Deadlines are for the 2026 election unless a row says otherwise.
