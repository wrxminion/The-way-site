Amendment 2: State property taxBan a state property tax

Legislative referral (constitutional amendment): HJR 81 (113th General Assembly) and SJR 1 (114th General Assembly), placed on the ballot by the Legislature as Senate Joint Resolution 1 (2025), first passed as House Joint Resolution 81 ↗. Passes with more YES than NO votes, and YES votes greater than half of all votes cast for governor.

Read the full text of the measure: Secretary of State's constitutional amendment page ↗

In plain words

Your property tax bill today goes to your county and city, not the state. The state stopped taxing property in 1949. This amendment adds words saying the Legislature "shall not levy, authorize, or otherwise permit any state tax upon such property." Nothing changes in your bill now. A future state property tax would need another constitutional amendment, which takes two votes of the Legislature (the second by two-thirds) and a statewide vote. (ballotpedia.org ↗)

Example

Illustration only: a family whose county tax bill is $1,500 a year would still pay that $1,500 to the county either way. What changes is that the state could not add its own tax on top of it unless voters first changed the constitution again. (ballotpedia.org ↗)

Biggest change

  • Bars the Legislature from levying, authorizing or permitting any state tax on real, personal or mixed property. source: ballotpedia.org

Who it affects most: Property owners, and future state budgets, since one way to raise money would be closed off.

Cost to the state: No change to current state revenue, because there is no state property tax today. No official cost estimate was found. Sen. Heidi Campbell says the state "could lose millions in revenue" in the future.

What your vote means

YES

The constitution would ban any state property tax; county and city property taxes stay.

NO

There is still no state property tax, but lawmakers could create one by passing a law.

Our one-line summary. Tennessee does not publish an official "what your vote means" statement; the ballot title above is the official description.

Why people want it, and why others object

Why this is on the ballot

The Legislature put this on the ballot. It changes the state constitution, so only another statewide vote can change it back. It passes with more YES than NO votes, and YES votes greater than half of all the votes cast in the governor's race.

  • Tennessee repealed its general state property tax in 1931 and set the education property tax rate to zero in 1949, two years after adopting a state sales tax. source: ballotpedia.org
  • Final 2025 votes: Senate 26-5, House 89-6. source: news.ballotpedia.org

Why supporters want it

  • Sen. Ferrell Haile (R): "There may always be a temptation when things get tight not to reduce spending but to increase taxes." Haile says property tax should be left to counties and cities. source: tennesseelookout.com
  • Rep. Tandy Darby (R): "This amendment ensures that the state cannot impose property taxes again." source: ballotpedia.org
  • Tennessee Stands says it "strengthens protections for property owners." source: news.ballotpedia.org

Why opponents object

  • Sen. Heidi Campbell (D): "There's really no need for a constitutional amendment," since the state hasn't taxed property for decades, and "we could lose millions in revenue." Campbell also warned about the state's bond rating. source: ballotpedia.org
  • Rep. John Ray Clemmons (D), House Democratic Caucus chair, said Republicans should put as much effort into lowering the cost of living as into amending the constitution. source: tennesseelookout.com

Who backs each side

  • For: a "Yes on 2" campaign chaired by Gov. Bill Lee, with regional chairs House Speaker Cameron Sexton, Rep. Tandy Darby and Sen. Ferrell Haile, and backed by Tennessee Farm Bureau, Tennessee REALTORS, the Tennessee Chamber of Commerce, NFIB, Americans for Prosperity and the Beacon Center. Against: no organized campaign found. No campaign money totals were found in public reports we checked. source: tennesseelookout.com
