Amendment 85Plain Language Ballot Titles

Placed on the ballot by citizen initiative • Passes with 55 percent of the vote. Blue Book page 27 (the printed page number; your PDF viewer's page count runs higher).

In plain words

A ballot title is the short description of a measure that voters read on the ballot. Amendment 85 would require every ballot title to be in plain language, at no more than an eighth-grade reading level. It would also block laws that require set wording in the titles of citizen-proposed measures, if that wording gets in the way of plain language. source: Blue Book, p. 27

Biggest change

  • Laws that require set wording in citizen initiative titles, such as wording about tax changes, could be blocked if they get in the way of plain language. source: Blue Book, p. 27
  • All ballot titles would have to be at an eighth-grade reading level or lower. The measure does not define plain language or say who decides if a title meets the standard. source: Blue Book, p. 27

Who it affects most: The state Title Board, which writes titles for citizen initiatives, people who propose initiatives, and voters who read the titles.

Cost to the state: Unknown. The Blue Book does not give an amount. It says the Title Board's workload may go up a small amount.

What your vote means

YES

Every ballot title must be at an eighth-grade reading level or lower, and laws cannot require initiative wording that gets in the way.

NO

Titles must still be in plain language under state law, with no set reading level, and laws requiring extra wording, such as for tax changes, stay.

The Blue Book's official wording of YES and NO (tap to open)

YES: A “yes” vote on Amendment 85 requires that ballot titles be written in plain language, defined as an eighth-grade reading level, and prohibits any law from interfering with this requirement.

NO: A “no” vote on Amendment 85 maintains current law, which requires plain language for ballot titles, but does not specify a particular grade-level reading standard.

Why people want it, and why others object

Why this is on the ballot

  • Citizens put this on the ballot by gathering voter signatures (a citizen initiative). It would change the Colorado Constitution, the state's highest set of laws, which only a statewide vote can change. It needs 55 percent of the vote to pass. source: Blue Book, p. 27
  • Before people can gather signatures for a citizen initiative, the state Title Board writes its ballot title. State law already says titles must be brief, plain, and avoid legal or technical terms when possible, but it sets no reading level. source: Blue Book, p. 27

Why supporters want it

  • While ballot titles are supposed to be written as plainly as possible, this measure provides additional guidance as to what that means by requiring an eighth-grade reading level as the standard. Providing a constitutional standard will help in crafting simple titles that voters may better understand when considering their vote, and may help them to navigate complicated and technical information when trying to understand a measure’s impact. source: Blue Book, p. 27
  • The measure creates a constitutional protection against the state legislature interfering with plain language in initiative ballot titles. Citizen initiatives are a way for the public to pass laws without restrictions created by the legislature. This measure ensures that no future legislature may add laws or change existing laws to create complicated or confusing initiative ballot titles. source: Blue Book, p. 27

Why opponents object

  • This measure is unnecessary as state law already provides ample guidance for the preparation of ballot titles in plain language. Ballot titles must convey important information to voters about complex policies. This measure creates a constitutional standard with no guidance about how its requirements are different from current law. This could lead to more challenges of initiative ballot titles, impacting the initiative process. source: Blue Book, p. 27
  • This measure puts vague and undefined language in the constitution, which could prevent the creation of laws to help voters better understand the effects of ballot measures. Laws that add language to ballot titles could be prohibited, even if that language provides critical information for voters. The measure may lead to challenges of existing laws that help voters understand how their vote affects state and local programs and funding. source: Blue Book, p. 27

  • No new tax, no change in state revenue and no local government effect is listed. (Blue Book, p. 27 ↗)

If it passes

All ballot titles must be in plain language at no more than an eighth-grade reading level. Laws requiring set wording in citizen initiative titles would be barred if they interfere with that standard. Titles for measures referred by the legislature are not covered by that ban. The measure does not define plain language or say who decides whether a title meets the standard. Because it is a constitutional amendment, changing it later would take another statewide vote.

If it fails

Current law stays. The Title Board must write brief, plain language titles understandable by the widest possible audience, but no grade level is set. Laws requiring extra title wording, such as for tax changes, stay in place.

Not yet known

  • Which current title wording laws would no longer apply. The Blue Book gives examples but no full list.
  • Who decides if a title meets the eighth-grade standard, and how it is measured.
  • Whether there would be more legal challenges to ballot titles, and any cost.
  • When the measure takes effect. The Blue Book does not say.

Can it actually be enforced?

Short answer: Hard to enforce in practice. It sets a standard (eighth-grade reading level) but does not define it or say who checks. Courts would likely end up deciding what it means.

  • It is a constitutional amendment, so the legislature could not repeal it or pass laws that conflict with it. (Blue Book, p. 27 ↗)
  • Who enforces it: the Blue Book says the measure does not define plain language or say who decides whether a title meets the standard. The Title Board would have to set its own standards. Disputes would likely go to court, which the Blue Book says could mean more title challenges. (Blue Book, p. 27 ↗)
  • It would override some state laws. Several current laws require specific wording in initiative titles, such as tax-change and spending-impact language. Those laws could be challenged if they interfere with plain language. A court would decide which ones fall. (Blue Book, p. 27 ↗)
  • Limited reach: it covers citizen initiatives. Titles written by the legislature for its own referred measures are not covered by the ban on required wording. (Blue Book, p. 27 ↗)
  • No federal issue: ballot titles for state measures are a state matter. We found no federal law that would override it. (Blue Book, p. 27 ↗)

Not yet known

  • How it fits with wording already required by the Colorado Constitution itself (for example TABOR's rules for tax questions). The amendment targets 'laws', and the Blue Book does not say whether constitutional wording rules are affected. We did not verify this.

This is our reading of the cited law, not legal advice. Court outcomes are uncertain.

Amendment 85 proposes amending the Colorado Constitution to:

  • require that ballot titles be in plain language, written at no more than an eighth-grade reading level; and
  • prohibit laws that require specific language in initiative ballot titles, if the laws interfere with plain language in the ballot title.

Summary and Analysis of Amendment 85

How do measures appear on the ballot and how are ballot titles written?

A person may propose a new law or change to existing laws through the initiative process. Through this process, the change may be placed on the ballot if the person collects enough signatures from registered voters.

Before collecting signatures, every initiative receives a ballot title written by the state Title Board. The board is made up of representatives from the Attorney General’s office, the Secretary of State’s office, and the legislature’s legal office. The ballot title is a brief statement intended to fairly and accurately represent the intent and meaning of an initiative.

State law requires that the Title Board write ballot titles that are brief and help voters understand the effect of a “yes” or “no” vote on the measure. The board must write titles in plain language, that are understandable by the widest possible audience, and that avoid legal, technical, or specialized terminology, when possible.

The state legislature may also refer measures to the ballot. When the legislature refers a measure, the legislature writes the ballot title. State law requires that bills and bill titles be written in plain, nontechnical language, use words with common meaning, and be understandable to the average reader.

What does Amendment 85 do?

Amendment 85 requires that all ballot titles be in plain language, written at no more than an eighth-grade reading level. The measure does not define plain language or designate who will determine if ballot titles meet the standard. An eighth-grade reading level is described as one that is easily and fully understood by a majority of people. This typically includes short sentences, familiar words, and a conversational tone.

Amendment 85 also prohibits laws requiring citizen initiatives to include language in the ballot title that may interfere with the plain language requirement. The ballot titles for measures referred by the state legislature are not subject to this prohibition. There are several current laws that require additional language in ballot titles. For example, if a citizen initiative raises or lowers state taxes, the initiative’s title must include specific language describing the changes and the impact on taxpayers. For citizen initiatives that reduce tax collections, the title must include an estimate of potential state spending reductions and how reduced revenue might impact the three largest areas of government spending.

Arguments For Amendment 85

1) While ballot titles are supposed to be written as plainly as possible, this measure provides additional guidance as to what that means by requiring an eighth-grade reading level as the standard. Providing a constitutional standard will help in crafting simple titles that voters may better understand when considering their vote, and may help them to navigate complicated and technical information when trying to understand a measure’s impact.

2) The measure creates a constitutional protection against the state legislature interfering with plain language in initiative ballot titles. Citizen initiatives are a way for the public to pass laws without restrictions created by the legislature. This measure ensures that no future legislature may add laws or change existing laws to create complicated or confusing initiative ballot titles.

Arguments Against Amendment 85

1) This measure is unnecessary as state law already provides ample guidance for the preparation of ballot titles in plain language. Ballot titles must convey important information to voters about complex policies. This measure creates a constitutional standard with no guidance about how its requirements are different from current law. This could lead to more challenges of initiative ballot titles, impacting the initiative process.

2) This measure puts vague and undefined language in the constitution, which could prevent the creation of laws to help voters better understand the effects of ballot measures. Laws that add language to ballot titles could be prohibited, even if that language provides critical information for voters. The measure may lead to challenges of existing laws that help voters understand how their vote affects state and local programs and funding.

Be it enacted by the People of the state of Colorado:

SECTION 1. In Colorado Constitution Article V, Section 1, add (5.6) as follows: (5.6) Ballot questions shall be in plain language at no more than an eighth-grade reading level. No statute may interfere with this provision by mandating language preceding, following, or in a ballot title of an initiative.
