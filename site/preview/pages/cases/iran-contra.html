Iran-Contra (secret arms sales and contra support, 1982 to 1993)

Evidence first. Conclusions revisable.

Descend into the record ↓
Sources checked 2026-10-19 · Preliminary research, not a final verdict
Report for revision

Members only. Sign in to send a correction.

In the mid-1980s, White House staff secretly sold missiles to Iran and ran a private network to supply Nicaraguan contra rebels while Congress had restricted that aid; the public learned of it in late 1986 from a downed plane and a Lebanese magazine, and the later prosecutions ended with fourteen people charged, eleven convicted, two convictions overturned on appeal, and six pardons.

In brief

In the mid-1980s, White House staff sold missiles to Iran and ran a private network to supply Nicaraguan rebels while Congress had limited that aid. Voters did not learn of it from their government. A supply plane was shot down on Oct. 5, 1986, and a Lebanese magazine reported the arms sales on Nov. 3. By Nov. 25 the White House admitted that some money had been diverted. The Independent Counsel, Lawrence Walsh, later charged fourteen people. Eleven were convicted, but two convictions were overturned because Congress had granted immunity for the televised hearings, and six people, including a former Defense Secretary, were pardoned in December 1992. Walsh found no direct evidence that President Reagan knew of the diversion, and he found that records were withheld and that "Congress was defrauded." The lesson here is not about one party or one president. It is that people can only check what they are shown, and that the tools which produced public disclosure, such as immunity and classification, also limited what could be proved.

The record

What this case is, and what we checked

The case has two parts that were joined in November 1986. One was a set of arms sales to Iran, partly aimed at freeing American hostages held in Lebanon. The other was a covert effort to keep the Nicaraguan contras supplied after Congress cut off or limited U.S. aid. The link was money: the Independent Counsel found that some profit from the Iran sales went to the contra effort.

North, 910 F.2d 843 (D.C. Sources we could not open: the full text of the 1987 congressional committees' report (the archive.org item page opened but its text did not; we read only its title page description), Reagan's March 4, 1987 address (the Reagan Library and American Presidency Project pages failed), and Walsh Chapters on Shultz, Weinberger, and Meese. Several tools returned machine summaries of long pages, so any quote below comes from the text those tools returned, and quotes are kept short.

The Boland limits

  • Dec. 1982: the first Boland Amendment, in the defense appropriations law, barred certain agencies' funds for "the purpose of overthrowing the government of Nicaragua" (Walsh Part I). Wikipedia says the House passed it 411 to 0 on Dec. 8, 1982 and Reagan signed it Dec. 21, 1982 (Wikipedia, Boland Amendment; not checked against the Congressional Record).
  • Oct. 1984: a stricter version took effect, barring funds that would have the effect of supporting military or paramilitary operations in Nicaragua (Walsh Part I). The Walsh executive summary says "Congress barred contra military aid from October 1984 to October 1986." Congress loosened the limits in late 1985 to allow CIA communications support and training (Part I).

Timeline

  • Aug. 20 and Sept. 14, 1985: Israel ships 96 and then 408 U.S.-made TOW missiles to Iran. Rev. Benjamin Weir is released Sept. 15, 1985 (Walsh Part I).
  • Nov. 1985: A shipment of 18 HAWK missiles reaches Iran out of 80 planned (Part I).
  • Dec. 5, 1985: Reagan signs a retroactive Finding covering the November HAWK shipment. He later said he did not recall signing it; Poindexter testified he did (Walsh Ch. 27).
  • Jan. 17, 1986: A Finding lists three purposes. None is funding the contras or profit (Ch. 27).
  • Feb. 1986: 1,000 TOWs go to Iran (Part I).
  • May 14 to 15, 1986: Iran deposits $15 million into a Swiss account controlled by Richard Secord (Part I).
  • Oct. 5, 1986: Nicaraguan soldiers shoot down a contra supply plane. Eugene Hasenfus survives (Walsh chronology). After the crash Oliver North began destroying ledgers of his payments to the contras (Part I).
  • Nov. 3, 1986: A Lebanese weekly reports the arms sales (chronology spells it "Al-Shiraa").
  • Nov. 6, 13, 19, 1986: Reagan denies arms sales, then admits them but denies they were for hostages, then denies U.S. involvement in pre-1986 shipments (chronology).
  • Nov. 21, 1986: Poindexter secretly destroys the signed December 1985 Finding (Part I).
  • Nov. 25, 1986: The White House discloses the diversion of sale proceeds to the contras (chronology). Poindexter resigns and North is fired (Wikipedia).
  • Dec. 1, 1986: Reagan appoints the Tower Commission. Dec. 19, 1986: Lawrence Walsh is appointed Independent Counsel (chronology).
  • Jan. 6 and 7, 1987: Senate and House create Iran-contra committees. Feb. 26, 1987: Tower Commission report.
  • July 7 to 21, 1987: North and Poindexter testify publicly under immunity (chronology).
  • Nov. 17 or 18, 1987: Congressional committees' report. The Walsh chronology says Nov. 18; Wikipedia gives both dates in different places. We did not resolve this.
  • Mar. 16, 1988: A 23-count indictment of Poindexter, North, Secord and Albert Hakim (Walsh executive summary).
  • May 4, 1989: North convicted on three counts. July 20, 1990: D.C. Circuit vacates and remands. Sept. 16, 1991: charges dismissed (Walsh summary of prosecutions).
  • Apr. 7, 1990: Poindexter convicted. Nov. 15, 1991: reversed on appeal (same).
  • June 16, 1992: Caspar Weinberger indicted. Dec. 24, 1992: President George H. W. Bush pardons six people.
  • Aug. 4, 1993: Walsh submits his final report (National Archives page).

What Walsh found and did not find

  • On the President: the Independent Counsel found "No direct evidence" that Reagan authorized or knew of the profiteering or the diversion (Ch. 27), and concluded proof beyond a reasonable doubt was lacking. Walsh also wrote that it was "doubtful" Reagan would have tolerated Iran's affronts in 1986 unless he knew the sales were funding the contras. Walsh wrote that impeachment, not criminal prosecution, was the constitutional remedy (Ch. 27, as summarized by our fetch tool; we did not read the sentence ourselves).
  • On the law: the executive summary says the "provision and coordination of contra support violated" the Boland ban. It also says Reagan "set the stage" by encouraging contra support during the cutoff and by authorizing the 1985 sales despite the embargo.
  • On records: "Large volumes of contemporaneous documents were systematically and willfully withheld" by several officials. Notes from Regan, Weinberger and Shultz were not obtained until 1991 and 1992. Bush's diary transcripts were found in Sept. 1992 and reached the Independent Counsel Dec. 11, 1992 (Ch. 28).
  • On Congress: "Congress was defrauded" (Part XI). Walsh found officials gave Congress false or misleading accounts after the 1986 disclosures.
  • Money: More than $47 million flowed through the Enterprise accounts (executive summary). The amount that went to the contras from the Iran sales is stated in different ways by different pages we read; we did not reconcile it (see Section 5).

Congress and the immunity problem

Congress gave North and Poindexter immunity for the 1987 hearings. The D.C. Circuit held in North that the government must show it made no use, direct or indirect, of immunized testimony and ordered a witness-by-witness hearing (910 F.2d 843). Walsh wrote that the appeals courts applied a strict standard and the grants of immunity left no full accounting of the criminal wrongdoing. He also said Congress, not the prosecutor, should prevail when the two roles conflict, because Congress is primarily responsible for public disclosure (Part X).

The pardons

Bush's proclamation named "patriotism" as the "common denominator" and objected to "the criminalization of policy differences." It also said the five "did not profit or seek to profit." Walsh said the Weinberger pardon was the first time a President pardoned someone in whose trial he might have been called as a witness (Ch. 28), and called the cover-up "completed" with it (Brown University page, quoting Walsh).

What this case shows

The question: ordinary people can only vote on, and fix, what they can know. This case is about what happened when the record was hidden.

  1. The public learned from outside, not from the government. The chain that opened the case was a crashed plane (Oct. 5, 1986) and a Lebanese magazine (Nov. 3, 1986). Reagan's public account changed three times in two weeks (Nov. 6, 13, 19). Voters cannot judge a policy they have been told is not happening.
  2. Congress, acting for voters, set limits through the one lever it has: appropriations. The Boland measures were the voters' representatives saying where the money could not go. Walsh found the contra support continued anyway (executive summary). The consent that voters had expressed through their representatives was bypassed.
  3. Records were shredded, withheld, or found late. North destroyed ledgers; Poindexter destroyed the signed Finding; the notes of three cabinet officers surfaced in 1991 and 1992; Bush's diary reached investigators in Dec. 1992, twelve days before the pardons. Each of those delayed what the public could know. Walsh's records are held by the National Archives and "are NOT available on line" (NARA page), with some still classified.
  4. The correction mechanisms partly failed, and the failure had a design cause. Immunity for the public hearings gave the country a televised account in 1987, and also, in Walsh's reading, prevented convictions of the two central staff. Voters got one kind of disclosure at the cost of another.
  5. The final act was a clemency decision. The pardons came after the last election-year votes had been cast and before Walsh's report was filed. The President who issued them had been named by Walsh as a possible witness. The public had no vote or process on that decision.
  6. The case shows what a standing independent check looked like. The Independent Counsel could not be removed by the official under investigation. Walsh wrote that the attorney general "must represent the public, not the President" (Part XI). Whether that structure is a useful model is a policy question we do not settle.

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: This was a policy dispute that prosecutors turned into crime. Bush used this phrasing in the proclamation. Reply: the 11 convictions included guilty pleas to withholding information from Congress, and Walsh's finding was about concealment, not the policy itself. But the objection has support: most pleas were misdemeanors, the two biggest convictions were overturned, and a person can reasonably say the criminal record was thin compared with the scandal. Both are true.
  2. Objection: Reagan did not know of the diversion. Walsh found "No direct evidence" he did. Reply: Walsh also found Reagan encouraged the contra effort during the cutoff and that sources disagree. Poindexter said he decided alone; North testified he believed the President had authorized it (executive summary). We do not claim Reagan knew. The question is how the public was informed, not what he knew.
  3. Objection: Boland did not apply to the National Security Council. This argument is associated with the minority report and the administration (we read it only secondhand, in Wikipedia's summary, and not in the report itself). Walsh's conclusion is the opposite. Courts did not decide the point because convictions came on other grounds. We state it as contested and unresolved in court.
  4. Objection: Secrecy was needed to save hostages and national security. There is some force here. Walsh nonetheless found the administration kept facts from Congress after the arms sales were public, and the Fernandez case was dismissed after the Attorney General blocked classified evidence, a choice Walsh criticized as unreviewable (Part XI). The record supports both a real classification interest and its use to shield the case from review.
  5. Objection: The pardons were a normal use of a constitutional power. True, the power is plain. Bush gave reasons in writing. Walsh and then-President-elect Clinton (quoted in the USIA wire report) objected on the ground that it signaled officials were "above the law." We describe both and do not rule on which weighs more.
  6. Objection: Immunity grants were Congress's own choice, so blaming courts or prosecutors is wrong. Correct. Walsh said so in his own report (Part X): Congress should grant immunity to potential defendants only in extraordinary circumstances.
  7. Objection: Wikipedia and a summary tool are weak sources for numbers. Agreed. Every count on this page comes from the Walsh report pages, not from Wikipedia. Wikipedia is used only for the Boland House vote and the congressional report quotes, and we say so.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • The diversion total. We saw $47 million through the Enterprise accounts (Walsh), $15 million Iran deposit in May 1986, a $10 million Brunei transfer misdirected, and North's $3,700 versus about $10,000 per TOW. We did not locate a single figure for how much reached the contras. Do not state one.
  • Congressional majority and minority wording. The quotes "secrecy, deception and disdain for the law" and "if the president did not know... he should have" come from Wikipedia's summary. We did not read them in the committee report. Check the archive.org full text (title: Report of the Congressional Committees Investigating the Iran-Contra Affair, Nov. 1987).
  • Report date. Nov. 17 or Nov. 18, 1987. Not resolved.
  • Poindexter's final dismissal. Walsh's table says 1993. Wikipedia says the Supreme Court declined to hear the case. We did not read the order.
  • Reagan's March 4, 1987 address. We did not read the text. Do not quote it.
  • The Weinberger notes and Shultz chapter. We read only one chapter summary on Weinberger. Whether his notes contradicted Reagan's and Bush's statements is disputed (Weinberger denied it, per Brown page). Read Walsh Part VIII.
  • Bush. Walsh gave reasons for not pursuing Bush (limitations, low likelihood, appearance of retaliation). We read these through a summary tool. Read Ch. 28 directly before quoting.
  • Boland and the NSC. We did not open the minority report or any court ruling on whether Boland covered NSC staff.
  • Hasenfus and the October 1986 timing. Date taken from the Walsh chronology; Wikipedia gave none.
  • If a primary document showed a clear presidential order to divert funds, our statement about "no direct evidence" would change.
