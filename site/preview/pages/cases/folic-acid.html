The Observatory · Case 28 · Public health · 1992 to 2016

Mandatory folic-acid grain fortification (United States, 1992 to 2016)

Evidence first. Conclusions revisable.

Descend into the record ↓
Sources checked 2026-10-19 · Preliminary research, not a final verdict
Report for revision

Members only. Sign in to send a correction.

After a 1992 health recommendation that women take folic acid proved hard to deliver through pills alone, the FDA required it in enriched grain products from 1998, and neural tube defects fell by roughly a quarter to a third, with unresolved questions about vitamin B12, cancer and who still goes without.

In brief

Neural tube defects were already declining in the 1970s and 1980s, and trials in 1991 and 1992 showed folic acid could prevent many of them. In 1992 the Public Health Service told women to take 400 micrograms a day, but by 2002 only 31% did so daily. In 1996 the FDA published a rule requiring folic acid in enriched grain products from January 1, 1998, after receiving about 170 comment letters, most favoring voluntary fortification, and rejecting that option. CDC data show pregnancies affected by these defects fell about 27% by 1999 to 2000, and a 2015 update puts the overall decline at 28% and savings at about $508 million a year. The benefit was uneven: Hispanic women had the highest rates throughout, and corn masa was covered only by a voluntary allowance in 2016. Questions about vitamin B12 and cancer remain open. The rule was written in public, but most people who ate fortified bread never knew they were part of it.

The record

The problem and the first policy: advice, not a mandate

  • Neural tube defects (NTDs) such as spina bifida and anencephaly were already declining before any fortification. Nationwide NTD rates fell from 1.3 to 0.6 per 1,000 births between 1970 and 1989 (15,503 cases), and in Atlanta from 2.0 to 0.6 between 1968 and 1989 (800 cases). The authors attributed part of that fall to prenatal diagnosis and suggested environmental factors; they called for study of multivitamin and folic acid use. (PubMed record 1496959, birth-defects surveillance study; abstract opened. The title, authors and journal were not shown in what we read.)
  • Trial evidence: a 1983 nonrandomized trial, the 1991 British MRC randomized trial (folic acid alone cut recurrence in women with a prior affected pregnancy) and a 1992 Hungarian randomized trial of multivitamins with folic acid against first occurrences. (CDC MMWR RR-13, 2002; opened)
  • In September 1992 the U.S. Public Health Service recommended that all women able to become pregnant consume 400 micrograms of folic acid a day. The same report says the Service predicted a 50 to 70% decline if all women did so, and that observed declines of about 20 to 30% after fortification were smaller. (MMWR RR-13)
  • Supplement behavior stayed low. Daily folic acid supplement use among women rose only from 25% in 1995 to 31% in 2002, and in 2002 only 20% of women knew folic acid can prevent certain birth defects (MMWR RR-13; survey details not independently checked).

The rule

  • FDA published a final rule on March 5, 1996 (61 Fed. Reg. 8781, Docket 91N-100S) amending the standards of identity for enriched grain products to require folic acid, at 140 micrograms per 100 grams of grain product. Effective date January 1, 1998; labeling-compliant use could start earlier. A companion rule (61 Fed. Reg. 8797, Docket 91N-100F) allowed folic acid as a food additive in such products and in breakfast cereals up to 400 micrograms per serving. (Federal Register, March 5, 1996, standards-of-identity rule; companion additive rule; both opened. We read the first 100,000 of 119,000 characters of the standards-of-identity rule.)
  • FDA's reasons: women do not take supplements reliably in the weeks before and early in pregnancy; about 4,000 pregnancies a year were affected by NTDs including 2,500 live births; and a citizen petition from Glenn Scott. FDA "rejected" voluntary fortification as likely too limited. It said most commenters favored voluntary fortification. About 170 letters were received. (Federal Register 96-5014)
  • Commenters urged using supplements or naturally folate-rich foods instead. FDA said supplements alone would not reach women in the critical early window and found merit in foods only as a complement. (96-5014)
  • FDA set 1 milligram of total folate a day as a safe upper limit and rejected requiring vitamin B12 alongside, finding no data showing that would be safe or effective. The agency concluded fortification at these levels was not likely to increase B12 masking. It cited an outside estimate of roughly 500 people a year who could develop neurologic disease from low-level fortification and noted other authors considered that an understatement. (96-5014; 96-5012, which sets the 1 mg limit)
  • A 1998 Institute of Medicine report set a tolerable upper intake level for folic acid at 1,000 micrograms a day for adults, noted that 10 to 30% of older adults cannot absorb food-form B12 well, and warned that adults with B12 deficiency who take excess folic acid face greater risk of progressive neurologic damage. (National Academies news release, April 1998; opened)
  • Fortification was voluntary from March 1996 to December 1997 and mandatory from 1998 (MMWR RR-13).

All opened. The 2015 report says the biggest drop was in 1999 to 2000, after which prevalence stayed fairly stable (6.4 to 7.6 per 10,000 in the periods reported). The 2004 report notes its data cover about half of U.S. births, rely on varying prenatal ascertainment so counts are probably underestimated, and show a smaller decline than trials predicted. The 2015 report notes the 19 programs may not represent the whole country and that its updated estimate is slightly above the earlier one because of rising births and surveillance differences. Wikipedia reports a CDC figure of 35% decline and about 4,100 affected pregnancies before fortification (an unsourced summary; we use the CDC reports above).

Other concerns, as reported

  • Colorectal cancer: Mason and colleagues (Tufts and USDA researchers, Cancer Epidemiology, Biomarkers and Prevention, July 2007) noted that 15 years of declining colorectal cancer rates reversed in 1996 in the U.S. and in 1998 in Canada, and estimated four to six additional cases per 100,000 people a year versus earlier trends. They called it a temporal association and hypothesis, not proof of cause, and said fortification is clearly beneficial for women of childbearing age. (ScienceDaily summary, July 2007; opened. The journal article itself was not opened.) Wikipedia says later meta-analyses are conflicting and that low versus high dietary folate was associated with a modest reduction in colon cancer. We did not open the underlying meta-analyses.
  • Unmetabolized folic acid: Wikipedia reports 70 to 80% of the population has detectable levels and the evidence of health effects is inconsistent. We did not verify this against a primary study.
  • Intake overshoot: Wikipedia says projected intake of 70 to 130 micrograms a day rose by almost double the projection, and many foods are fortified at 160 to 175% of the required amount. FDA's rule intended an average increase of about 100 micrograms a day (MMWR RR-13). The Wikipedia figures were not verified.

Who was left out

  • NTD prevalence fell in non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic mothers, but Hispanic mothers had the highest prevalence throughout (MMWR 2015, from a figure; no numbers given in the text).
  • Corn masa flour was not covered by the 1998 grain standard. In 2016 FDA allowed masa makers to add folic acid "if they wanted to"; fortification remains voluntary, and CDC says it is unclear how much masa or masa-based products contain folic acid. CDC epidemiologist Jenny Williams said Hispanic women have the highest NTD rates and that "we need to be able to get that prevention to the people that need it the most." (WUNC, Dec. 8, 2023; opened. The FDA 2016 notice and a PubMed Central paper on masa effects were not opened because of a browser check.)
  • Disadvantaged groups: spina bifida declined less among disadvantaged groups in North Carolina; a Georgia clinic sample had a median serum folate of 8.9 ng/mL versus 13.0 nationally (MMWR RR-13; these are single-site findings).

Abroad

  • Australia required fortification in 2009. The UK announced in November 2024 that it would require it by end of 2026. New Zealand abandoned a mandate in 2012 for a voluntary program after bakers and the Green Party called it "mass medication." (Wikipedia, Folic acid; unverified against national sources.)

What this case shows

  1. Voters and the public could read the decision. The 1996 rule is a public document with comment, response and reasoning. FDA recorded that most commenters favored voluntary fortification and then rejected that. The tradeoffs (B12, upper limit, cost per case of neurologic disability about $538,000, an outside estimate of 500 people a year at risk) are written in the open. (Federal Register 96-5014)
  2. A recommendation reached too few people. The 1992 advice to take 400 micrograms stayed a pill-taking habit for about a third of women or fewer, so the food supply carried the change. This is the clearest case in this set of a policy built for people who never see it. (MMWR RR-13)
  3. The people most affected did not choose it, and some were left out. Nobody who ate bread voted on the additive. Hispanic women, who had the highest NTD rates, were not covered by the original grain standard for masa, and the 2016 allowance was voluntary. (MMWR 2015; WUNC)
  4. The benefit is measured but modest and disputed in size. A 20 to 35% drop, about 1,300 fewer affected births a year, and a savings estimate of about $508 million a year. All of it depends on surveillance coverage that its authors call incomplete. (MMWR 2004; MMWR 2015)
  5. A tradeoff remained open. Advice on B12 and cancer is a standing debate that voters can follow, since the numbers are published, but the policy did not include a mechanism to revisit them on a schedule that we could verify.

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: NTDs were already falling and supplements were rising, so fortification gets too much credit. Mitigation: True that rates fell from 1970 to 1989 (1.3 to 0.6 per 1,000). The 2015 CDC analysis shows the sharpest drop in 1999 to 2000, immediately after the mandate, and a plateau after. Puerto Rico's CDC report attributes its 1996 to 2003 NTD decline "likely" to both fortification and a supplement campaign, and does not separate them. We cannot separate the effects and say so. (MMWR, Jan. 2008; opened)
  2. Objection: Mass fortification is medication without consent. Mitigation: We report the New Zealand objection (bakers and the Green Party, "mass medication") and the U.S. commenters who preferred voluntary fortification and supplements. FDA's answer was that voluntary uptake would be too limited. Both are on record; we do not rule between them.
  3. Objection: Fortification may raise colorectal cancer or hide B12 deficiency. Mitigation: The 2007 paper is a hypothesis; its authors say not proven. FDA held that masking was unlikely to increase at the chosen level, and the Institute of Medicine warned of risk for B12-deficient older adults. We did not find a verified, current answer on either, and list both as open.
  4. Objection: The effect is smaller than trials predicted. Mitigation: The 2002 and 2004 CDC reports say exactly this: 20 to 30% versus the 50 to 70% predicted. The 2015 update gives 28% overall. We present the gap.
  5. Objection: The sources include Wikipedia and a press summary. Mitigation: Federal Register rules and CDC MMWR reports carry the core numbers. Wikipedia and ScienceDaily are flagged where used alone.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • The FDA 2016 masa notice, and any current share of fortified masa, were not opened. WUNC says CDC does not know.
  • The 2007 Mason paper was seen only through a press summary. Later cohort and trial evidence on colorectal cancer and on B12 masking was not opened. Wikipedia statements on these are unverified.
  • The 1996 rule text was read only to about 100,000 of 119,000 characters; the section giving FDA's estimate of cases averted was not reached. A cost to industry was not found.
  • Hispanic and Black NTD numbers (MMWR 2015) appear only in a figure we could not read.
  • Whether the NTD decline continued after 2011 is unverified.
  • Canada's 46% figure and the 30% meta-analysis figure came from Wikipedia only.
  • The 1970 to 1989 surveillance study's title and authors were not seen.
  • The Puerto Rico 2008 report is a territory, not the states.
  • The Institute of Medicine 1998 report itself was not opened; only the news release.
  • Would change our minds: a controlled comparison of fortified and unfortified populations in the U.S. (none found), or an NTD time-series with supplement use measured year by year.
