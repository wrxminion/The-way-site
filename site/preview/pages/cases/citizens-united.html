Citizens United and SpeechNow (United States, 1976 to 2025)

In 2010 the Supreme Court (5 to 4) and the D.C. Circuit lifted limits on independent political spending by corporations and by groups funded by unlimited contributions, upholding disclosure; independent expenditures later reached billions of dollars a cycle, and polls show wide public opposition.

In brief

In 1976 the Supreme Court allowed limits on campaign contributions but struck down limits on independent spending. On January 21, 2010, in Citizens United v. FEC, it ruled 5 to 4 that corporations may spend without limit on independent political communication, and 8 to 1 that disclosure rules may still apply. Two months later the D.C. Circuit held in SpeechNow v. FEC that groups making only independent expenditures cannot be subject to contribution limits, while upholding their reporting duties. The FEC reports that independent expenditure-only committees received $5.1 billion in 2023 and 2024, and independent expenditures in presidential and congressional races totaled $4.4 billion. A Washington Post and ABC News poll reported in February 2010 found about 80% opposed the ruling, including majorities of Democrats, Republicans and independents. Supporters answer that the First Amendment protects speakers whatever polls say. The record shows a major rule change made by courts, with disclosure as the piece the public can still check.

The record

Background: Buckley v. Valeo (1976)

  • The Court decided Buckley on January 30, 1976, in a per curiam opinion. It upheld the $1,000 limit on individual contributions to a candidate, treating limits on contributions as a way to address corruption or its appearance (it used the phrase "quid pro quo"). It upheld reporting and recordkeeping requirements and presidential public financing. It struck down the $1,000 limit on independent spending "relative to a clearly identified candidate," limits on candidates' own spending and overall spending caps. It reasoned that spending money on political communication is itself speech, so limits on spending restrict expression more than limits on contributions. It also held that the Federal Election Commission's appointment structure violated the Appointments Clause. (Buckley v. Valeo, Justia; opened, first 100,000 characters)

Citizens United v. FEC (2010)

  • Decided January 21, 2010, No. 08-205. Justice Kennedy wrote the opinion. Citizens United, a nonprofit corporation, had made Hillary, a documentary critical of then-Senator Hillary Clinton, and planned to offer it by video on demand within 30 days of primaries and to run television ads. It feared penalties under 2 U.S.C. section 441b, which limited corporate spending on electioneering communications and express advocacy. (Cornell LII, Citizens United syllabus and opinion; Justia; both opened)
  • Holding: the ban on corporate independent expenditures is invalid and cannot be applied to Hillary. Austin v. Michigan Chamber of Commerce was overruled, and the part of McConnell v. FEC that upheld the extension of these limits was overruled. The Court said that independent expenditures "do not give rise to corruption or the appearance of corruption," and that "quid pro quo corruption" remains a recognized interest that does not reach independent spending. It also wrote that speakers' influence over or access to officials "does not mean that those officials are corrupt." (Cornell; Justia syllabus text)
  • Disclosure: BCRA sections 201 and 311 (disclaimer and disclosure rules) were upheld as applied to the ads and the film. The Court said disclosure serves an informational interest for voters and that the group's members had shown no threats or reprisals. (Cornell)
  • Vote: the main holding was 5 to 4. Kennedy was joined by Roberts, Scalia and Alito; Thomas joined all but Part IV. Part IV (disclosure) drew eight votes; Stevens, Ginsburg, Breyer and Sotomayor joined it, and Thomas dissented on that part, saying he would have invalidated the disclosure requirements and stressing that "anonymity was important." Justice Stevens, joined by Ginsburg, Breyer and Sotomayor, concurred in part and dissented in part. We could not read his dissent text; the tool pages we opened gave only summaries. (Justia header and syllabus summary)

SpeechNow.org v. FEC (D.C. Circuit, en banc, 2010)

  • Decided March 26, 2010 (argued January 27, 2010), Nos. 08-5223 and 09-5342, opinion by Chief Judge Sentelle. SpeechNow was a group that planned to make only independent expenditures and to take unlimited contributions. (D.C. Circuit opinion PDF; opened)
  • Holding 1: the contribution limits in 2 U.S.C. section 441a(a)(1)(C) and 441a(a)(3) are unconstitutional as applied to contributions to an independent-expenditure-only group. The reasoning follows Citizens United: if independent expenditures do not corrupt, there is no anti-corruption interest in capping contributions to a group that makes only such expenditures. The ruling does not affect limits on direct contributions to candidates.
  • Holding 2: the political-committee organization, registration and reporting requirements were upheld as applied. The court wrote that "the public has an interest in knowing who is speaking about a candidate and who is funding that speech," and that "disclosure is a less restrictive alternative to more comprehensive regulations of speech."
  • Whether the decision was unanimous: the opinion page lists nine judges and one opinion "for the Court" but states no vote count. An Institute for Justice press release headline says the court "unanimously" struck the limits; we saw the headline in a search result and did not open that release. Treat as not confirmed.
  • The result is the legal basis for "Super PACs," which the FEC calls "Independent Expenditure-Only Political Committees." (FEC statistical summary, below)

Public opinion

  • Washington Post and ABC News poll, results reported February 17, 2010, within a month of the decision: about eight in 10 opposed the Court's ruling, 65% strongly; 72% favored Congress acting to reinstate limits; opposition by party was Democrats 85%, Republicans 76%, independents 81%. (Progressive Policy Institute, Ed Kilgore, Feb. 17, 2010; Mother Jones summary; both opened. The poll's field dates, sample size and exact question wording were not on those pages. The original poll release was blocked.)
  • A critic's reading: John Samples of the Cato Institute (Feb. 17, 2010) said the poll found "just over 70 percent" wanted to reinstate restrictions and argued that majority opinion on campaign finance in the abstract does not decide what the First Amendment allows. Note the 72% (PPI) versus "just over 70 percent" (Cato): the two wordings refer to the same question as summarized, and we show both without choosing. (Cato, "Democracy against Free Speech?"; opened)
  • We did not open any later poll. A later poll, by name and date, would be needed to say whether opinion has changed since 2010.

Disclosure rules

  • The Court upheld disclaimer and disclosure requirements in 2010 as applied, and SpeechNow upheld committee registration and reporting. Super PACs file reports with the FEC. Which other organizations, such as nonprofit groups, spend on elections without disclosing their donors, and how much, was not examined in the sources we opened. Unverified.

What this case shows

  1. The legal change was decided in two court rulings, not by a vote. Five justices decided the main holding, and eight agreed on disclosure. Ordinary people did not vote on it, and polls reported soon afterward showed majorities against the main result. Whether a court should decide this is argued on both sides below. (Cornell; PPI)
  2. Disclosure is the part the public can use. Both courts upheld it, and the SpeechNow court said the public has an interest in knowing "who is funding that speech." What people can know depends on how much of the spending is reported; Super PACs report to the FEC, while the extent of spending that does not disclose donors was not measured here. (SpeechNow; Cornell)
  3. The sums are large and measurable. In 2023 to 2024, IE-only committees received $5.1 billion and independent expenditures reached $4.4 billion. Residents can read the FEC data, but the scale gives a handful of large funders a visible place in campaign communications. We do not name donors or score anyone. (FEC)
  4. The free-speech argument gives a real reason to protect the ruling. The majority held that banning corporate independent expenditures is a ban on speech; Cato's Samples argues the First Amendment protects unpopular speakers regardless of poll results. People excluded from official channels, including small nonprofits like Citizens United itself, gained the right to speak. (Cornell; Cato)
  5. The corruption argument is also about public trust. The Court said influence and access are not corruption; the dissent in the record argued the opposite. Voters hold both views, and polls found opposition in all parties. Our question question is whether people can see and judge the spending. (Justia; PPI)

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: The ruling protects speech and the polls do not matter. Mitigation: We state this argument (Samples) and the majority's reasoning. The polls are reported as opinion, not as a legal test.
  2. Objection: Independent spending cannot be coordinated, so corruption is not shown. Mitigation: That is the Court's holding. The dissent's disagreement is recorded, but its text was not opened, so we do not attribute specific arguments to it. We do not infer motive from outcomes.
  3. Objection: Large totals prove undue influence. Mitigation: The totals show amounts, not influence. Elections, donors and results are not linked here. The figures are not scored against any party or candidate.
  4. Objection: Super PACs existed because of SpeechNow, not Citizens United. Mitigation: We cite both. SpeechNow relied on Citizens United's reasoning, and each decision covers a different rule (corporate expenditures versus contribution limits to IE-only groups).
  5. Objection: The poll is old and its wording is not shown. Mitigation: Agreed. We give the date reported, flag that the field dates and wording were not available to us, and treat it as one data point.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • Totals for independent expenditures in 2008, 2010, 2012, 2016 and 2020 from FEC or OpenSecrets. OpenSecrets blocked us; FEC bulk files were not downloaded.
  • Stevens's dissent text and the other separate opinions. Not read.
  • Whether SpeechNow was unanimous. Opinion text did not say.
  • Spending by organizations that do not disclose donors, and the legal rules for them. Not opened.
  • Later polls with names and dates, including any that ask about free speech and corruption separately.
  • Evidence linking spending to legislative or electoral outcomes. We did not examine any study, and we draw no such link.
  • The complete text of Buckley's passages on independent expenditures (about 17,000 characters were not read).
