U.S. Drug Policy and Mandatory Minimums, the Long Record (United States, 1619 to 2023)

Evidence first. Conclusions revisable.

Federal drug law grew from tax and registration rules in 1914 into scheduling, a dedicated agency and fixed minimum sentences by 1986, with documented gaps between crack and powder cocaine penalties and a separate public-health record of overdose deaths.

In brief

Federal drug law began as a tax. The Harrison Act of 1914 required registration and a small tax, and the Supreme Court in 1919 upheld its order-form rules by a 5 to 4 vote. In 1937 Congress taxed marihuana after hearings in which the Narcotics Commissioner and a hemp grower both testified, and the law excluded stalks, fiber and sterilized seed. Fixed minimum sentences came in 1951 and 1956, were mostly repealed in 1970, and returned in 1986. In 1973 a new Drug Enforcement Administration brought Treasury, Health and Justice functions together, and its own table shows its nominal budget growing from about $75 million in FY1973 to $394 million in FY1986. The 1986 Act set a 100-to-1 gap between crack and powder cocaine, passed with no committee report, and the 2010 Fair Sentencing Act cut that to about 18 to 1. In FY2000, 84.7% of crack offenders in federal court were Black, according to a Commission report quoting another group. The record shows what the laws said and who they reached. It does not show why. Overdose deaths stood at 105,007 in 2023.

The record

This case follows the written law, the agency record and the numbers. It does not decide what drove any law. Where a source gives a reason, the reason is attributed to that source. Alcohol Prohibition is a linked context (see the short subsection below). Convict leasing and private prisons are covered in other cases.

Early hemp and opium rules

  • The Virginia General Assembly act of 1619 mentions hemp in one clause: "we do require and enjoin all householders" who have hemp, flax or aniseed to "make trial thereof the next season." It sets no penalty for hemp. Separate clauses on silk flax, vines and mulberry trees do name penalties set by the Governor and Council. The text read here is a reprint, not the original manuscript. (Teaching American History reprint; opened)
  • The claim that every colonial or Revolutionary farmer was required to grow hemp is not supported by this one clause, which asked householders with seed to run a trial. Other colonial hemp statutes were not opened.
  • An ordinance of the San Francisco Board of Supervisors from November 1875 is said to bar anyone from keeping, joining or visiting a place where opium is smoked. We could only see it quoted in a student journal that cites a 2024 magazine article. The wording applies to "no person." We did not open the ordinance book, arrest records or newspapers, so we cannot say who was arrested. (Princeton Historical Review, Winter 2025; opened, secondary)
  • In re Sic, 73 Cal. 142 (1887), concerned Stockton Ordinance No. 192 against assembling to smoke opium. The California Supreme Court discharged the petitioner because the ordinance covered "precisely the same acts" as state Penal Code section 307. The opinion does not use the word Chinese. (Justia; opened)

Harrison Act, 1914, and the physician cases

  • The Harrison Narcotics Tax Act was signed December 17, 1914 (Pub. L. 63-223, 38 Stat. 785). It required registration and a special tax for people who produce, import, make, deal in or give out opium, coca and their derivatives. It allowed distribution by physicians "in the course of his professional practice only." It took effect March 1, 1915. (Wikipedia, Harrison Act; opened, secondary. The statute text itself was not opened.)
  • United States v. Doremus, decided March 3, 1919, upheld the order-form rules of section 2 as having a "reasonable relation" to the section 1 tax. Four justices dissented (the Chief Justice and Justices McKenna, Van Devanter and McReynolds), arguing the statute reached into states' police power. (Justia; opened)
  • Webb v. United States, decided the same day, held that a physician's order for morphine to keep an habitual user comfortable was not a "physician's prescription," calling the contrary reading "so plain a perversion of meaning." (Justia; opened)
  • Linder v. United States, 268 U.S. 5, decided April 13, 1925 (Justice McReynolds), reversed a physician's conviction. It held that section 2 did not reach a registered physician who in good faith gave an addict moderate amounts of morphine or cocaine to relieve addiction. The Court called the Act "essentially a revenue measure." It kept the rule that a "mere pretense" of practice cannot cover sales. (Justia; opened)

Marihuana Tax Act, 1937

  • The House Ways and Means Committee held hearings on April 27 to 30 and May 4, 1937 (secondary: Wikipedia; the hearing volume itself was not opened). House Report 75-792 is dated May 11, 1937 and reports H.R. 6906. (GovInfo catalog; opened)
  • The report says the bill uses the taxing power "to raise revenue" and to discourage use. It models the bill on the Harrison Act and the National Firearms Act. It says marihuana "is used only to a negligible extent by the medical profession" and lists "many industrial uses" of the hemp plant. It cites 195 tons seized by state authorities in 1935 and newspaper reports from over 100 cities. It says testimony linked the drug to violent crime and school children "driven to crime and insanity." (House Report 75-792, GovInfo PDF; opened, via a summary tool)
  • Senate Finance Subcommittee hearing, July 12, 1937 (Senators Brown, Herring and Davis). Commissioner of Narcotics Harry Anslinger said use "leads to insanity" and that the drug's medical use "could be abandoned without any suffering." He read a prosecutor's letter describing a murder by "one colored young man" as evidence of the drug's effects. A hemp grower, Matt Rens, said he had "no objection whatever to controlling narcotics" and asked that viable seed and hemp be treated differently. Treasury's Clinton Hester described seed uses (birdseed, oil, cake). The excerpt we read did not include the AMA's Dr. William C. Woodward. (Senate Finance Committee PDF; opened)
  • Another source reports that Woodward opposed the tax for the AMA because it fell on physicians and pharmacists, and proposed adding cannabis to the Harrison Act instead. (Wikipedia; secondary only.)
  • The Act was approved August 2, 1937. It set an occupational tax on importers, makers and dealers and transfer taxes of $1 and $100 per ounce, with fines up to $2,000 and prison up to five years. "Marihuana" excluded mature stalks, stalk fiber, seed oil and cake, and sterilized seeds. The $100 rate was for unregistered buyers per the House report. (Statutes at Large, vol. 50, GovInfo; opened via summary tool)
  • In Leary v. United States (1969) the Supreme Court found the stamp-registration scheme conflicted with the Fifth Amendment, and Congress repealed the Act in 1970 through the Controlled Substances Act (secondary: Wikipedia).
  • Popular accounts say the Act was meant to destroy the hemp industry for named business owners. The sources opened show hemp exclusions in the text and a hemp grower in the record but contain no document proving or disproving that motive. We leave it as an attributed claim. A 2026 scholarly review reported to dispute several popular accounts could not be opened.

Mandatory minimums: 1951, 1956, 1970

  • Boggs Act: Pub. L. 82-255, 65 Stat. 767, signed November 2, 1951 (House bill by Rep. Hale Boggs). The U.S. Sentencing Commission lists minimums of 2 years for a first violation, 5 for a second and 10 for a third. (USSC 2011 report, ch. 2; opened. Wikipedia, Boggs Act; opened)
  • Narcotic Control Act of 1956: a 10-year minimum for selling heroin to a juvenile and 5 years for a first offense of possessing narcotics on a vessel (USSC 2011). The statute text was not opened.
  • The Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970 "eliminated" nearly all drug mandatory minimums, keeping the continuing criminal enterprise provision (10 years first offense, 20 years second). (CRS R45074; opened. USSC 2011 agrees.)

Controlled Substances Act, 1970, and the federal agency

  • The CSA is Title II of Pub. L. 91-513, signed October 27, 1970 (84 Stat. 1236). It sets five schedules. Schedule I requires "a high potential for abuse," "no currently accepted medical use in treatment in the United States," and "a lack of accepted safety" under supervision. Marihuana is listed in Schedule I(c). (21 U.S.C. 812, Cornell LII; opened. Wikipedia, CSA; opened)
  • Scheduling can begin with DEA, HHS or a petition; HHS must give a scientific evaluation, and if HHS finds a substance should not be controlled, DEA may not control it (Wikipedia, CSA). A rescheduling of cannabis has been reported on that page; we did not confirm its present legal status. Unverified.
  • Reorganization Plan No. 1 of 1968 (message to Congress February 7, 1968) merged the Treasury's Bureau of Narcotics and the Health, Education, and Welfare Department's Bureau of Drug Abuse Control into a Bureau of Narcotics and Dangerous Drugs in the Justice Department. The message called the earlier split "fragmented." (Presidency Project, UCSB; opened)
  • Reorganization Plan No. 2 of 1973 (transmitted March 28, 1973) created the Drug Enforcement Administration in Justice, abolished the BNDD, and moved Treasury's drug investigation functions to the Attorney General. Treasury kept border-inspection searches and seizures. It took effect on July 1, 1973 or when the statute provided, whichever was later. (87 Stat. 1091, GovInfo; opened)

1986 and 1988 acts and the crack and powder thresholds

  • The Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1986 (Pub. L. 99-570) was signed October 27, 1986. The House passed it 392 to 16 on September 11 and the Senate 97 to 2 on September 30. The deaths of basketball player Len Bias (June 19, 1986) and Don Rogers (June 27) are reported as prompting House leaders to draft it. (Wikipedia, ADAA 1986; opened, secondary)
  • The Act set five-year minimums for 5 grams of crack or 500 grams of powder cocaine, and ten-year minimums for 50 grams of crack or 5,000 grams of powder. That is a 100-to-1 quantity ratio. (USSC 2002 report; opened)
  • The Commission wrote that the enacted version had "no committee hearings and no Senate or House Reports," and that "no authoritative legislative history" explains the 100-to-1 choice. Earlier bills used 50-to-1 and 20-to-1 ratios. (USSC 2002)
  • The 1988 Act (Pub. L. 100-690) added a five-year minimum for a first simple possession of more than 5 grams of crack. Powder possession by a first offender was a misdemeanor with a one-year maximum. The 1988 Act also raised the continuing criminal enterprise minimum from 10 to 20 years. (USSC 2002; USSC 2011)
  • Reasons supporters gave, as the Commission describes the 1986 record: crack seemed extremely addictive, tied to violence, dangerous to users and prenatally exposed children, attractive to youth, and cheap and easy to sell. The Commission wrote that some of these beliefs "are no longer accurate," especially on violence. It also found that both forms produce the same effects, that smoking crack acts faster than snorting powder, and that in 2000 about three quarters of federal crack offenders had no weapon involvement. (USSC 2002)
  • Disparity, as documented: in FY2000, crack offenders were 84.7% Black, 9.0% Hispanic and 5.6% White. The Commission report cites this from a third party's quotation of Commission data; we did not open the Commission's own table. Hispanic offenders were 50.8% of powder offenders that year. (USSC 2002)

Fair Sentencing Act, 2010

  • Public Law 111-220, approved August 3, 2010. Section 2 replaced "50 grams" with "280 grams" and "5 grams" with "28 grams" in 21 U.S.C. 841(b)(1). Section 3 removed the mandatory minimum for simple possession. Section 8 told the Commission to issue conforming guidelines within 90 days. (Public Law 111-220, GovInfo; opened)
  • CRS describes the result as a ratio of roughly 18 to 1 (28 grams against 500; 280 against 5,000). (CRS R45074)
  • Whether the 2010 change applied to people already sentenced is not shown in the sources opened. Reason reported that it "doesn't apply retroactively." Unverified here.

Alcohol Prohibition, linked context (short)

The Eighteenth Amendment was ratified January 16, 1919 and repealed by the Twenty-First on December 5, 1933. The Federal Judicial Center reports average annual federal criminal cases of 17,269 (1904 to 1917) and 75,400 (1921 to 1933), and average federal prisoners of 3,720 (1920) and 13,352 (1933). The Wickersham Commission reported January 7, 1931. These figures cover alcohol and all other federal crime, so they are not drug counts. (Federal Judicial Center; opened) Prohibition is its own case; here it is a comparison for how a national ban was enforced and then repealed.

What this case shows

  1. Ordinary people were asked to follow rules whose reasons were in documents few read. The 1937 House report named crime and children as concerns, and the Senate hearing included one hemp grower and one Treasury official on seed. The record is public, but the 1986 minimums passed with "no committee hearings" and no reports, so the reasoning for the 100-to-1 ratio is not in a report a voter could read. (House Report 75-792; USSC 2002)
  2. Courts, not voters, set the early limits. Doremus (5 to 4) and Webb (1919) upheld the tax scheme and restricted physician prescribing for maintenance. Linder (1925) limited that reach. The practical boundary of medical treatment was set in opinions. (Justia)
  3. Law moved from taxes to schedules and a single agency. The 1968 and 1973 reorganization plans merged Treasury, Health and Justice functions. The CSA gave a scheduling process in which HHS has a binding no-control finding. Voters could weigh in through Congress; schedule placement later moved to agency petitions.
  4. Documented disparities are specific. The 100-to-1 ratio, the 1988 crack possession minimum with no powder counterpart, and the FY2000 figure of 84.7% Black among crack offenders are all in federal sources. The sources do not establish why Congress chose these numbers. The Commission itself says the reason is not in the legislative history.
  5. Cost and staffing are on public tables. DEA's nominal budget rose about 5.3 times in 13 years while positions rose about 1.7 times. That is an agency's own table, open to anyone, but it does not show outcomes such as use or overdose.

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Enforcement view: strict penalties responded to real harm. Supporters of the 1986 Act pointed to crack's addictiveness, violence and youth involvement, and the Act passed the House 392 to 16 and the Senate 97 to 2. Mitigation: we state this as the record gives it. We also state the Commission's later finding that some beliefs "are no longer accurate," while noting the Commission still found crack and powder differ in how they are used.
  2. Public-health view: criminal penalties did not address overdose or addiction. Overdose deaths were 105,007 in 2023 (CDC), with 72,776 involving synthetic opioids. Treatment providers were reported disappointed when 1986 appropriations fell below authorizations (Wikipedia, secondary). Mitigation: this case does not link any penalty to an overdose count. We did not open studies on treatment cost-effectiveness (a RAND study was not retrievable), so no comparison of treatment and enforcement is made.
  3. Objection: the Marihuana Tax Act is often told as a story of hidden motives. Mitigation: the sources opened show a revenue framing, hemp exclusions, a hemp witness and a record quoting a racial descriptor in a prosecutor's letter. They do not show any author's intent. We report words and let readers read the hearing.
  4. Objection: disparity in numbers implies intent. Mitigation: we give counts and shares only. The Commission found no authoritative explanation of the ratio, and we do not supply one.
  5. Objection: Fair Sentencing Act closed the issue. Mitigation: it reduced the ratio to about 18 to 1, it did not end it, and its effect on people already sentenced is not confirmed here.
  6. Objection: federal figures ignore state systems. Mitigation: agreed. Most of the 1,230,100 prisoners are in state prisons, and we do not claim federal trends describe state practice.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • The 1937 House hearing volume (H.R. 6385) was not opened, only a Senate hearing excerpt and the House report. Woodward's testimony is from a secondary source.
  • The 2026 scholarly review of Anslinger and the hemp narrative could not be opened.
  • The 1875 San Francisco ordinance text and enforcement records were not opened.
  • Harrison Act FY1921 conviction count: 1,692 versus 1,583 is not resolved from a scan of Treasury's own report.
  • Boggs and Narcotic Control Act statute text, including any later death-penalty or parole language, was not opened.
  • The CSA's original 1970 schedule list and the Shafer Commission report (March 1972) were not opened.
  • Whether DEA and Justice budget series differ for the same years, and what each counts, was not checked.
  • Forfeiture, grants and contracts as financial incentives were not researched in primary records. Nothing here supports or refutes them.
  • The Commission's own FY2000 and later racial tables, and the 2007 and 2015 Commission reports, were not read.
  • Retroactivity of the 2010 law, and later changes after 2010, were not checked.
  • BJS drug-offense shares of prisoners (Tables 16 to 20) and other overdose series before 2021 were not read.
  • Evidence on treatment versus enforcement outcomes would be needed for any comparison.
