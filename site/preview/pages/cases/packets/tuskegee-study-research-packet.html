2. The article

For forty years a federal agency observed hundreds of poor Black men with syphilis without their informed consent and without offering them effective treatment, and the men had little political or legal power to stop it until a whistleblower and the press brought it to light.

In brief

In 1932 the U.S. Public Health Service began studying syphilis in 600 Black men in Macon County, Alabama: 399 who had the disease and 201 who did not. The men were told they were being treated for "bad blood," and no informed consent was collected. By 1943, according to the CDC, penicillin was the treatment of choice for syphilis, yet the men were not offered it. A federal official, Peter Buxtun, objected in writing in the 1960s and was ignored. The study ended only after an Associated Press story in July 1972. A class action brought a $10 million settlement in 1974, and that year Congress passed the National Research Act, which led to the Belmont Report's rules for human research. The connection to voting is indirect but real. The men lived in a county where a poll tax and literacy test kept nearly all Black residents off the voter rolls for decades, so they had little power to object to how their own government treated them.

The record

Background: who could vote in Macon County

  • 1901: Alabama's constitution required voters aged 21 through 45 to pay a "cumulative poll tax of $1.50 every year" (Section 194) and required applicants to read and write an article of the U.S. Constitution, show a year of employment, and meet property qualifications (Section 181). The ratification vote was 108,613 to 81,734, a result historians largely attribute to fraud in the Black Belt counties. (Encyclopedia of Alabama, Alabama Constitution of 1901)
  • 1941 onward: The Tuskegee Civic Association, founded in 1941, ran voter registration clinics against barriers the Encyclopedia of Alabama lists as intimidation through violence, the poll tax, and unfair literacy tests. (Encyclopedia of Alabama, Tuskegee Boycott)
  • Late 1950s: In the federal voting suit later decided as United States v. Alabama, Macon County figures showed "97.3 percent of the eligible white population was registered to vote (3,016 of 3,100)" compared with "only 7.8 percent of the eligible black population (1,100 out of 14,000)." The source does not give the exact year of these figures. (Encyclopedia of Alabama, United States v. Alabama)
  • 1957 to 1960: Alabama's Local Act No. 140 (1957) redrew the city of Tuskegee from a square into "a strangely irregular twenty-eight-sided figure," alleged to remove "all but four or five of its 400 Negro voters" while "not removing a single white voter or resident." On November 14, 1960, in Gomillion v. Lightfoot, 364 U.S. 339, the Supreme Court (Justice Frankfurter) held that if proven, this would violate the Fifteenth Amendment. (Justia, Gomillion v. Lightfoot)

The study

  • 1932: The U.S. Public Health Service, working with the Tuskegee Institute, began the study. It "initially involved 600 Black men," 399 with syphilis and 201 without. "Informed consent was not collected." Researchers told the men they were being treated for "bad blood." In exchange they received free medical exams, free meals, and burial insurance. (CDC, Timeline) A second CDC page says the 399 "were initially recruited because they already had late-latent syphilis" and the 201 controls "did not have the disease." (CDC, About the study)
  • 1943 to 1953 (penicillin): CDC states that "By 1943, penicillin was the treatment of choice for syphilis," but "the participants in the study were not offered treatment." (CDC timeline; CDC Stacks archived page, last reviewed Dec. 5, 2022) The 1973 federal Ad Hoc Advisory Panel used a later date: "Penicillin therapy should have been made available to the participants in this study not later than 1953." A National Archives blog post gives 1947. See the dates table below. (Ad Hoc Advisory Panel Final Report; National Archives, Rediscovering Black History, 2022)
  • 1966 to 1972: Peter Buxtun, who had worked since 1965 tracking venereal disease cases for the Public Health Service in the San Francisco area, learned of the study in 1966, obtained reports from the Communicable Disease Center, and twice wrote to superiors calling it unethical. He was told to drop the matter. (AP via WECT, "How an AP reporter broke the Tuskegee syphilis story," 2022)
  • July 25, 1972: AP reporter Jean Heller's story ran. AP's 2022 retrospective reports that, as of the story, at least seven men had died directly of syphilis and another 154 of heart disease. (AP via WECT) The National Archives says the leaked information was used by Heller in an article in the Washington Star. (National Archives, Rediscovering Black History, 2017)
  • 1972: The Assistant Secretary for Health and Scientific Affairs appointed an Ad Hoc Advisory Panel, which found the study "ethically unjustified." In October 1972 the panel advised stopping the study; in November 1972 the Assistant Secretary announced its end. (CDC timeline)
  • April 1973: The panel's final report (Charge I report dated April 24, 1973; transmittal April 28, 1973) found the study "ethically unjustified in 1932," reported "no evidence that such consent was obtained," and recommended that Congress create a permanent independent body to regulate federally supported human research. It estimated about 125 participants, including 50 controls, were still alive. It noted that at a 1969 review only seven deaths had been ascribed primarily to syphilis, while panel member Jay Katz wrote that the life expectancy of at least forty subjects was markedly decreased for lack of treatment. Chairman Broadus N. Butler abstained from concurrence in the final report. (Ad Hoc Advisory Panel Final Report, hosted by LSU Law Center)
  • February to March 1973: The Senate Subcommittee on Health held hearings titled "Quality of health care, human experimentation, 1973" (93rd Congress, 1st session; Part 1 on February 21 and 22, 1973). (UNT Monthly Catalog record) A March 9, 1973 Washington Evening Star article reports that on the previous day survivors Charlie Pollard, 87, and Lester Scott, 72, told Sen. Edward Kennedy they had been told they had "bad blood," and that attorney Fred D. Gray represented about 40 survivors and some 15 heirs. (OCR scan; NLM Digital Collections)
  • March 1973: The panel advised HEW to provide all necessary medical care to survivors, and the Tuskegee Health Benefit Program was set up. (CDC timeline)
  • 1973 to 1974: A class-action lawsuit (Pollard v. United States, Civ. A. No. 4126-N) was filed in 1973 and ended in 1974 with a "$10 million out-of-court settlement." (CDC timeline; National Archives, 2017) CDC lists the payments: $37,500 to living members of the syphilitic group, $15,000 to heirs of deceased members, $16,000 to living controls, and $5,000 to heirs of deceased controls. (CDC, About)
  • July 12, 1974: The National Research Act, Public Law 93-348, established the "National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects of Biomedical and Behavioral Research" and required institutions seeking federal grants for human-subjects research to set up "Institutional Review Boards." (GovInfo, 88 Stat. 342) HHS describes the Act as a response to outrage over a case in which the government "let hundreds of Black men in rural Alabama go untreated for syphilis for 40 years." (HHS OHRP, National Research Act 50th Anniversary)
  • 1975: Wives, widows, and children were added to the Tuskegee Health Benefit Program. (CDC timeline)
  • April 18, 1979: The Commission's Belmont Report set out three principles (respect for persons, beneficence, justice) and said "the Tuskegee syphilis study used disadvantaged, rural black men to study the untreated course of a disease that is by no means confined to that population," and that subjects "were deprived of demonstrably effective treatment in order not to interrupt the project, long after such treatment became generally available." (HHS, Read the Belmont Report)
  • May 16, 1997: President Clinton apologized at the White House: "The United States government did something that was wrong -- deeply, profoundly, morally wrong," and "what the United States government did was shameful, and I am sorry." He said "eight men" survived. (Clinton White House archive)
  • January 2004: The last study participant died. January 2009: the last widow receiving program benefits died. (CDC timeline)

Infected wives and children

CDC states that "some women contracted syphilis from men who participated in the study's syphilitic group" (CDC, About), and the National Archives says some wives and children contracted syphilis (National Archives, 2017). Neither gives a count, and we did not find one in a primary source we could load. Figures circulating online (for example, specific numbers of wives and children) are not used here.

The research debate: did the revelation reduce Black men's health-care use?

  • Alsan and Wanamaker, "Tuskegee and the Health of Black Men," Quarterly Journal of Economics 133(1), 2018, pp. 407 to 455, find that "the disclosure of the study in 1972 is correlated with increases in medical mistrust and mortality," estimate that "life expectancy at age 45 for black men fell by up to 1.5 years in response to the disclosure," equal to "approximately 35% of the 1980 life expectancy gap between black and white men," and report fewer outpatient and inpatient physician visits among older Black men. Their design compares groups by closeness to the study's victims, before and after 1972. (NBER Working Paper 22323)
  • Robert Kaestner (Econ Journal Watch 21(2), September 2024, pp. 304 to 338) concludes that the paper "was based on unsupported theoretical assumptions and faulty empirical methods" and that the data show no credible evidence that the revelation increased Black male mortality. He argues the comparison groups were not justified in advance, results are sensitive to that choice, distance from Tuskegee is an implausible channel given national news coverage, and age and cause-of-death patterns do not fit the mechanism. He examined "only the mortality component," not the health-care-use findings. The journal invited Alsan and Wanamaker to reply in a future issue; we found no reply. (Econ Journal Watch abstract; Kaestner PDF; Issue page)
  • Earlier survey work cuts against a simple story: Brandon, Isaac, and LaVeist (Journal of the National Medical Association, July 2005), surveying 277 Black and 101 white Baltimore adults in 2003, found that knowledge of the study "does not increase distrust in medical care," that "Only two-fifths" had heard of it, and that "black race was a consistent predictor of mistrust, even after adjusting for knowledge of the Tuskegee Study." (Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health, 2005)

The fair summary: medical mistrust among Black Americans is well documented, but how much of it, and of later health outcomes, was caused by the 1972 revelation is contested.

What this case shows

  1. The government chose its subjects from among people with the least power to object. The Belmont Report itself says the study "used disadvantaged, rural black men" for a disease "by no means confined to that population." (HHS, Belmont Report)
  2. The men were misled and denied treatment for decades. They were told they had "bad blood," no informed consent was collected, and they were not offered penicillin after it became standard. A federal panel called the study "ethically unjustified in 1932." (CDC timeline; Ad Hoc Panel)
  3. Internal objection failed; only outside exposure worked. Buxtun's written objections in 1966 and later did not stop the study. It ended within months of the July 1972 AP story. (AP via WECT; CDC timeline)
  4. The county had almost no Black political voice to act as a check. Alabama's 1901 constitution imposed a cumulative poll tax and literacy test, and even by the late 1950s only 1,100 of 14,000 eligible Black Macon County residents were registered, versus 3,016 of 3,100 whites. When Tuskegee's Black voters did organize, the state redrew the city to exclude them, which the Supreme Court addressed in Gomillion v. Lightfoot. (Encyclopedia of Alabama, 1901 Constitution; Encyclopedia of Alabama, United States v. Alabama; Justia, Gomillion)
  5. Redress came through courts, Congress, and law, and it changed national rules. A Black lawyer's class action produced a $10 million settlement; Senate hearings put survivors on the record; and the National Research Act of 1974 created the commission behind the Belmont Report and required IRBs for federally funded research. (CDC; 88 Stat. 342)

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: This is a medical ethics case, not a voting case. Mitigation: Correct, and we say so plainly. No source we found shows the study was designed because the men could not vote, and the study's own records do not discuss voting. The tie is indirect and structural: the men lived in a county where nearly all Black residents were kept off the voter rolls, so local and state officials answered to an almost all-white electorate. We should not claim disenfranchisement caused the study.
  2. Objection: The registration figures are from the late 1950s, not the 1930s. Mitigation: True. We did not find verified Macon County registration counts for the 1930s. The 1901 poll tax and literacy test were in force throughout the study, and the late-1950s numbers, after years of registration drives, show how low Black registration still was. Any essay should label the figures by period.
  3. Objection: Federal officials, not local voters, ran the study, so local disenfranchisement is beside the point. Mitigation: Partly fair. The Public Health Service was a federal agency, and the remedy came largely from federal action. But the men also lacked the other routes this project looks at: they were not told the truth, so they could not seek redress, and the one official who objected was ignored for six years.
  4. Objection: The death toll is unclear and sometimes inflated. Mitigation: Agreed. Sources differ (seven deaths ascribed primarily to syphilis at the 1969 review; Katz's "at least forty" with shortened life expectancy; AP's 1972 count of seven plus 154 heart-disease deaths, which are not all attributable to syphilis). We state no total. The argument rests on the undisputed facts of deception and withheld treatment.
  5. Objection: Penicillin was not clearly available in 1943, so "withheld for 30 years" overstates it. Mitigation: The dates do differ (CDC 1943, a National Archives post 1947, the 1973 panel "not later than 1953"). Even on the panel's conservative date, treatment was withheld for nearly two decades.
  6. Objection: The "Tuskegee effect" on Black men's health is overstated. Mitigation: This is a live dispute. Alsan and Wanamaker found large effects; Kaestner's 2024 reanalysis rejects their mortality estimates; the 2005 Baltimore survey found knowledge of the study did not predict mistrust. We present the study as one contested estimate, not settled fact, and do not rely on it for the core argument.
  7. Objection: The system corrected itself. Mitigation: Partly. Exposure led to an end, a settlement, benefits for families, new law, and a presidential apology. But it took forty years, depended on a whistleblower and a reporter, and came after most of the men had died.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • Verified Macon County voter registration counts by race for the 1930s and 1940s would let us describe the study's own era directly rather than through the 1901 rules and late-1950s figures. The 1959 U.S. Commission on Civil Rights report (usccr.gov) failed to load.
  • A primary-source count of infected wives and children (for example, from the 1973 Senate hearing record or the Pollard case file at the National Archives at Atlanta) would let us state a number. We found none we could verify.
  • The exact filing date of Pollard v. United States and the full text of Jean Heller's July 25, 1972 story were not loaded in primary form.
  • A reply from Alsan and Wanamaker to Kaestner, or an independent replication of the health-care-use findings, would move our view of the "Tuskegee effect" in either direction.
  • Evidence that the study's designers considered the men's political powerlessness explicitly would strengthen the voting tie; evidence that comparable studies targeted fully enfranchised groups would weaken it.
