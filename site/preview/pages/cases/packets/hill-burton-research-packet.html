Hill-Burton hospital construction grants (United States, 1946 to 1997)

Not yet reviewed by a person. This page was researched and written with the help of AI tools. Every key claim is labeled by evidence status near the end, but no person has checked the sources yet. Treat it as a draft.
Sources checked 2026-10-19. Preliminary research, not a final verdict. Found an error? Report it on the online page; corrections stay on the record with their date and reason.

A 1946 federal law paid for thousands of hospitals and clinics and tied the money to free-care promises, but it also let states build "separate but equal" facilities by race until a 1963 appeals court struck that clause down.

In brief

In 1946 Congress passed the Hill-Burton Act to help states build hospitals. Over the next five decades it helped pay for roughly 6,800 to 6,900 facilities in more than 4,000 communities, with about $4.6 billion in grants and $1.5 billion in loans by HRSA's count. In return, a hospital promised "a reasonable volume" of free care for people who could not pay. The same section allowed "separate hospital facilities for separate population groups," so states could build segregated wings and hospitals with federal money. In 1962 a Greensboro dentist, George Simkins Jr., and other Black doctors and patients sued two hospitals that had received about $3.2 million. In November 1963 the Fourth Circuit struck the clause down in a 3 to 2 vote. Title VI of the 1964 Civil Rights Act and Medicare in 1966 then pressed hospitals nationwide. The free-care promise lasted on paper, but patients had to find it and apply. The record shows a public program that built much and also wrote exclusion into its text, and that was corrected by people who had been left out.

The record

What the law did

  • The Hospital Survey and Construction Act, known as the Hill-Burton Act, was approved August 13, 1946 (Public Law 79-725, 60 Stat. 1040). It is named for Senators Lister Hill of Alabama and Harold Burton of Ohio. (Public Law 79-725, scanned text, NIH history office copy; Wikipedia, Hill-Burton Act; both opened. Wikipedia gives two dates, July 13 in its text and August 13 in its box. The statute says August 13.)
  • States had to survey their hospitals and plan construction from a "State-wide inventory of existing hospitals and survey of need" (sections 612 and 623). The statute authorized $3 million for surveys and $75 million a year for construction grants for the first five years (sections 611 and 621). (Public Law 79-725; opened, scan partly garbled)
  • Each funded hospital had to make available "a reasonable volume of hospital services to persons unable to pay therefor." (Section 622(f); Public Law 79-725)
  • The same section required service "without discrimination on account of race, creed, or color," and then added that "an exception shall be made in cases where separate hospital facilities are provided for separate population groups." That is the "separate but equal" provision. (Section 622(f); Public Law 79-725)
  • Later law changed the program. A 1975 amendment created Title XVI of the Public Health Service Act and moved the free-care duty from 20 years to "in perpetuity" for new grants. HRSA says federal funding stopped in 1997. (HRSA, Hill-Burton Free and Reduced-Cost Health Care; HRSA, compliance page; both opened)

The separate-but-equal clause and the Simkins case

  • In Simkins v. Moses H. Cone Memorial Hospital, 323 F.2d 959 (4th Cir. 1963), Dr. George Simkins Jr., a dentist, Dr. Alvin Blount, other Black physicians and dentists, and patients sued Cone Hospital and Wesley Long Community Hospital. They said the hospitals denied staff privileges to Black doctors and admission to Black patients. (NCpedia, "Simkins v. Cone"; Wikipedia, Simkins; both opened)
  • The opinion records Hill-Burton appropriations of $1,269,950 for Cone and $1,948,800 for Long, together about $3.2 million. Cone's share was about 15 percent of its construction cost (the opinion's table shows 17.2 percent) and Long's about 50 percent (table: 49.6 percent). Wikipedia gives $2.8 million, and NCpedia gives $3.2 million "in state and federal" funds. The opinion's own amounts add to $3,218,750. (Opinion, law.resource.org copy; opened)
  • Judge Simon Sobeloff wrote for the majority, joined by Albert Bryan and J. Spencer Bell. Judge Clement Haynsworth dissented, joined by Herbert Boreman. The majority held "we have state action here" because the hospitals "operate as integral parts of comprehensive joint or intermeshing state and federal plans." It held that the federal provisions "undertaking to authorize segregation by state-connected institutions are unconstitutional," and that the implementing regulation, 42 C.F.R. 53.112, fell with them. It left the general ban on discrimination standing. (Opinion; opened, through a summary tool, not a verbatim read)
  • The dissent argued that Hill-Burton aid "differs neither in kind nor degree from any other provision for grants in aid" of private work, so it did not turn private hospitals into the state. (Opinion; opened)
  • U.S. Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy filed on the side of the plaintiffs (NCpedia). The Supreme Court declined to hear an appeal, so the ruling bound only the Fourth Circuit states: Maryland, North Carolina, South Carolina, Virginia and West Virginia (Wikipedia, Simkins).
  • A 1965 report of the U.S. Commission on Civil Rights says nearly 7,000 projects before 1963 had been classified "nondiscriminatory," yet the department's reading still allowed patient segregation and exclusion of Black staff and trainees. After Simkins the Public Health Service stopped approving new separate-but-equal applications. (U.S. Commission on Civil Rights, Equal Opportunity in Hospitals and Health Facilities, March 1965; opened)

Segregation before and after: contested or thinly sourced

  • A summary at the aasurg.org blog says only 6 percent of Southern hospitals served Black patients without restriction, 31 percent denied admission, and 47 percent used separate floors. It does not name an original survey. Medicare in 1966 gave the government a reimbursement lever; the blog says compliance had been under 20 percent before the campaign and was 92 percent the day before Medicare began and 98 percent six months later, while several hundred Deep South hospitals without federal funds stayed out of Medicare. (Health Policy in Action, brief history of hospital desegregation; opened. A secondary source with no cited primary table.)
  • Economists Douglas Almond, Kenneth Chay and Michael Greenstone estimate that Title VI hospital desegregation helped 5,000 to 7,000 additional Black infants survive from 1965 to 1975. Their abstract does not mention Hill-Burton. (SSRN abstract, MIT working paper 07-04; abstract opened, full paper not read)

Free care: promise and enforcement

  • The free-care duty is the lesser of 10 percent of federal aid received (adjusted) or 3 percent of annual operating costs, less Medicare and Medicaid payments. Title VI grants carry a 20-year duty, loans until repaid, Title XVI grants forever. Eligible patients are at or below the poverty guideline for free care; up to 200 percent (300 percent for nursing homes) for optional reduced-cost care. Patients must apply; care is not automatic. (CRS 98-968; HRSA; both opened)
  • A January 1986 opinion column by Dick Meister claims "study after study" found private recipients treated poor patients mainly when Medicare or Medicaid paid, and that many turned people away. It names Gordon Bonnyman, a Nashville attorney, but cites no study by name. (Christian Science Monitor, Jan. 23, 1986; opened. Opinion.) We could not confirm those studies.

What this case shows

  1. The racial rule was written into the statute, so it was voted on in public. Section 622(f) said in plain words that separate facilities were allowed. Voters and members of Congress could read it. The thesis question, what ordinary people could know and vote on, is answered yes for the text. What most patients could not see was how a state agency applied it to each project. (Public Law 79-725)
  2. People who were excluded went to court, not to the ballot. Black physicians and patients in one North Carolina city, with the NAACP and the Justice Department, removed the clause in 1963. Congress then acted in 1964. The fix came from the excluded, not from the program's ordinary review. (Opinion; NCpedia)
  3. Taxpayers bought buildings and a promise, and the promise was hard to find. A patient had to know the duty existed, apply, and prove income. The notice duty, a written statement of denial and a federal hotline exist because the right was easy to miss. (CRS; HRSA)
  4. Money followed state plans, so local power shaped who was served. States ran surveys and set priorities. The Commission on Civil Rights found that "nondiscriminatory" labels still allowed segregated wards. (Commission, 1965)
  5. Success and exclusion came in the same program. Construction reached over 4,000 communities, and the same funds built facilities where Black patients were separated or turned away. A fair account holds both. (HRSA; Commission, 1965)

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: The program greatly improved access, especially in rural areas. Mitigation: We agree it built thousands of facilities in over 4,000 communities (HRSA, CRS) and may have raised bed supply. The bed and "nearly 40%" figures come from Wikipedia only, so we flag them. The thesis is about who was included, and both facts are true.
  2. Objection: Separate but equal was the political price of passage and was normal in 1946. Mitigation: This is probably right as history. The clause was lawful under the then-current reading of the Constitution. We do not say the 1946 Congress acted in bad faith. We say the clause existed, what it allowed, and that a court removed it after 17 years. We did not read the 1946 floor debate, so we cannot say how it was justified.
  3. Objection: Simkins was narrow and did not integrate hospitals by itself. Mitigation: Correct. The ruling bound five states, and the Supreme Court denied review. The blog source says it lacked an enforcement tool like the one that followed Brown. Title VI in 1965 and Medicare in 1966 did much of the practical work. We do not credit the court alone.
  4. Objection: The free-care duty was weak and often unmet. Mitigation: The strongest statements come from a 1986 opinion column. HRSA reports more than $6 billion in free care since 1980, which is a program figure, not an audit. We have no independent compliance study, so we do not claim the program failed, only that proof is thin on both sides.
  5. Objection: Federal money should not be tied to private hospital conduct. Mitigation: The Simkins dissent made this argument and we report it. The majority disagreed because of joint state and federal planning.
  6. Objection: Wikipedia and a blog are weak sources. Mitigation: Key legal facts rest on the statute scan, the opinion text, a Commission report and CRS. Wikipedia-only figures are labeled.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • We did not read the 1946 committee reports or floor debates. How the separate-but-equal exception got into the bill, and who argued for it, is unverified.
  • The bed counts (3.2 to 4.5 per 1,000) and "nearly 40%" of short-stay beds come only from Wikipedia. Unverified.
  • Facility and dollar totals differ across HRSA, CRS and a 1986 column. We have not found a single official ledger.
  • The federal share: the statute scan is garbled, and sources say one-third, one-third to two-thirds, and 66 2/3 percent for Kentucky. Unresolved.
  • The Supreme Court's denial of review (date and citation) was not confirmed from an original docket. Unverified.
  • The hospital segregation percentages (6, 31 and 47 percent) and Medicare compliance figures (92 and 98 percent) rest on one secondary blog without a named primary survey. Unverified.
  • The Almond, Chay and Greenstone paper was read only as an abstract and does not discuss Hill-Burton directly.
  • No independent audit of free-care compliance was found. The 1986 claims are from an opinion column.
  • The 1997 end date comes from HRSA; Wikipedia and a Kentucky source say construction funding stopped about 1974. Which programs ended when needs the Public Health Service Act text.
  • What would change our minds: a primary survey showing how many Hill-Burton projects were built as separate-race facilities, or a federal audit of free-care delivery.
