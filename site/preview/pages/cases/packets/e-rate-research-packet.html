2. The article

A 1996 law let the FCC order discounted telecom and internet service for schools and libraries, paid by fees on phone service; classroom internet access rose from a minority to nearly all rooms, but audits found waste and fraud and the government's own watchdog said the gains could not be cleanly credited to the program.

In brief

The Telecommunications Act of 1996 told phone carriers to offer schools and libraries service at lower rates, and in 1997 the FCC turned that into the E-rate program, with discounts of 20 to 90 percent paid from fees on telephone service. Funding began in 1998 under a $2.25 billion annual cap. Federal data show instructional rooms with internet access rising from 51 percent in 1998 to 94 percent in 2005, though other sources give different starting points. A California study found about 66 percent more connected classrooms than there would have been, with the biggest response in urban schools with many Black and Hispanic students, and no change in test scores. Audits told a harder story. The FCC's inspector general reported that about a third of audits found substantial violations, a Senate hearing heard of more than 40 criminal cases, and the Government Accountability Office said in 2005 that the data could not show how much of the new access the program caused. The Supreme Court upheld the program's funding in 2025. The record shows a public program that reached many classrooms and a public that had little means to measure it.

The record

The law and the program

  • Section 254(h)(1)(B) of the Communications Act, added by the Telecommunications Act of 1996, requires carriers to serve "elementary schools, secondary schools, and libraries for educational purposes" on request "at rates less than the amounts charged for similar services to other parties." The FCC sets the discount for interstate service and states for intrastate service, as "appropriate and necessary to ensure affordable access to and use of such services." Section 254(b) lists, among its universal service principles, access to advanced services for schools, health care and libraries. (47 U.S.C. 254, Cornell Legal Information Institute; opened)
  • The FCC adopted its implementing order (Order 97-157) on May 7, 1997. Discounts run from 20 to 90 percent, depending on poverty level and urban or rural status. The program is paid for out of the Universal Service Fund, which is fed by fees on telecommunications providers, and a private nonprofit, the Universal Service Administrative Company (USAC), runs it under FCC direction. Funding began in 1998 with an annual cap of $2.25 billion. (Wikipedia, E-Rate; GAO-05-151; both opened. The May 1997 date is from Wikipedia only; we could not open the FCC order or FCC program page.)
  • The Children's Internet Protection Act (2000) requires schools and libraries that get E-rate discounts to filter certain content. (Wikipedia; one source)

What GAO found in 2005

  • The FCC had not set useful performance goals. GAO wrote that "there was a lack of data for accurately characterizing program results in terms of increased Internet access." It also raised accountability concerns about the FCC and USAC structure and found about 527 appeals pending, roughly 458 past the 90 days the rules allow. GAO made three recommendations to the FCC chair, all later marked implemented: assess accountability, set goals and measures, and cut the appeals backlog. (GAO-05-151; page summary opened, not the full report)

What research on effects found

  • Austan Goolsbee and Jonathan Guryan used data on every California school from 1996 to 2000. The subsidy raised internet investment (a price elasticity of demand between -0.9 and -2.2). By the last sample year there were about 66 percent more internet classrooms than without it. Urban schools and schools with large Black and Hispanic enrollments responded most; rural schools and mostly white and Asian schools much less. They found "no measurable impact on any measure of student achievement." Before the program, the richest schools had almost 50 percent more internet-connected classrooms per teacher; the authors report that gap closed. They note it may be too early for gains and that California may not represent other states. (NBER Working Paper 9090; NBER Digest, Feb. 2003; both opened, the later journal version in the Review of Economics and Statistics, 2006, was not opened)
  • Wikipedia also mentions a Texas study linking the program to better test scores, graduation and college admissions. We did not find or open that study. (Unverified)

Waste, fraud and oversight

  • FCC Inspector General H. Walker Feaster III told a House subcommittee that the program had an unacceptably high risk of fraud, waste and abuse; of 122 audits, about a third found substantial violations. Example: about 74,000 wireless cards bought in Puerto Rico in 1999 for roughly $24 million were found unopened. Rep. Jim Greenwood said children were "deprived of the educational benefits." Education groups backed more monitoring but defended the program. (NBC News)
  • At the Senate hearing on October 5, 2004, Chairman McCain said over 40 criminal cases were pending, that NEC Business Network Solutions agreed to plead guilty and pay $20.6 million, that Atlanta had spent nearly $73 million, and that USAC had disallowed over $500 million in applications linked to IBM's bidding approach. Feaster's statement said 48 of 135 audits (36 percent) found noncompliance, with over $17 million in recommended recoveries, and that he could not "provide assurance that the program is protected from fraud, waste and abuse." Senators Olympia Snowe and Jay Rockefeller defended the program: Snowe called it "an enormous success." Senator Conrad Burns said some waste and fraud existed and that finding it was good. (Senate hearing; opened through a summary tool)
  • The House staff report (November 2005, bipartisan) found the FCC "completely lacking" tangible measures of impact; only 65 of 135 audited beneficiaries fully compliant; an IG estimate of up to $180 million a year in improper payments; more than $100 million for a Puerto Rico network never made operational; USAC denying about $517 million for 21 districts in funding year 2002 for non-competitive procurement; and $832 million in E-rate funds to IBM over the life of the program. It also found consultants and an advocacy group with financial stakes in vendor business. (House Committee Print 109-E; the first 100,000 characters read, the last 34,000 not read) (report)
  • Counts of audits and compliance differ across sources: 122 audits with about a third showing violations (NBC News), 135 audits with 48 noncompliant (Senate record) and 135 audits with 65 fully compliant (House report). These can fit together (some audits showed partial compliance) but none of our sources says so, so we do not reconcile them.

Law and legal challenges

  • In June 2025 the Supreme Court, in FCC v. Consumers' Research, held 6 to 3 that the universal service contribution scheme does not violate the nondelegation doctrine. Justice Kagan wrote for the majority; Justice Gorsuch dissented, joined by Justices Thomas and Alito. The Fifth Circuit had ruled against the scheme in 2024. (SCOTUSblog case page; Education Week; both opened)
  • Wikipedia says the Fifth Circuit ruled in 2014 that the False Claims Act could not be used for E-rate pricing fraud. We did not verify this. We also did not verify Wikipedia's later items about FCC votes in 2025 and 2026.

What this case shows

  1. The rule and its funding were legislated, then argued over in public. Section 254 is a short statute, the FCC order set the discount scale, and the Supreme Court in 2025 ruled on how it is funded. Anyone can read each step. Yet most people never saw the "fee" on their phone bill that pays for it. (47 U.S.C. 254; SCOTUSblog)
  2. The program aimed at the less well-off, and one study found the gap closed. Discounts run from 20 to 90 percent by poverty and rurality. In California, richer schools had almost 50 percent more connected classrooms per teacher before the program; the authors report the gap closed. That speaks to who was included. (Goolsbee and Guryan)
  3. Rural schools were less served, even so. The same study found rural schools responded much less to the subsidy, and Education Week reported rural fiber applications stalled at the FCC in 2017. Included on paper, less reached in practice. (NBER; Education Week)
  4. The public could not tell what the money bought. GAO said the FCC lacked data to characterize results, and the House staff report found "completely lacking" measures of impact. Access rose, but no one could show how much was due to the program. (GAO-05-151; Committee Print 109-E)
  5. The costs of weak controls were large. Audits, criminal cases and settlements show the money was exposed to fraud, and the cost fell on phone customers and on children whose schools lost funds. (Senate hearing; House report)

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: Connectivity rose because of the market, not E-rate. Mitigation: GAO supports caution, and the guardrail in our own reading is the same. The California study is the only causal estimate we opened, and it finds a 66 percent effect on classrooms there. The national series differ by source, so we do not credit the program with the full rise.
  2. Objection: The program is a success and fraud was a small share. Mitigation: Senators Snowe and Rockefeller said so at the hearing. We include their words. We found no official improper-payment rate for the whole program in the sources we opened. The IG estimate of up to $180 million a year and the audit rates are the figures we have, and we show them with their limits.
  3. Objection: Subsidies without learning gains waste money. Mitigation: The only effect study we opened found no achievement gain, but its authors say it may be too early and that California may not represent the nation. Access is a different goal from test scores.
  4. Objection: The tax-like fee is an unconstitutional delegation. Mitigation: The Fifth Circuit agreed in 2024; the Supreme Court disagreed 6 to 3 in 2025. We report both and take no side.
  5. Objection: Wikipedia and news articles are weak sources. Mitigation: Core claims rest on the statute, GAO, a Senate hearing print, a House committee print and NCES. Wikipedia-only items are flagged below.
  6. Objection: Audit samples were not random. Mitigation: We did not find how audits were chosen, so we do not generalize from them to all applicants.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • We could not open the FCC's 1997 order or FCC program pages (fetch failed). The May 7, 1997 date, discount range and cap rely on GAO, NBC News and Wikipedia. The NCES 2006 report page also failed to load.
  • GAO-05-151 was read as a landing-page summary, not the full report.
  • The national connectivity series disagree (51, 27, 14 and 3 percent starting points). We need NCES's original tables for 1994 to 2005 and their definitions.
  • Wikipedia's Texas study (test scores, graduation) was not found. Unverified.
  • Wikipedia's claims about the 2014 Fifth Circuit False Claims Act ruling, a 2025 FCC vote on hotspots and a 2026 proposal to scale back the program were not verified.
  • We found no official overall improper-payment rate for E-rate. Unverified.
  • The later years of the Senate and House records (2006 to 2024), including USAC reforms and program-wide audit results, were not read.
  • The 2017 Education Week article gave no 2013 baseline for the 100 kbps measure.
  • The Goolsbee and Guryan journal version (2006) was not opened; only the working paper and digest.
  • What would change our minds: a national study that isolates E-rate's effect on classroom access, or a full-program audit with a stated sample method.
