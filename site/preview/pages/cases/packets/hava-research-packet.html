2. The article

After the disputed 2000 presidential count, Congress passed a 2002 law that gave states money and new federal minimums for voting machines, voter lists, provisional ballots and first-time mail-registrant ID, and the record shows promised funds, access and security goals pulling in different directions.

In brief

On December 12, 2000, the Supreme Court ended Florida's recount in Bush v. Gore, holding that uneven recount standards violated equal protection. Less than two years later, on October 29, 2002, President Bush signed the Help America Vote Act. It created the Election Assistance Commission and set federal minimums: voting systems that let a voter check the ballot, a statewide voter list, provisional ballots for voters whose names were not found, and ID for some first-time voters who registered by mail. Congress authorized about $3.86 billion and, by 2010, had appropriated about $3.54 billion. The ID rule is narrow, yet the national debate over photo ID shows how access and security goals collide. A 2005 commission urged photo ID, and one of its members warned it could block eligible voters. The record shows who wrote the rules and what they say. It does not show, in the sources we read, how many voters were helped or turned away.

The record

What the law is

  • The Help America Vote Act of 2002 (HAVA), Public Law 107-252, was signed by President George W. Bush on October 29, 2002. The Election Assistance Commission (EAC) says it made "sweeping reforms to the nation's voting process" after problems seen in the 2000 election. (EAC, About HAVA; Public Law 107-252, GovInfo; both opened. The GovInfo page lists "Oct. 29, 2002" and does not name the signer in the part we read; the signer comes from the EAC page.)
  • HAVA created the EAC as "an independent entity" with "four members appointed by the President, by and with the advice and consent of the Senate," and requires "at least three of its members" to approve any action. (Pub. L. 107-252, sections 201, 203, 208; opened.) The Congressional Research Service (CRS) says the EAC replaced the Federal Election Commission's Office of Election Administration, has had no sunset, and has never been reauthorized, though it has been funded every year since FY2005. (CRS RS20898, 2011; opened)
  • Title III set federal minimums that states must meet. We opened the statute text for these: voting systems must "permit the voter to verify (in a private and independent manner) the votes selected by the voter on the ballot before the ballot is cast and counted" (section 301); provisional ballots (section 302); a computerized statewide voter list and identification rules for some mail registrants (section 303). Section 401 lets the U.S. Attorney General sue for enforcement. (Pub. L. 107-252, titles of sections 301 to 303 and 401, and the section 301 text; opened. The GovInfo page was cut off before the operative text of sections 302, 303 and 401, so the wording below comes from the U.S. Code.)

Provisional ballots (52 U.S.C. 21082)

  • A voter who is not on the list, or whom an official says is ineligible, "may cast a provisional ballot in that election," after signing a written affirmation of being registered and eligible.
  • The state must give written information on how to check whether the ballot counted and, if not, why, through a free system such as a toll-free number or website.
  • If the election official finds the voter eligible under state law, the ballot "shall be counted as a vote in that election," following state law. Eligibility is therefore decided by state rules. (52 U.S.C. 21082, Cornell LII copy of the U.S. Code; opened)

Statewide list and first-time mail registrants (52 U.S.C. 21083)

  • A registration application for a federal election must include either a driver's license number or the last four digits of the Social Security number.
  • A voter who registered by mail and has not voted in the jurisdiction before must show "current and valid photo identification" or a document such as a "utility bill" showing name and address, in person or with a mailed ballot.
  • Exempt: mail registrants who sent a copy of such ID with the application, or whose license or Social Security digits were matched to an existing record, and voters covered by the federal law on military and overseas absentee voting. (52 U.S.C. 21083, Cornell LII copy of the U.S. Code; opened)
  • This is a narrow federal rule. CRS says state voter-ID rules vary "from nothing beyond the federal rule to photo ID for all voters," and that whether stricter ID prevents fraud or blocks eligible voters "remains unresolved." (CRS RS20898; opened)

Context: Bush v. Gore

  • On December 12, 2000, the U.S. Supreme Court reversed the Florida Supreme Court's order for a statewide manual recount. The per curiam opinion held that recount standards varied across counties and even within a county, which violated equal protection, and that no valid recount could be finished by the federal deadline. The remedy vote was 5 to 4; seven Justices saw a constitutional problem. The Court wrote that its "consideration is limited to the present circumstances." (Bush v. Gore, 531 U.S. 98 (2000), Justia; opened; the last part of the page was not read)
  • CRS notes that more than a dozen election reform bills had been introduced in Congress before the Supreme Court ruling. (CRS; opened) We did not find, in what we read, a document that states HAVA was passed because of the Court's ruling alone. HAVA's own purposes are stated in the EAC summary above.

Later review of the rules

  • In September 2005 the Commission on Federal Election Reform, co-chaired by former President Jimmy Carter and former Secretary of State James Baker, recommended that all voters show photo ID and that electronic machines produce voter-verifiable paper records. Commission member Spencer Overton objected that the ID rule was stricter than any state's law and could block eligible voters; Rep. John Conyers also opposed it. (Nextgov, Sept. 2005; opened; a secondary news report)

What this case shows

  1. A public failure led to a public rule. A recount dispute that ended in a Supreme Court order was followed by a statute with open text, a named commission and enforcement by the Attorney General. People could read the minimums and see who had to meet them. (Justia; Pub. L. 107-252)
  2. Access tools were put in the law. The provisional ballot gives a voter who is missing from the list a way to vote, and the law requires a free way to learn whether it counted and why not. That is information given back to the ordinary voter. (52 U.S.C. 21082)
  3. Security and access were both built in, and they pull apart. The ID rule for first-time mail registrants and the matching of license or Social Security digits are aimed at fraud and list accuracy. The 2005 commission's debate shows the same rules can be read as a barrier. The record does not settle which risk is larger. (52 U.S.C. 21083; Nextgov; CRS)
  4. Authorization is not funding. Congress authorized about $3.86 billion and had appropriated about $3.54 billion by FY2010. States were told to meet standards, and the money to do it depended on later votes. Local officials and voters could not tell from the statute alone what would be paid for. (CRS)
  5. Counting a provisional ballot depends on state law. The federal text sends the eligibility decision to the state. Two voters in two states can cast the same kind of ballot and face different rules for counting it. (52 U.S.C. 21082)

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: HAVA fixed what went wrong in 2000. Supporters can point to replaced punch-card systems, statewide lists and provisional ballots. Mitigation: we list these as stated goals and funded programs. We did not find outcome measures in our sources, such as error or rejection rates by year, so we make no claim that problems were reduced.
  2. Objection: ID rules protect the integrity of elections. Mitigation: Yes. The Carter-Baker commission, which included both parties' figures, urged photo ID. We report the rule, the commission's proposal and the dissent, and CRS's statement that the effect is unresolved.
  3. Objection: ID and list-matching rules keep eligible people from voting. Mitigation: The federal rule is narrow and has several routes (license number, Social Security digits, a bill or bank record). We do not claim a number of people were blocked, because we did not find a source that measures it.
  4. Objection: Congress underfunded the Act. Mitigation: The gap between $3.86 billion authorized and $3.54 billion appropriated through FY2010 is from CRS. We did not read why. Congress also did not repeal the standards, and states had other funds we did not review.
  5. Objection: Bush v. Gore is partisan ground and should not frame HAVA. Mitigation: We state only what the Court held and what CRS says about pending bills. We do not assess the ruling and do not link any party to HAVA's design.
  6. Objection: Cornell LII is not the Statutes at Large. Mitigation: It is a copy of the U.S. Code, which codifies the Act. The GovInfo public law page would not show the operative text of sections 302 and 303 in our read. A reader should check the Code on uscode.house.gov.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • Effective dates for sections 301 to 303 (several were later than 2002) were not read. Unverified.
  • Appropriations after FY2010 and total state spending by category were not found. The EAC spending report would not open.
  • Provisional ballot counts and rejection rates by year and state (EAC Election Administration and Voting Survey) were not read; the 2024 report would not open as text. We make no claim on rates.
  • Whether the Act's ID rule reduced fraud or blocked eligible voters: no measuring study was opened.
  • Why requirements payments were not appropriated in FY2005 to FY2007 is not in the sources read.
  • Full text of the operative sections of Public Law 107-252 (302, 303, 401, 906) should be checked in the Statutes at Large.
  • The EAC's later history (quorum gaps, voting system certification, 2005 and later standards) was not researched.
  • Any state-level court cases applying HAVA provisional ballot rules (for example on out-of-precinct ballots) were not reviewed.
