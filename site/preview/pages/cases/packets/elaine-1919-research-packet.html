2. The article

Black sharecroppers who organized to get fair payment for their cotton were met with mass killing and then mass prosecution by an all-white justice system, and the U.S. Supreme Court ruled in Moore v. Dempsey (1923) that a mob-dominated trial violates due process.

In brief

In the fall of 1919, Black sharecroppers in Phillips County, Arkansas, met at a church near Elaine to hire a lawyer and seek fair payment for their cotton. A shootout at the meeting left a white officer dead, and in the days that followed armed white crowds and federal troops swept the area. Five white people died. The number of Black people killed is unknown, and estimates range from dozens to several hundred. Afterward, 122 Black people were charged, and twelve men were sentenced to death after trials that, according to their later court filings, lasted under an hour before all-white juries. Those juries were all white partly because Arkansas required jurors to be voters, and laws from the 1890s had pushed most Black citizens out of voting. State courts upheld the convictions. Only in 1923 did the U.S. Supreme Court rule, in Moore v. Dempsey, that a trial dominated by a mob denies due process. The case shows how losing the vote could also mean losing a fair hearing.

The record

Background: who could vote and serve (1891 to 1919)

  • 1891: Arkansas passed an election law that created standard state ballots, adopted the secret ballot, and centralized election control in a state board. The Encyclopedia of Arkansas calls it "a subtle literacy test for voters." It reports that the estimated Black vote fell "from 71 percent of eligible voters in 1890 to 38 percent in 1892," while the white vote fell from 75 to 67 percent, and that Black state representatives went from eleven in 1891 to none by 1895. (Encyclopedia of Arkansas, Election Law of 1891)
  • 1892 to 1893: Voters considered a poll tax amendment in September 1892; it took effect after April 14, 1893. The fee was one dollar. Because the original was found not properly ratified, a second poll tax amendment (Amendment 9) was submitted in 1908. "There were 65,000 fewer voters in 1894 than in 1890." (Encyclopedia of Arkansas, Poll Tax)
  • To vote, "a person had to show a receipt or some other evidence that the tax had been paid." The same entry notes that Arkansas "was not the most restrictive in the South," since Black men "could legally vote," and that Arkansas "long had an all-white Democratic primary." (Encyclopedia of Arkansas, Voting and Voting Rights)
  • Because one party controlled most offices, its primary was the decisive contest, and the all-white primary was a "formidable barrier to African American voters in Arkansas." (UA Little Rock Public Radio, 2016) The Arkansas Supreme Court upheld all-white primaries in Robinson v. Holman (1930). (Encyclopedia of Arkansas, Arkansas Negro Democratic Association)
  • Jury service was tied to voting. In an Arkansas Supreme Court opinion of the same era (141 Ark. 496, an appeal from St. Francis County involving events of May 1919), the court stated: "Under the statutes of this State, a petit juror must be an elector," and analyzed a juror's qualification under the poll tax amendment. (Caselaw Access Project, 141 Ark. 496)

The events of 1919

  • April to September 1919: Lodges of the Progressive Farmers and Household Union of America were set up across Phillips County. Organizer Robert Lee Hill urged tenant farmers to "demand better prices for their crops," and enlisted the Little Rock firm Bratton, Bratton and Casey to provide legal aid. (UA Little Rock exhibit, Robert Lee Hill)
  • Night of September 30, 1919: About 100 Black sharecroppers met at a church at Hoop Spur, three miles north of Elaine, "to obtain better payments for their cotton crops." The union had contracted with lawyer Ulysses S. Bratton. Armed Black guards stood outside. A shootout followed; accounts conflict about who fired first. W. A. Adkins, a white railroad security officer, was killed and Deputy Sheriff Charles Pratt was wounded. (Encyclopedia of Arkansas, Elaine Massacre) The petitioners later said "their meeting was to employ counsel for protection against extortions practiced upon them by the landowners." (Moore v. Dempsey, Justia)
  • October 1, 1919: An estimated 500 to 1,000 armed white people converged on the Elaine area, where Black residents "outnumbered whites ... by a ratio of ten to one." County authorities telegraphed Gov. Charles Brough for federal troops. (Encyclopedia of Arkansas)
  • October 2, 1919: "583 soldiers from the Third Division and the Fifty-Seventh Infantry" arrived in Elaine at 8:15 a.m. and stayed until October 7. They took "hundreds of black sharecroppers into custody" and held them in a school used as a stockade. (UA Little Rock exhibit, Military Intervention) The same day the four Johnston brothers were killed while returning to Helena from a hunting trip. (Encyclopedia of Arkansas)
  • Deaths: Five white people died. The number of Black people killed "is unknown"; estimates "ranged into the hundreds." (Encyclopedia of Arkansas) See the disputed-numbers table below.
  • October 1919: Gov. Brough appointed a Committee of Seven (planters, businessmen, officials) to investigate. Within days, 285 Black people were moved to the Helena jail, which had space for 48. (Encyclopedia of Arkansas) According to affidavits summarized by the Supreme Court, the Committee called "colored witnesses and having them whipped and tortured until they would say what was wanted," and "a mob marched to the jail for the purpose of lynching them." (Moore v. Dempsey, Cornell LII; Justia)
  • October 31, 1919: A Phillips County grand jury charged 122 African Americans. (Encyclopedia of Arkansas)
  • November 1919: By November 5, the first twelve Black men tried (the "Elaine Twelve") were convicted of murder and sentenced to death. Sixty-five others took plea bargains of up to twenty-one years. Appointed defense attorney Jacob Fink admitted he had not interviewed witnesses, did not seek a change of venue, and did not challenge a single juror. (Encyclopedia of Arkansas) In the Moore defendants' trial, as alleged and accepted on demurrer, "The trial lasted about three-quarters of an hour and in less than five minutes the jury brought in a verdict of guilty of murder in the first degree," before "a white jury," with "blacks being systematically excluded from both grand and petit juries." (Cornell LII)
  • 1920: Hill was arrested in Kansas on January 20, 1920; Arkansas sought his return, and after NAACP pressure on Kansas officials he was free by October. (UA Little Rock exhibit) The Arkansas Supreme Court affirmed the Moore group's convictions. It held the jury-exclusion claim was raised too late ("comes too late to be now considered") and said of the mob atmosphere, "We are unable, however, to say that this must necessarily have been the case." (Caselaw Access Project, 143 Ark. 158) The six Ware defendants won new trials on a technical point about verdict forms; they were later freed. Black attorney Scipio Africanus Jones led the defense with an NAACP-hired firm. (Encyclopedia of Arkansas)
  • February 19, 1923: In Moore v. Dempsey, 261 U.S. 86, Justice Holmes wrote for the Court that "if in fact a trial is dominated by a mob so that there is an actual interference with the course of justice, there is a departure from due process of law," and that relief is available if "the whole proceeding is a mask." Justices McReynolds and Sutherland dissented. The Court did not find the facts true; it said "we are not affirming the facts to be as stated, but only what we must take them to be," and sent the case back for a hearing. (Cornell LII; Justia)
  • March 1923 to January 13, 1925: Jones negotiated pleas to second-degree murder for the Moore defendants. On January 13, 1925, Gov. Thomas McRae released them on indefinite furloughs. (Encyclopedia of Arkansas)
  • Later: All-white primaries were held unconstitutional in Smith v. Allwright (1944), and Arkansas Democrats admitted Black members in 1950 after the Gatlin suit. (Encyclopedia of Arkansas, ANDA) Arkansas replaced the poll tax with voter registration by an amendment approved in November 1964. (Encyclopedia of Arkansas, Poll Tax) A memorial to the victims was dedicated in Helena-West Helena on September 29, 2019. (Encyclopedia of Arkansas)

The safest statement: five white people died; the number of Black people killed is unknown, with serious estimates from dozens into the hundreds.

What this case shows

  1. Organizing for economic redress was treated as insurrection. The sharecroppers' stated aim was to hire a lawyer to get fair payment for their crops, a lawful route to redress. The response was mass violence and the charging of 122 Black people. (Encyclopedia of Arkansas; Moore v. Dempsey)
  2. The courtroom was closed to Black citizens, and jury service ran through the ballot box. The Supreme Court described the petitioners' allegation of "blacks being systematically excluded from both grand and petit juries." Under Arkansas law a petit juror had to be an "elector," and elector status was tied to the poll tax amendment. So the disfranchising laws of the 1890s fed directly into who judged the defendants. (Cornell LII; 141 Ark. 496; Election Law of 1891)
  3. Political power was already gone, so there was no local political check. Black legislative representation fell to zero by 1895 and did not return until 1973; the decisive primary was all-white. In a county area where Black residents outnumbered whites ten to one, the officials, prosecutors, judge, and jurors were all chosen without meaningful Black votes. (Poll Tax entry; Voting and Voting Rights; Elaine entry)
  4. State courts did not correct the problem. The Arkansas Supreme Court rejected the jury-exclusion claim as untimely and declined to find mob domination. Redress came only from federal court, four years later, after outside lawyers and funding. (143 Ark. 158; Moore v. Dempsey)
  5. The case changed national law. Moore v. Dempsey established that a mob-dominated trial is a "departure from due process of law" that federal courts can review on habeas corpus, a lasting protection for people with little local power. (Cornell LII)

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: The death toll is unknowable, and big numbers like 856 are not reliable. Mitigation: Agreed. We report the range and its sources and rest no argument on a precise count. The argument depends on facts not in dispute: five white deaths, at least dozens of Black deaths by any serious estimate, 122 Black people charged, and twelve death sentences. (We did not find a source stating whether any white participant was charged; that should be checked before claiming none were.)
  2. Objection: Shots were fired at Hoop Spur and a white officer was killed; maybe it was a real insurrection. Mitigation: Accounts do conflict about who fired first, and we say so. Spokesman E. M. Allen called it a "deliberately planned insurrection"; most historians disagree, per the Encyclopedia of Arkansas. Even if some union members fired, that would not justify the trials described: 45 minutes, a five-minute verdict, no defense witnesses. (Encyclopedia of Arkansas)
  3. Objection: The Supreme Court never found the torture and mob claims true. Mitigation: Correct. The Court took the allegations as true on demurrer and ordered a hearing. But the state chose to demur rather than contest the facts, and the cases were then resolved by plea deals and release, so the allegations were never rebutted in court. Some facts (the 122 charges, appointed counsel's admissions) come from the record itself.
  4. Objection: Elaine was about labor and land, not voting. Mitigation: Fair. The link to voting is indirect. Nothing shows the union sought the vote. The connection is structural: disfranchisement removed Black people from office and, through elector-based jury rules, from juries, which shaped how the crisis was handled. We should not claim the massacre was caused by an election dispute.
  5. Objection: Arkansas law did not bar Black men from voting, and the poll tax applied to everyone. Mitigation: True on paper, and poor white voters were also affected (white turnout fell too). But the combined measures cut estimated Black turnout from 71 to 38 percent in two years and, with the all-white primary, removed Black influence over who held power. (Election Law of 1891; ANDA)
  6. Objection: The system eventually worked; the defendants went free. Mitigation: Partly. Federal courts corrected the trial, which shows the value of constitutional review. But it took more than five years, national fundraising, and years in prison, and most killings were never prosecuted. Redress was available only to those who could reach outside help.
  7. Objection: The governor and troops were sent to restore order. Mitigation: Sources differ on the troops' role: Col. Jenks recorded two Black deaths by troops, Capt. Passailaigue wrote "about twenty," and others blamed soldiers for most deaths. We note the dispute without assigning motive. (UALR exhibit)

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • Phillips County voter rolls or poll tax receipt counts by race for 1919 would show directly how many Black residents could vote or serve on juries. We did not find such figures.
  • A verified primary text of Walter White's December 1919 Nation article and Ida B. Wells-Barnett's 1920 pamphlet would let us quote their numbers firsthand rather than through secondary sources.
  • If credible evidence showed a planned uprising, it would change the framing of the first night, though not the due process findings about the trials.
  • The original case name and decision date of 141 Ark. 496 should be confirmed from a full reporter copy (the Caselaw Access Project page loaded but did not show the caption).
  • Archaeological or archival work (for example, burial records) could narrow the death-toll range.
