Clean Air Act (United States, 1955 to 1990, centered on 1970)

Evidence first. Conclusions revisable.

After smog disasters and two weak federal laws, Congress passed the 1970 Clean Air Act almost unanimously, setting national health-based air standards with public hearings and citizen suits; EPA studies find large health benefits, while other research finds local job losses and today's exposure remains uneven by race.

In brief

In 1948 the Pennsylvania town of Donora saw smog kill 20 people. Congress answered first with research money in 1955, a 1963 law and a 1967 law that left standards to states. In 1970 it passed a stronger Clean Air Act, 375 to 1 in the House and 73 to 0 in the Senate, with national health-based standards, public hearings and a clause letting citizens sue. EPA's studies say benefits far exceed costs, and critics have challenged parts of the method. One economist finds counties that missed the standards lost about 590,000 manufacturing jobs in 15 years, and a 2021 study finds people of color still breathe more fine particles from most sources. For citizens the case shows a real tool, the right to sue and a public record, and also shows that who paid and who still breathes the worst air were not the same people as those cheering the vote.

The 1970 Act

  • House bill H.R. 17255 was introduced by Rep. Paul Rogers (D-FL) on April 27, 1970 and passed June 10, 1970, 375 to 1. The Senate bill S. 4358 was introduced by Sen. Edmund Muskie (D-ME) on Sept. 17, 1970 and passed Sept. 22, 1970, 73 to 0. The House adopted the conference report Dec. 18 without a recorded vote and the Senate by unanimous consent. President Nixon signed it Dec. 31, 1970 (Public Law 91-604) (farmdoc daily). The Michigan exhibit names Rep. Leonard Farbstein as the lone "no" vote in the House.
  • EPA was created Dec. 2, 1970. The 1970 Act "shifted federal policy toward comprehensive regulation of stationary and mobile sources" and created National Ambient Air Quality Standards, State Implementation Plans, New Source Performance Standards and hazardous-pollutant standards (EPA, Evolution).
  • The Michigan exhibit says about two dozen auto-industry lobbyists met the Public Works Committee on Aug. 27, 1970 to argue the proposed deadlines were impossible; environmentalists asked for a rebuttal meeting and got it. It says the Nixon administration backed industry and government efforts to weaken the bill, and that Nixon signed it "with few changes." It says the Senate bill required public hearings when states set standards and included all but one of 18 points in Environmental Action's "Plan for Clean Air." This is one university exhibit and we did not trace each claim to hearings.
  • Citizen suits. Section 304 allowed citizens to sue to enforce the Act's requirements. On Sept. 21, 1970, Sen. Roman Hruska (R-NE) opposed it, arguing it would flood courts and divert agency resources. Muskie answered by inserting the committee report, stressing limits: citizens could enforce only the Act's requirements, had to give notice in most cases, and courts could award costs against frivolous suits. The report says Section 304 "does not authorize a 'class action'" (Congressional Record, Sept. 21, 1970, p. 32925, via Bates College).
  • Later amendments. 1977: prevention of significant deterioration and rules for areas not meeting standards. 1990: acid-rain control, operating permits, toxic pollutant program expanded to 189 pollutants, stratospheric ozone protection, stronger enforcement (EPA, Evolution).

Court ruling on costs

Whitman v. American Trucking Associations, 531 U.S. 457 (decided Feb. 27, 2001). Justice Scalia wrote that EPA "may not consider implementation costs in setting primary and secondary NAAQS under §109(b)." Standards must be those "requisite to protect the public health," read as "not lower or higher than is necessary." The Court said claims of economic or technological infeasibility are for the state agency writing the implementation plan. On this question all nine justices agreed on the result; Justice Breyer concurred in part separately, so the reasoning drew eight. (Cornell LII; law.resource.org copy.)

Benefits and costs: what EPA says and what others say

  • EPA says its 1970 to 1990 retrospective study (issued Oct. 15, 1997) found benefits "exceeded the costs of its programs by a large margin." The 1990 to 2020 study (issued Mar. 1, 2011) found the central benefit estimate exceeded costs by "a factor of more than 30 to one," a high estimate 90 times and a low estimate about three to one. The amendments "will prevent over 230,000 early deaths" in 2020 (EPA Second Prospective Study page). Policy Integrity reports about $65 billion in compliance costs and about $2 trillion in benefits for 2020; the EPA page we opened gave no dollar totals, so those two figures come from an advocacy group's summary.
  • National Geographic (Dec. 2020) says EPA-commissioned research found benefits "more than 40 times greater than its cost" and that removing lead from gasoline spared each child two to four IQ points. That "40 times" does not match EPA's "more than 30 to one" and may refer to a different study or year. We could not reconcile them; use EPA's own wording.
  • Critics: the Cato exchange (1996) shows economists Crandall, Rueter and Steger criticized EPA's draft study; EPA's co-directors Brenner and Morgenstern replied that errors in emissions data run in both directions and that the link between fine particles and early death rests on epidemiology while conceding "no scientific consensus on the biological mechanism." They admit lower life-value assumptions shrink benefits, though benefits still "substantially exceed" costs. We did not read the critique itself.

Costs and harms that fell on particular people

  • Greenstone (NBER 8484, 2001; published in the Journal of Political Economy 2002) studied about 1.75 million plant observations in the Censuses of Manufactures 1967 to 1987. Counties out of compliance (called "nonattainment") lost roughly 590,000 jobs in pollution-intensive industries relative to compliant counties in 1972 to 1987, about $37 billion in capital stock and about $75 billion in output (1987 dollars). He calls the declines "modest compared to the size of the entire manufacturing sector." The work counts local job losses; it does not net out jobs moved elsewhere, and we did not read the full paper.
  • Exposure remains uneven. Tessum, Marshall and coauthors (Science Advances, April 28, 2021), using EPA's 2014 National Emissions Inventory, found most emission sectors lead to above-average fine-particle (PM2.5) exposure for people of color, and Black people faced above-average concentrations from all sectors. White people were below average except for agriculture and coal. National Geographic says more than 60,000 Americans still die early from air pollution each year, disproportionately poor, Black and Latino people. We did not open the paper itself and did not find who counted the 60,000.

What this case shows

  1. The first two laws left the choice to states and showed the limits of that. The 1963 law funded research; the 1967 law asked states to set their own standards. EPA says the 1970 Act "shifted" to national standards and sources. The sequence shows a legislature trying local control first and revising when it did not work to the extent the 1970 sponsors wanted. We did not read the legislative findings that say so.
  2. Almost every vote was yes, so the public was not choosing between two sides. A 375 to 1 House vote and a 73 to 0 Senate vote left voters with little to sort on a recorded vote. The real contest was over details, such as deadlines and cost, where lobbyists and environmental groups met committee members in August 1970 (Michigan exhibit). A voter who wanted to know who pushed which deadline had to follow committee meetings that are not in roll calls.
  3. The Act gave ordinary people a tool to enforce it. Section 304 let citizens sue to enforce the Act's own requirements, with limits Muskie described. Senator Hruska's objection that it would flood courts is the other side. This is the clearest "fix" power in the case: a person did not have to wait for an agency.
  4. Standards are public and measured, so people can check. EPA publishes the standards and, through its studies, its estimate of what the rules cost and prevent. The Tessum findings use EPA's own emissions inventory. Public data let outside researchers show that some groups breathe worse air. That is institutions disclosing the record enough for critics to use it.
  5. Costs and benefits were not spread evenly. Greenstone finds local job and capital losses in counties that missed the standards; Tessum finds higher exposure for people of color from most sectors. Those who bore the costs of cleanup in jobs and those who still bear exposure are not always the same as those who gained, and neither group is fully identified in the data we read.
  6. The Supreme Court locked cost out of the standard-setting step. Whitman means the standard is set on health, and cost is argued later, in state plans. That tells voters where to look: costs and feasibility fights happen in state implementation, where most people never attend.
  7. The studies that judge the Act were done by the agency that enforces it. EPA's cost-benefit studies carry ratios of 30 to 1 or more, and outside economists disputed parts of the method in 1996. A public that wants to judge the Act depends on a self-evaluation that has been challenged and defended in public.

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: The near-unanimous vote shows the public strongly wanted the Act. Mitigation: votes show Congress's choice, not public opinion. We did not find a 1970 poll and do not claim one. The unanimity also hides the amendment fights.
  2. Objection: EPA's own benefit numbers cannot be trusted. Mitigation: EPA's central ratio is "more than 30 to one," its own low estimate is about three to one, and outside critics in 1996 challenged emissions data and the particle-mortality link. EPA officials answered. We show both and do not pick. Policy Integrity's summary figures are from an advocacy group and we label them.
  3. Objection: Greenstone proves the Act destroyed jobs. Mitigation: he says the effect is local and "modest" compared to the whole manufacturing sector, and the study counts losses in non-complying counties relative to complying ones. It does not account for jobs created in cleaner equipment or health gains. Do not state it as net job loss.
  4. Objection: The racial exposure gap is a failure of the Act. Mitigation: Tessum shows a gap in 2014 emissions, which is not the same as showing the Act caused or failed to cure it. We did not find a study that compares exposure gaps before and after 1970 in the sources we opened. We say only that the gap exists today.
  5. Objection: Citizen suits led to abuse. Mitigation: Hruska argued that in 1970; Muskie pointed to notice requirements and cost awards. We did not find data on suit volume or outcomes and make no claim.
  6. Objection: Nixon signed it, so he and the administration were for it (or against it). Mitigation: The Michigan exhibit says the administration backed efforts to weaken the bill but signed with few changes. We do not score any president or party; both statements are in the record and we present them as one exhibit's account.
  7. Objection: The Act was not about voting or hidden records. Mitigation: Agreed that the link is indirect. The tie is who could know about air quality, who could press for enforcement, and who paid. We do not claim hidden-record misconduct here.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • Statute text. We did not read 42 U.S.C. 7401 or the 1970 Act's findings. A reader should check the stated purposes and the exact citizen-suit and public-hearing wording (Sec. 304, Sec. 110).
  • Vote counts. Taken from a single farm-economics site; confirm in the House Journal, Senate Journal or Congressional Record.
  • Donora and New York death counts. Only from the American Lung Association timeline. Check the Pennsylvania Department of Health 1949 report or a peer-reviewed review.
  • EPA dollar totals and the "40 times" figure. Get the Second Prospective Study full report and the 1997 retrospective; confirm cost and benefit totals and resolve the 30 to 1 versus 40 to 1 difference.
  • Critique content. We did not read the Crandall, Rueter and Steger critique or any recent critique of PM2.5 valuation (the Regulatory Review fetch failed).
  • Greenstone, net effects. Whether displaced jobs reappeared elsewhere. Read the full paper and responses.
  • Tessum and the 60,000 figure. Open the paper; find who produced the National Geographic number.
  • Public participation in practice. How many state hearings under the 1970 Act drew the public, and how many citizen suits were filed and won. Not found.
  • Lead and acid rain. Not researched here; the lead and acid-rain claims in popular coverage are unverified on this page.
  • Massachusetts v. EPA (2007) and later climate cases are not covered.
  • If a primary source showed the 1970 hearings were poorly attended, or if exposure gaps narrowed after 1970, arguments 2 and 5 would change.
