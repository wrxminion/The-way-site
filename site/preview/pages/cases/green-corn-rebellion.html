Green Corn Rebellion (Seminole, Pontotoc and Hughes Counties, Oklahoma, August 1917)

Evidence first. Conclusions revisable.

In early August 1917, a few hundred tenant farmers in rural Oklahoma, many tied to the Working Class Union, rose against the military draft; the uprising ended within days, and the counts of how many were arrested, tried, convicted and sentenced differ by source, from 86 to 175.

In brief

In August 1917, a few hundred tenant farmers, many linked to a group called the Working Class Union, gathered in southeastern Oklahoma to resist the new military draft. They cut telegraph lines and burned railroad bridges, and three rebels died; the rising was over within days. Authorities arrested about 458 men, and 266 were later released for lack of evidence. Federal prosecutors charged the rest, mostly with conspiracy to obstruct the draft, and the sentences ran from 60 days to ten years. How many were convicted is still disputed: sources say 86, about 150 or 175. The Oklahoma Historical Society, citing a scholar's work, says 150. What is clear is that the people who rose were men who had no vote on the draft and little land, and that the public record of what the courts did to them is harder to read than it should be.

The record

Background: land, debt and the draft

  • By 1917 "over half" of Oklahoma's farmers were tenants, after speculation and falling crop prices. Many saw the war as "a rich man's war, poor man's fight." (Oklahoma Historical Society, Encyclopedia of Oklahoma History and Culture, entry by Nigel Sellars; opened)
  • The Working Class Union (WCU) was based in Van Buren, Arkansas. It claimed 35,000 members in Oklahoma, which the OHS entry calls "questionable." One other account puts it at 18,000 to 35,000. Membership rose when cotton prices collapsed in 1914 to 1915, fell when prices rose in 1916, and revived after the United States entered the war. (OHS entry; Chris Mahin, "Oklahoma's dispossessed rebel against poverty and a 'rich man's war,'" via libcom.org; opened)
  • The Selective Service Act became law on May 18, 1917, and the Espionage Act followed on June 15, 1917. (Sellars, 2002 law review article, hosted by the National Agricultural Law Center; opened, partly; the fetched text was truncated)
  • The IWW and the Socialist Party had no official role in the rising, though authorities used it to pursue both. The Oklahoma Socialist Party disbanded afterward. (OHS entry)

Who was involved and what they faced

  • Deaths: three men died in the fighting (OHS; Wikipedia; Smithsonian; Clio). One of the three was Clifford Clark, a Black tenant farmer (Wikipedia). Smithsonian adds an innocent schoolteacher shot at a roadblock. Sellars's 2002 article says "four men were dead" in the introduction but "Three rebels died" later, plus one non-rebel schoolteacher. The four-versus-three gap is probably the schoolteacher; this is our inference, not stated.
  • Charges: the rebels were first considered for treason but were charged with conspiracy to obstruct the draft. (Sellars 2002)
  • Federal prosecutors named in Sellars: U.S. Attorneys John Fain and W. P. McGinnis and assistants Redmond Cole and W. J. Turnbull. Judge John Hazelton Cotteral heard the Enid trial. Defense lawyers named: Patrick Nagle, John Carney, Ben Williams and Roscoe Arrington. Over eighty witnesses testified at Enid.
  • Bail: $10,000 for major leaders, $5,000 to $7,500 for lesser leaders, $500 to $5,000 for others. By one group count, only three of about 50 prisoners made bail. (Sellars 2002)
  • Confessions: 32 full confessions were obtained by August 10. A government witness, J. C. Harrod, attempted suicide to avoid testifying, and another witness, Scott Mitchell, was arrested for perjury. (Sellars 2002)
  • Leniency: McGinnis concluded most rebels had been duped and recommended parole for most. One defendant, Spearman Dalby, was released after offering to serve in the army. (Sellars 2002)
  • Jones Family trial at Enid: the jury convicted 8 of 11 men on conspiracy and acquitted three (Daniel Isenhour, Frank Banning, J. L. Bohannon). Judge Cotteral sentenced the eight to six years in Leavenworth and fined each $5,000; an appeals court later upheld the verdict. (Sellars 2002) This is one trial, not the whole rebellion.

What reconciles and what does not:

  • Arrests agree within a few: 450 to 458, plus a "more than 400" round figure. Sellars is the only source we opened that gives an exact 458 with the 266 releases. 458 minus 266 is 192, which matches his "192 to face trial." Wikipedia and Mahin say 184 charged. The 8-person gap is unresolved.
  • The 150 figure has the widest support (OHS, Wikipedia, Clio, Mahin), but the three that carry it appear to descend from Sellars. Sellars's own "about a third" matches roughly 150 of 450 to 458.
  • The 86 figure comes from one Smithsonian piece (repeated by a local site). It says guilty pleas, not total convictions. A plausible, but unverified, reading is that 86 counts pleas only and 150 counts all convictions. We could not confirm this.
  • The 175 figure is from a 1922 partisan article that also says "nearly 300" were involved. We treat it as a contemporaneous claim, not a count.
  • No primary record of the total (the federal court docket, grand jury returns, or Leavenworth and Bureau of Prisons intake files at the National Archives) was opened. The count cannot be settled from what we have. We state a range: about 86 to 175 sentenced, with 150 the most repeated.

What this case shows

  1. Rural people with little land had no ordinary channel left. Over half of Oklahoma's farmers were tenants, the war was called "a rich man's war," and the draft covered men with no say in the decision. OHS notes that organizing (WCU) grew with falling crop prices. Our question is what people could vote on and fix. Here the dispute was about a national decision already made. (OHS)
  2. The state answered with mass detention before it sorted guilt. Of 458 arrested, 266 were released for insufficient evidence at preliminary hearings. At the end of August 257 were still held at McAlester. Most could not make bail. Detention came first and release came later, after bonds were cut and landowners posted bail for some tenants. (Sellars 2002)
  3. Who had a lawyer and a hearing is visible in the record. The Jones Family defendants had named defense counsel and a long trial; three were acquitted on health, youth and limited involvement. Yet the Espionage Act context, 32 early confessions and a witness arrested for perjury show how thin the process could be for others. (Sellars 2002)
  4. The public record of the total outcome is itself unclear. Nobody can say from the sources we opened how many were convicted: 86, 150 or 175. A reader can look up a death toll for a labor battle; here the conviction count is contested. Our question is what ordinary people could know. A clear docket would let them know.
  5. Leniency came from officials, not from a right. McGinnis recommended parole for most rebels on his own view that they were "duped." Release depended on prosecutors' judgment. (Sellars 2002)

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: This was armed insurrection, not a civic dispute. Men burned bridges, cut wires and ambushed a sheriff's party; three rebels died. Mitigation: Agreed. The case does not defend the violence. It asks how the state handled hundreds of arrests and what the public can now check. The record shows crime and weak process together.
  2. Objection: The courts were doing wartime enforcement under valid laws. The Selective Service Act and the Espionage Act were lawful, and an appeals court upheld the Jones Family verdict. Mitigation: True. We report that the law was followed on its terms. We do not claim the trials were unlawful; we note what is missing from the public record.
  3. Objection: The conviction numbers are just sloppy secondary writing. Mitigation: That is the point of the table. Tertiary and popular pieces repeat 150 or 86; none we opened points to a docket. The most careful source (Sellars) was only partly readable. We state a range and say which figures rest on which sources.
  4. Objection: WCU numbers and motives are inflated by sympathetic writers. Mitigation: OHS itself calls the 35,000-member claim "questionable." We give the claim and its doubt. We rely on OHS and Sellars, not on partisan accounts, for the core facts.
  5. Objection: Pardons show the system corrected itself. Mitigation: Partly. Most rebels were soon paroled or pardoned (Wikipedia, no primary), some leaders were pardoned in 1921 (Mahin), and five were still in Leavenworth in February 1922. Release came by executive grace, years after the sentences.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • The federal court docket and grand jury returns (U.S. District Courts for the Eastern and Western Districts of Oklahoma, held at the National Archives) would settle how many were indicted, pleaded guilty and were convicted. Not opened.
  • Leavenworth intake records would settle how many served federal time (Smithsonian captions say 28; a local site says two). Not opened.
  • We could not read the final pages of Sellars's 2002 article. His totals for Ardmore trials, Munson and Spence sentences, and the number of guilty pleas are unverified.
  • The 1921 pardon claim and the "most were soon paroled or pardoned" claim rest on secondary writing only. Unverified.
  • The death count: three rebels (OHS) versus Sellars's one "four men were dead." Unverified; names of the three are not all confirmed (only Clifford Clark is named, in Wikipedia).
  • Whether 86 means guilty pleas only. Unverified.
  • The WCU membership figure (18,000 to 35,000) is not independently documented.
  • Burbank, When Farmers Voted Red (1976), and Sellars, Oil, Wheat, and Wobblies (1998) were not opened; they would be the best secondary tests of the counts.
