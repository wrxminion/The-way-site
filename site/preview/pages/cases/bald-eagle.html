Bald eagle recovery and wildlife protections (lower 48 states, 1917 to 2021)

Evidence first. Conclusions revisable.

Sources checked 2026-10-19 · Preliminary research, not a final verdict

The bald eagle fell to a few hundred nesting pairs by the 1960s, and after a 1940 protection law, a 1972 DDT ban, endangered listing and local work it was delisted in 2007 with 9,789 pairs; the recovery had several causes, carried costs for some groups, and left threats such as lead poisoning.

In brief

By the 1960s, the bald eagle in the lower 48 states had fallen to a few hundred nesting pairs, with one federal count of 417 in 1963. Alaska had paid a bounty on eagles from 1917 to 1953, and over 100,000 birds were killed. Congress protected the birds in 1940, and the government listed the species as endangered in 1967. After months of public hearings, EPA Administrator William Ruckelshaus ordered DDT cancelled in June 1972. Cotton growers faced higher costs, and critics later argued the campaign harmed malaria control. The Fish and Wildlife Service reclassified the eagle in 1995 and delisted it in 2007, with about 9,789 pairs. It credited the recovery "in part" to reduced pesticides and also to habitat protection. A 2021 federal release counts over 71,400 pairs. Lead poisoning still affects many eagles. The record shows several decisions across decades, argued in public, with no single cause.

The record

Decline and early protection

  • Wikipedia reports the pre-1700s population at 300,000 to 500,000 and says the Bald and Golden Eagle Protection Act of 1940 banned commercial trapping and killing of the birds and collecting eggs. (Wikipedia, Bald eagle; opened. The 1940 statute text was not opened.)
  • The Alaska Territorial Legislature created an eagle bounty in 1917 after fox farmers and fishers said eagles preyed on their animals and catch. The Alaska Department of Fish and Game says "over 100,000 eagles were killed before the bounty was removed in 1953," and that later investigation found the claims mostly unfounded. Federal protection applied in Alaska after statehood in 1959. (Alaska Department of Fish and Game, eagle fact sheet; opened)
  • In 1967 the Secretary of the Interior listed bald eagles south of 40 degrees north as endangered, citing a decline "caused by DDT and other factors." In 1978 the Fish and Wildlife Service listed the species as endangered in 43 contiguous states and threatened in Michigan, Minnesota, Wisconsin, Oregon and Washington. A rule published July 12, 1995, reclassified it from endangered to threatened in the 43 states. (Federal Register, July 9, 2007, 72 FR 37346; opened, first 100,000 of about 129,500 characters)

The DDT decision

  • EPA Administrator William D. Ruckelshaus issued the DDT cancellation order on June 14, 1972, and general use became illegal December 31, 1972. He concluded continued heavy use posed "unacceptable risks to the environment." Exceptions covered public health uses, quarantine uses, a few minor crops and exports. (EPA archive, DDT ban takes effect; opened)
  • EPA hearings ran from August 1971 to March 1972 and took testimony from public health officials, manufacturers, farmers, the Department of Agriculture and the Environmental Defense Fund. (EBSCO Research Starters, U.S. Government Bans DDT; opened. A tertiary source.)
  • EPA's 1975 impact statement, as summarized there, found little effect on agriculture or public health. The ban raised cotton farming costs by at least six dollars an acre. Critics, including Bate and Tren and Beatty, argue the anti-DDT campaign hampered malaria control and that benefits of DDT outweighed risks. (EBSCO; the books and the 1975 statement were not opened)

Delisting

  • On July 9, 2007 (effective August 8), the Fish and Wildlife Service removed the bald eagle in the lower 48 states from the list. It cited about 9,789 breeding pairs. It credited the recovery "in part" to reduced levels of persistent organochlorine pesticides such as DDT, plus habitat protection and management. The Service received 387 new comments, and two of three peer reviewers responded, one supporting delisting and one not. (72 FR 37346)
  • After delisting, the Bald and Golden Eagle Protection Act and the Migratory Bird Treaty Act still apply. A June 5, 2007, rule defined "disturb," which the Service says protects nests, important foraging areas and communal roosts. A 20 year monitoring plan was proposed. Commenters raised mercury, PCBs and heavy metals, which the Service called mostly local threats. (72 FR 37346)
  • Only members of federally recognized tribes can get permits for whole eagles, parts or feathers for religious use. The National Eagle Repository in Colorado distributes dead birds. (72 FR 37346)

Numbers after delisting

  • A March 24, 2021, Fish and Wildlife Service release, based on 2018 and 2019 aerial surveys and eBird data, reports over 71,400 nesting pairs and about 316,700 individual eagles in the lower 48 states. It says the population roughly quadrupled since 2009 and cites 417 known nesting pairs in 1963. (Department of the Interior, March 24, 2021; opened. The FWS final report was not opened.)

Ongoing threats

  • A study in Science (Slabe and coauthors, February 17, 2022) sampled eagles in 38 states. A news report says harmful lead levels were found in the bones of 46 percent of bald eagles, and the American Eagle Foundation, citing the study, says 47 percent showed chronic lead exposure and up to 33 to 35 percent acute poisoning. The news report says lead suppresses population growth. (WKBW/Scripps news report; American Eagle Foundation, top threats; both opened. The Science paper was not opened.)

What this case shows

  1. Public decisions changed the odds, in more than one place. A 1940 federal law, a 1967 listing, a 1972 EPA order after public hearings, and a 1995 and 2007 change of status each appear in the record. No single vote explains the recovery, and the Fish and Wildlife Service itself says "in part." (72 FR 37346)
  2. The DDT decision was made in open hearings. EPA held hearings for months in 1971 and 1972 with farmers, manufacturers, health officials and a citizen group. People outside government shaped a record a court could review. (EBSCO; EPA archive)
  3. Some groups paid, and the record shows it. Cotton farmers faced at least six dollars an acre more in costs, Alaska fishers and fox farmers had used the bounty, and critics say the DDT ban harmed malaria control. Each claim can be checked and argued. (EBSCO; Alaska Fish and Game)
  4. Who was included in the final rule is concrete. Federally recognized tribes get a permit path for eagle feathers for religious use, while others do not. The Federal Register records 387 comments on the delisting. (72 FR 37346)
  5. Success did not end the problem. Lead from carcasses still sickens a large share of eagles. The 2007 rule promised a 20 year monitoring plan. A voter can ask whether it was completed. (Slabe 2022 via news; 72 FR 37346)

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: The DDT ban was the cause and everything else is noise. Mitigation: Wikipedia calls the ban "most significant," and the Service credits it "in part." We present both and make no single-law claim. Habitat protection, the 1940 law, and local work are also in the record.
  2. Objection: Delisting was premature or driven by politics. Mitigation: The Service received 387 new comments, one of two responding peer reviewers did not support delisting, and the rule kept two statutes in force and set a 20 year monitoring plan. We did not read the full comment responses.
  3. Objection: The DDT ban killed people through malaria. Mitigation: EBSCO summarizes critics (Bate and Tren, Beatty) and EPA's 1975 finding of little health effect on the United States. The ban's exceptions allowed public health uses and exports. We do not adjudicate the global malaria claim, which is beyond this case.
  4. Objection: The population numbers are unreliable. Mitigation: The 1963 count differs by source (417 versus 487), and 2020 totals come from surveys and a model. We show both and use 9,789 pairs as the official 2007 figure.
  5. Objection: Government credit-taking ignores private and local action. Mitigation: The record names state agencies, the Audubon estimate, and Cornell eBird data. We did not research hatchery or reintroduction programs, so we make no claim about them. See Section 5.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • The 1963 count: 417 or 487. The underlying Audubon survey was not opened.
  • The 1940 statute text, the 1973 Endangered Species Act and the 1962 golden eagle amendment were not opened. Our statements about them rest on Wikipedia and the Federal Register summary.
  • The last 29,500 characters of the 2007 Federal Register rule (including any discussion of lead, wind power and take permits) were not read.
  • The Science lead paper and the Fish and Wildlife Service 2020 population report were not opened; figures come from an Interior release, a news report and an advocacy group.
  • The role of state agencies, hacking and reintroduction programs, and private landowners was not researched. A single-law claim would need evidence separating them from DDT effects.
  • Whether the 20 year post-delisting monitoring plan was finalized and completed: not checked.
  • EPA's 1975 DDT impact statement and the 1972 cancellation decision were not opened; EBSCO is a tertiary summary.
  • Any record of eagle deaths from wind turbines and related take permit rules (post-2007) was not verified. The turbine figure on the American Eagle Foundation page covers all birds, not eagles.
  • The Wikipedia figure for 1980s population (about 100,000) appears inconsistent and was not used.
  • Evidence that delisting increased deaths, or that lead exposure is rising, would change our reading of the outcome.
