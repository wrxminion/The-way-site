Urban renewal and highway clearance (United States, 1949 to 1974 and after)

Evidence first. Conclusions revisable.

Federal housing and highway laws paid cities to clear neighborhoods called slums or blighted, courts upheld the takings, and estimates of the people displaced range from about 300,000 families to over a million people, with Black residents bearing a large share.

In brief

In 1949 Congress set a goal of "a decent home and a suitable living environment" for every American family and paid cities to clear what it called slum and blighted land. In 1954 the Supreme Court upheld a Washington, D.C. clearance plan, saying a community may be "beautiful as well as healthy." In 1956 Congress funded 41,000 miles of Interstate highways with a 90 percent federal share. Estimates of the people displaced differ. One summary of HUD data counts roughly 1.35 million people before 1972; another says at least 300,000 families, perhaps 1.2 million people; a transportation estimate cited by an advocacy group says 475,000 households and over a million people for the Interstates. Sources say Black residents were a larger share of those displaced than of the population, and that relocation took well under one percent of project costs. The record is clear on the laws and rulings. It is less clear on who agreed to what.

The record

The laws

  • Housing Act of 1949. Congress declared a goal of "a decent home and a suitable living environment" for every American family, and set five approaches: private industry supplies as much housing as it can; government help lets industry meet more of the need; local public bodies are encouraged to plan and build; clearance aid goes to localities that document unmet needs; and farm housing aid goes to owners who cannot get credit. (42 U.S.C. 1441, Cornell LII; opened, summarized by the reader tool, not quoted in full)
  • Per a Wikipedia summary, the Act's Title I authorized $1 billion in loans for cities to acquire slum and blighted land and $100 million a year for five years in grants covering two-thirds of the gap between land cost and reuse value, and its public housing title required a slum unit to be demolished or renovated for each public housing unit built. The Senate passed it 57 to 13 on April 21, 1949; the House 227 to 186 on June 29; Truman signed it July 15, 1949. Not checked against the statute. (Wikipedia, Housing Act of 1949; opened)
  • Housing Act of 1954, signed August 2, 1954 (Pub. L. 83-560), per Wikipedia, "created the modern urban renewal apparatus" and favored private-sector-led redevelopment. It adds that slum clearance under the 1949 Act had proven costly and politically unpopular by 1953. Not checked against the statute. (Wikipedia, Housing Act of 1954; opened)
  • Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956, signed June 29, 1956: added 1,000 miles to bring the Interstate System to 41,000 miles; authorized $25 billion for fiscal years 1957 through 1969; set a 90 percent federal share. The FHWA history says urban Interstates took about half of Interstate funds, far more than the early $27 billion estimate anticipated, and that the Act required public hearings when a project bypassed or went through a city, town or village. The history does not say the Act addressed displacement directly. (FHWA Highway History, "The Greatest Decade"; opened)

The courts

  • Berman v. Parker, 348 U.S. 26 (decided November 22, 1954, argued October 19): Justice Douglas wrote for the Court upholding the District of Columbia Redevelopment Act of 1945 as applied to taking commercial property for a comprehensive plan, with later transfer to private parties. The Court wrote that "it is within the power of the legislature to determine that the community should be beautiful as well as healthy," and that public values are "spiritual as well as physical, aesthetic as well as monetary." The record described Southwest Washington Project Area B: 5,012 people; 64.3 percent of dwellings beyond repair and 18.4 percent needing major repairs; 57.8 percent with outside toilets; 60.3 percent with no baths; 29.3 percent without electricity; 83.8 percent without central heating. (Justia; opened; the page did not state the vote)
  • Kelo v. City of New London (decided June 23, 2005): 5 to 4, Stevens for the Court. Economic development is a permissible public use under the Fifth Amendment. O'Connor, Rehnquist, Scalia and Thomas dissented; O'Connor wrote "Today the Court abandons this long-held, basic limitation on government power." The plan for Fort Trumbull was projected to create over 1,000 jobs near a planned $300 million Pfizer facility. According to Justia's commentary, the development corporation never obtained funding, the property became an empty lot and Pfizer changed plans. That outcome is the commentary's, not the opinion's. (Justia; opened; last 7,700 characters not read)

How many people were displaced? The sources disagree.

Two points on the table. First, the national urban renewal figures (1.35 million people; 300,000 families or perhaps 1.2 million people) are close in the upper range but count different things and do not come with a shared method. Second, the highway estimate overlaps in time and in places with urban renewal, so the two cannot be added without double counting. We have not found a primary count that separates them.

Who was displaced

Source Finding
Rockefeller Institute "more than half of the families displaced by urban renewal projects were 'nonwhite'" (HUD and Census data, per the authors). In New York 1950 to 1966, nonwhite families were 41 percent of 39,647 families displaced or threatened with displacement
Cebul Black Americans were "just 13 percent of the total population in 1960" but "at least 55 percent" of those displaced; examples in small cities such as Greenville, North Carolina (207 families of color and 11 white families)
Gans "Nearly two-thirds" of cleared slum units were occupied by Black residents

The three measure different groups ("nonwhite," Black) and different places. We do not choose among them.

Compensation and relocation

Source Figure
Rockefeller Institute Relocation was 0.4 percent of gross project costs, 1950 to 1966; more than $13 billion in federal funds over 25 years (both from a HathiTrust document cited by the authors, not opened)
Gans About 0.5 percent of federal urban renewal spending from 1949 to 1964 went to relocating families (about 2 percent including payments)
Gans, citing studies A 1961 study of 41 cities found 60 percent of displaced tenants moved into other slums; Philadelphia 1958, over 70 percent; West End of Boston, median rent rose from $41 to $71 a month; Chicago nonwhite families earning under $3,000 saw rent rise from 35 to 46 percent of income
Rockefeller Institute Economist Anthony Downs estimated in 1968 Senate testimony that less than half of contract buyers had been reimbursed for seized property

The 0.4 and 0.5 percent figures cover different years (1950 to 1966 and 1949 to 1964) and are close but not the same measure.

Local examples (from sources we opened)

  • Rondo, St. Paul: Wikipedia says the core of Old Rondo "was demolished between 1956 and 1968" for Interstate 94 and "at least 650 families were displaced." In 2015 Mayor Chris Coleman said "Today we acknowledge the sins of our past," and in 2016 MnDOT Commissioner Charlie Zelle and Coleman formally apologized, per the page. We did not open a MnDOT or city record. (Wikipedia, Rondo neighborhood; opened)
  • Overtown, Miami: Wikipedia, citing Hirsch and Mohl (1993), says the population was "decimated by nearly 80 percent." The page attributes the decline to highway construction and urban renewal. It gives no count of people displaced. We did not open the cited book. (Wikipedia, Overtown; opened)
  • Greenwood, Tulsa: no source on highway clearance was opened for this case. See Case 15 for the 1921 destruction. Not claimed here.

Benefits claimed

  • Conditions in cleared areas were severe in at least one adjudicated example (Berman: 64.3 percent of dwellings beyond repair, 60.3 percent with no baths).
  • Anderson, as described by a Dartmouth Alumni Magazine reviewer, called the program "very costly, destructive of personal liberty, and not capable of achieving the goals put forth by Congress." Gans, summarizing Anderson's data, reports the private market cut substandard housing from 37 to 19 percent between 1950 and 1960. The reviewer disputed the conclusion that the program was a disaster and doubted that free enterprise alone could end blight. Census statistics cited there show 10.9 million substandard units remained in 1960. (Dartmouth Alumni Magazine review, 1965; opened)
  • Gans acknowledged later reforms (fewer clearance projects, rehabilitation loans, 221(d)(3) housing, 1964 relocation aid) but called them small. (Gans, "The Failure of Urban Renewal," Commentary; opened)
  • The FHWA history frames the Interstate System as a national network with a 90 percent federal share. We did not open travel time or mobility evidence. The claimed benefit of faster movement is therefore unverified on this page.

What this case shows

  1. The goals were public and the law gave a pathway for local review, but the people most affected had the least power in it. The 1949 Act spoke of a "decent home." The 1956 Act required public hearings for routes through cities. Neither source we read shows who in a targeted neighborhood attended or what they could change. Estimates say Black residents were a much larger share of the displaced than of the population. (42 U.S.C. 1441; FHWA; Cebul)
  2. The courts treated the takings as legal, and by the same reasoning private transfer was allowed. Berman said "beautiful as well as healthy" is a legitimate public aim, and Kelo extended that to economic development. Ordinary owners could vote in city elections but had little to appeal on once a plan was adopted. (Berman; Kelo)
  3. Relocation help was a tiny share of cost. Two sources give 0.4 and 0.5 percent. The families that bore the cost got little of the money. (Rockefeller Institute; Gans)
  4. Replacement housing lagged demolition. Gans reports 126,000 units demolished and about 28,000 built by March 1961, with new-unit median rent of $158 a month in 1960. (Gans)
  5. Promised benefits sometimes did not arrive. In Kelo's case the planned development was not built, according to a commentary on the opinion. This is a single example and it is an outcome, not proof of intent.

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: the slums were dangerous, and clearance was a response to real conditions. Berman's record shows severe deficiencies. Mitigation: we report them. Our question question is not whether conditions were bad but who decided, who could object and who paid.
  2. Objection: urban renewal included later reforms and some projects worked. Gans acknowledged reforms; Anderson's critics said private enterprise could not do it alone. Mitigation: we report both sides and do not count projects as successes or failures. We have no project-level evaluation data.
  3. Objection: displacement figures are inflated and mix different programs. Mitigation: agreed that they are uncertain, which is why we show a table with each figure and its source. We do not sum highway and renewal numbers.
  4. Objection: highways brought mobility and economic gain to many people. Mitigation: we did not open evidence on that benefit. The record we read shows costs borne by particular neighborhoods and a federal share of 90 percent of construction. Whether the gains outweighed the costs is outside what we verified.
  5. Objection: the cases of Rondo and Overtown are told from Wikipedia. Mitigation: yes. We flag them as secondary and make no claim about route choice or motive. The Wikipedia page also reports a 2016 remark by the Transportation Secretary about "racially motivated routing." We did not verify it and do not rely on it.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • The statutes (1949, 1954, 1956) were not opened; Housing Act details come from Wikipedia and the Cornell statement of policy.
  • The HUD 1972 Statistical Yearbook, the 1964 House report, the USDOT highway displacement estimate and the Gans and Anderson sources behind unit counts were not opened.
  • No primary record for Rondo (MnDOT, city) or Overtown was opened. The displaced counts for both are secondary.
  • Greenwood (Tulsa) was not researched for highway clearance.
  • Evidence on mobility and economic benefits of the Interstates, and on benefits of slum clearance for those who stayed, was not found.
  • The vote in Berman v. Parker was not seen on the Justia page.
  • The 1970 Uniform Relocation Act and its effect on later displacement were not opened.
  • A project-by-project evaluation of outcomes for displaced households would change Section 3.
