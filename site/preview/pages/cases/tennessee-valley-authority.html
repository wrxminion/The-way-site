Tennessee Valley Authority (Tennessee Valley, 1933 to 2023)

Evidence first. Conclusions revisable.

Congress created a federal power and flood-control agency in 1933 that brought electricity to a poor region, but built dams on land where thousands lived, hired and housed Black workers unequally, took on debt, and in 2008 caused a major coal ash spill.

In brief

Congress created the Tennessee Valley Authority on May 18, 1933, to control floods, improve navigation and provide power. Private companies had electrified only 3 percent of the valley's farms by 1933, and by 1946 electricity reached 668,752 households. Building Norris Dam, which began in October 1933, meant removing families and moving about 5,200 graves; sources give figures from about 3,000 families to 2,841 families, and the agency's reservoirs displaced an estimated 72,000 people by 1946. A 1934 article in Opportunity reported that Black residents were passed over for jobs, kept off the dam work and excluded from the planned town of Norris. In 2008 a dike failure at the Kingston plant released about 5.4 million cubic yards of coal ash, and cleanup workers later sued the contractor Jacobs Engineering, which settled in 2023. TVA also carries a $30 billion statutory debt limit. The record shows broad benefits, real removals and exclusions, and a public agency whose costs were set by decisions outside the valley's voters' reach.

The record

The agency

  • The Tennessee Valley Authority Act was enacted May 18, 1933 (ch. 32, 48 Stat. 58). It created "a body corporate by the name of the 'Tennessee Valley Authority'" to run federal properties near Muscle Shoals, Alabama, "in the interest of the National defense and for agricultural and industrial development," to "improve navigation in the Tennessee River," and "to control the destructive flood waters in the Tennessee River and Mississippi River Basins." (16 U.S.C. 831, Cornell LII; opened, a copy of the statute)
  • Tennessee Encyclopedia: Roosevelt signed on May 18, 1933; Norris Dam was the first project (1933 to 1936); by the end of World War II seven dams stood on the main river, nine on tributaries, and five more were acquired. (Tennessee Encyclopedia, TVA; opened)
  • Hawks Nest (the 1930s West Virginia tunnel disaster, Case 20) was a private-contractor project and is not a TVA project. No source I opened links TVA to it. It is mentioned here only so readers do not confuse the two.

Electrification and income

  • Private power companies had electrified only 3 percent of the valley's farms by 1933. In Union County in 1933, 1 percent of farm owners had indoor plumbing, 4 percent had telephones and 8 percent had radios. Electricity reached 668,752 households by 1946. Per capita income rose from 44 percent of the national average in 1933 to 61 percent by 1953. TVA spent about $2.1 billion in the valley from 1933 to 1958. (Tennessee Encyclopedia)
  • History.com: by 1935 power cost in the valley had dropped to 30 percent below the national average. (History.com, Sarah Pruitt; opened. A general-audience source.)

Who was included and who was left out

  • Contemporary account: Cranston Clayton, a white Southerner, wrote in Opportunity, Journal of Negro Life (April 1934) that on the first civil service exams for twelve counties around Norris only 1.9 percent of qualifiers were Black though Black residents were 7.1 percent of the population; that a committee found two Black TVA employees in the area around December 1933; that TVA said it would not use Black labor on Norris Dam itself, citing the cost of separate accommodations; and that Black residents were excluded from the planned town (in the summary I read, he wrote that the government "absolutely excludes them"). TVA officials told him they hired Black workers in proportion to the local population at the same pay. (Opportunity, via VCU Social Welfare History Project; opened. This is a 1934 published article, close to primary, but one writer's account.)
  • Loyola University Chicago's Norris essay, drawing on Walker: TVA "maintained a policy of institutionalized segregation and discrimination against blacks" (Walker, p. 418); the all-white Family Removal staff generally did not give Black families the services white families got; about 5 percent of the population in the Norris reservoir area was African American. In one oral history a Black woman's small farm was appraised at $60 to $70 an acre. The page does not describe exclusion from the town of Norris. (Loyola, Class, Race and Displacement; opened)
  • Wikipedia's Norris, Tennessee article says TVA excluded Black families in the 1930s, "effectively making it a sundown town," citing the New Deal Network and James Loewen's Sundown Towns (2018); it says the NAACP complained about TVA hiring, housing and training discrimination in 1934, 1935 and 1938; and that the 2020 census count was 0.4 percent Black or African American (6 residents). (Wikipedia, Norris, Tennessee; opened. I did not open Loewen or the New Deal Network.)
  • The National Register nomination for Norris describes about 1,400 residents on about 4,000 acres, about 400 dwellings, and construction from 1935 to 1937, and does not address race. (National Park Service nomination; opened, scan partly garbled)

Kingston coal ash spill, 2008

  • On December 22, 2008, around 1:00 a.m., a dike failed at TVA's Kingston Fossil Plant. EPA says about 5.4 million cubic yards of coal ash were released into Swan Pond Embayment and adjacent sloughs, about 300 acres beyond the plant's storage area. TVA and EPA Region 4 signed an Administrative Order on Consent under Superfund on May 11, 2009; a project completion meeting was held June 4, 2015. (EPA Region 4 archived page; opened)
  • The Government Accountability Office (GAO) says three homes were made uninhabitable and 23 damaged, and that TVA estimated cleanup at $933 million to $1.2 billion over two to three years, excluding litigation and penalties. As of October 30, 2009, EPA did not directly regulate coal ash disposal in surface impoundments; of 36 states with them, 12 required liners. (GAO-10-85R; opened)
  • Cleanup workers sued Jacobs Engineering, TVA's contractor. Per the Tennessee Lookout: a federal jury in 2018 sided with the workers; the Sixth Circuit rejected Jacobs's derivative-immunity claim in May 2022; Jacobs confirmed a settlement in May 2023 with terms undisclosed and a $35 million offer from late 2021 rejected. Workers say they were given no dust masks and not told the ash was radioactive. TVA agreed to indemnify Jacobs, and Jacobs demanded TVA cover its legal costs in 2015. These are the workers' allegations and a news report. (Tennessee Lookout, May 2023; Tennessee Lookout, May 18, 2022; both opened. I could not open the court opinion.)

Debt and rates

  • GAO-17-343 (March 23, 2017): TVA is under a statutory $30 billion debt limit; debt was about $26 billion in fiscal 2016, with a plan to reduce it by about $4 billion by fiscal 2023 through rate increases, limits on expense growth and lower capital spending; GAO found cost estimates might be optimistic and recommended TVA report debt goals. As of September 30, 2025, the pension plan had about $8.6 billion in assets against about $10.3 billion in liabilities. (GAO-17-343; opened)
  • Tennessee Encyclopedia: a 1959 agreement with the Eisenhower administration required the power program to be financed by rates and bonds, not appropriations; the nuclear program required heavy borrowing and a 25 percent rate increase in 1970; plant closures angered ratepayers. A TVA Inspector General report says the debt ceiling could force higher-cost financing or significant rate increases; its issue date did not show on the page I opened. (TVA OIG on Oversight.gov)

What this case shows

  1. A federal statute made a regional utility, and residents of the valley did not vote on where the dams went. The 1933 Act sets purposes in public law. Families in the reservoir area were removed, with counts ranging from about 3,000 people to 72,000 people overall depending on scope.
  2. Benefits and costs fell on different people. Electricity and income rose across the valley (3 percent of farms electrified in 1933; income from 44 to 61 percent of the national average by 1953). The people moved from Norris and the Black residents told the agency would not employ them at the dam bore costs that were not matched by those benefits, according to Clayton and Walker.
  3. Exclusion is documented in a contemporary article and in later scholarship. Clayton's 1934 report and the Loyola essay agree on unequal services and hiring; the Norris town exclusion rests on Clayton and on Loewen via Wikipedia.
  4. Public power meant public debt and public rate decisions. A $30 billion statutory ceiling, a 1959 self-financing rule and a 1970 rate increase are all matters of public record.
  5. The Kingston spill shows a regulatory gap and a worker-protection dispute. GAO found no direct federal rule on coal ash impoundments in 2009; workers later sued a contractor, and a court rejected the contractor's immunity argument.

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: TVA lifted one of the poorest regions in the country. Mitigation: we state the figures. The Tennessee Encyclopedia says many of those removed later felt better off. We do not weigh that against the removals.
  2. Objection: TVA officials said Black workers were hired in proportion and paid equally. Mitigation: Clayton reports exactly that and says he had no facts to contradict it, then reports the exam, committee and Norris Dam statements.
  3. Objection: the sundown-town label is secondary and the NRHP nomination is silent. Mitigation: we show that the label comes from Wikipedia citing Loewen, which I did not open, and that Clayton (1934) is the closest contemporary source.
  4. Objection: Kingston was an industrial accident, not a TVA design failure, and the worker case blames a contractor. Mitigation: the Sixth Circuit rejected Jacobs's immunity theory and a jury found against Jacobs; TVA's indemnity is reported but is a newspaper's account. We make no claim about intent.
  5. Objection: debt and rate stories are told by agency critics. Mitigation: the debt figures come from GAO.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • The Norris relocation count was not found in a TVA or archival record. 2,841 families (Wikipedia) has no inline citation.
  • The Norris town exclusion rests on Clayton (1934) and on Wikipedia citing Loewen and the New Deal Network; neither was opened. TVA's own policy documents were not found.
  • The Sixth Circuit opinion and the district-court record in the Jacobs case were not opened; Tennessee Lookout is the source for the verdict and settlement. Plaintiff and illness counts conflict.
  • The cleanup's final cost was not found; TVA's $933 million to $1.2 billion is a 2009 estimate.
  • The Tennessee Encyclopedia's displacement and economics claims were not independently checked; the "better off" claim has no data in the page.
  • The TVA OIG report date and the history of the $30 billion ceiling (the 2019 law and earlier) were not confirmed.
  • Current TVA rates were not examined.
