01 / The jobs

Three branches. Different work.

These cards describe the federal government. Open a question for the next layer.

Makes federal laws

Congress

The House and Senate debate and vote on bills. Both must approve the same text before it goes to the president.

What can it decide?

Congress can levy taxes, authorize borrowing and make spending laws. A senator or representative cannot pass a law alone.

Source: Constitution, Article I ↗
Who gets which job?

House members serve two-year terms. Senators serve six-year terms, with two senators per state.

Source: Constitution, Article I ↗
Source: Constitution, Article I ↗

Carries out federal laws

The executive branch

The president leads the executive branch. Departments and agencies handle much of the day-to-day work.

Is the president in charge of everything?

No. Congress makes federal laws, and courts interpret them. The president can veto a bill; Congress can override that veto.

Source: USAGov: branches ↗
What are agencies?

Departments, agencies, boards and commissions are part of the executive branch. Their jobs differ.

Source: USAGov: branches ↗
How do agencies make rules?

Congress passes a law, then agencies write the detailed rules that carry it out. Proposed rules are normally published for public comment before they take effect.

Source: Federal Register ↗
Where can I comment on a proposed rule?

Regulations.gov lists open proposed rules and lets anyone submit a comment.

Source: Regulations.gov ↗
Source: USAGov: branches ↗

Interprets federal laws

The federal courts

The Supreme Court and other federal courts apply laws to cases and decide constitutional questions.

Do judges write the budget?

No. The Constitution gives Congress the power to make appropriations. Courts resolve cases within their jurisdiction.

Source: Constitution, Articles I and III ↗
Who chooses federal judges?

The president nominates Supreme Court justices and other Article III judges. The Senate votes on confirmation.

Source: USAGov: branches ↗
How are federal courts organized?

Trial courts are the U.S. district courts. Appeals go to the regional courts of appeals. The Supreme Court is the final federal appeal and has nine justices.

Source: U.S. Courts: court role and structure ↗Source: Supreme Court: current justices ↗
How long do federal judges serve?

Article III judges hold office “during good Behaviour.” In practice that means for life unless they resign, retire or are removed by impeachment.

Source: Constitution, Article III, Section 1 ↗

02 / Shared power

No single control panel.

Choose a connection to see what it means. These are examples of checks, not lines of supervision.

President → Congress: veto

The president can return a bill with objections. Congress may override the veto with a two-thirds vote in each chamber.

Source: Constitution, Article I, Section 7 ↗
President + Senate → courts: appointments

The president nominates federal judges; the Senate confirms or rejects nominees. Confirmation is not day-to-day supervision of a judge.

Source: USAGov: branches ↗
Courts → laws: constitutional review

Courts decide constitutional questions in cases. Since Marbury v. Madison (1803), federal courts have reviewed laws for conflicts with the Constitution.

Source: National Archives: Marbury v. Madison ↗
House + Senate: impeachment

The House can impeach an official by majority vote. The Senate holds the trial. Conviction requires two-thirds of the senators present.

Source: Constitution, Article I, Sections 2 and 3 ↗
Congress + states: amending the Constitution

An amendment must be proposed by two-thirds of both chambers, or by a convention called by two-thirds of the states. Three-fourths of the states must then ratify it.

Source: Constitution, Article V ↗
03 / Who gets the jobs

Choosing the people in charge.

Different offices are filled in different ways. Open a card to see how.

President

The president is chosen by electors, not by one national vote.

How does that work?

Each state names electors. A candidate needs 270 of the 538 electoral votes to win. A president must be a natural-born citizen, at least 35 years old and 14 years a resident of the United States.

Source: Constitution, Article II and the Twelfth Amendment ↗
Is there a term limit?

Yes. The Twenty-second Amendment limits a person to being elected president twice.

Source: Twenty-second Amendment ↗

U.S. Senate

Two senators per state, elected by the voters of that state.

How long do they serve?

Six-year terms. About one third of the Senate is up for election every two years. Senators have been elected directly since the Seventeenth Amendment.

Source: Seventeenth Amendment ↗

U.S. House

Seats are divided among the states by population.

How long do they serve?

Two-year terms, so every seat is up for election every two years. The House has 435 voting members.

Source: House.gov ↗

A smaller group takes a closer look

Committees study bills, discuss them and can change them. A bill may stop here instead of reaching a floor vote.

One chamber considers the proposal

The House or Senate debates and votes under its own rules. A bill passing one chamber has not yet become law.

4 / Same text

Both chambers must agree

The other chamber considers the bill too. If the versions differ, the differences must be resolved. Both chambers must pass identical text.

5 / President

Sign, veto or take no action

The president may sign or veto the bill. After ten days, excluding Sundays, an unsigned bill becomes law unless an adjournment prevents its return; that is a pocket veto.

Source: Constitution, Article I, Section 7 ↗
Link to this step

6 / Possible override

A veto is not always the end

Congress can override a returned veto with a two-thirds vote in each chamber. A pocket veto cannot be overridden. This path shows lawmaking, not every later step in carrying a law out.

Source: USAGov: making laws ↗
Does every bill start in the House?

No. Bills can start in either chamber, but bills that raise revenue must originate in the House. The Senate can propose amendments.

Source: Constitution, Article I, Section 7 ↗
05 / Follow the money

Who taxes. Who spends.

The Constitution gives these powers to Congress. Agencies carry out spending that Congress has approved.

Taxing and borrowing

Congress has the power to lay and collect taxes and to borrow money.

Where does that say so?

Article I, Section 8 lists the taxing and borrowing powers of Congress.

Source: Constitution, Article I, Section 8 ↗

Spending

Federal money can be spent only as Congress allows by law.

What is an appropriation?

An appropriation is a law that lets an agency spend a set amount for a set purpose. The Constitution says no money may be drawn from the Treasury except by appropriations made by law.

Source: Constitution, Article I, Section 9 ↗

Your tax dollars

Official budget figures are public.

Where can I see them?

The Treasury publishes federal spending and revenue data for the public.

Source: Treasury: America’s Finance Guide ↗
06 / Zoom in

“Government” is more than Washington.

Start by asking which jurisdiction makes the decision. These are orientation cards, not researched job descriptions for every state or town.

Federal

Congress, the president and federal courts work within the powers set out by the U.S. Constitution.

A starting point

For a proposed federal law, start with Congress. For a federal court case, start with the court record.

Source: USAGov: branches ↗

State

The Tenth Amendment reserves powers not delegated to the United States, or prohibited to the states, to the states or the people.

Why state details need their own sources

A federal diagram does not establish a state officer's duties. State constitutions and statutes must be checked for the role in question.

Source: Tenth Amendment ↗
What do all 50 states share?

Every state has its own constitution, a legislature that makes state laws, a governor who leads the executive branch, and a state court system. The details differ by state. Nebraska, for example, has a one-chamber legislature. Many states also let voters decide some laws by ballot measure.

Source: USAGov: state governments ↗
