The Way started with a simple question: if democracy depends on people making informed decisions, why is understanding government so difficult for so many people?

This longer essay is a working research introduction. It brings together history, economic ideas and the proposed tools. Figures are attributed to the dated studies listed at the end; all require a final source and context check before public release.

Why blame the people who don't vote?

Voting is often described as an individual moral choice: go vote, or don't complain. But that skips the conditions in which choices are made.

Census data on the 2024 election show a pronounced turnout gap by education: about 77% of people with a bachelor’s degree voted, against about 52.5% of people with only a high school diploma. That doesn't establish that education alone caused the gap. It does give us a question worth examining alongside registration rules, working hours, transportation and access to information.

The Sentencing Project estimated roughly four million Americans were disenfranchised by felony convictions in 2024. These people are not simply apathetic non-voters. Many had no legal vote to cast.

An institution interested in voting should study the full path from education to registration to polling place to representation, not just the final turnout figure.

Education isn't a side issue

James Madison warned that popular government without popular information or a way to acquire it could become farce or tragedy. John Adams defended educating ordinary people at public expense. Their own era fell far short of extending rights and opportunities to everyone, but the argument remains clear: a public expected to govern itself needs access to education.

Jonathan Kozol's Savage Inequalities explored the differences between schools serving wealthy and poor communities. More recently, EdBuild's widely cited analysis found a $23 billion annual funding disparity between predominantly white and predominantly nonwhite school districts, using the districts and period defined in its study.

The point is not that each school should get the same dollar amount. Children face unequal needs. Equal educational opportunity may require unequal resources.

The 2022 National Assessment of Educational Progress reported only 22% of eighth graders reaching proficiency in civics. The definition of proficiency matters; it is a demanding assessment benchmark, not a claim that every other student knows nothing. Still, the result warrants attention.

The Way wants education and accessible explanation at the center of its work, not in a footnote.

From Oklahoma to Washington

The Dust Bowl is more than an image of a farmer standing in an empty field. It was an environmental and economic catastrophe that displaced families and reshaped communities.

Photographers for the Resettlement Administration and the Farm Security Administration—among them Dorothea Lange and Arthur Rothstein—helped document the people who lived through the Depression and Dust Bowl. The photographs did not replace statistics; they gave the statistics faces and places.

In May 1934, dust from the Great Plains reached Washington, D.C. In the spring of 1935, while Congress was weighing a permanent soil conservation agency, another dust storm darkened the capital. A popular story says Hugh Hammond Bennett stretched out his testimony so lawmakers would see the sky turn brown; it comes mostly from a later admiring biography, and the details vary from telling to telling. What is on the record: on April 27, 1935, the Soil Conservation Act made the Soil Conservation Service permanent.

What stays with me is the underlying contrast: suffering was real before its consequences arrived at the capital. We should build systems that make evidence visible before a disaster must announce itself in person.

Once, the dust reached Washington. This time, we're bringing the water.

Oklahoma's other history

Woody Guthrie came from Okemah, Oklahoma. His songs gave voice to migration, work, poverty and belonging. They remind us that people experience economic arrangements as lives, not only as numbers on a chart.

In 1917, poor Oklahoma tenant farmers and others, including Black, white and Native participants, took part in the Green Corn Rebellion against conscription and economic conditions. The rebellion involved unlawful acts and violence and was suppressed. It should neither be romanticized nor erased. It demonstrates how multiple grievances can converge when people believe ordinary political channels are closed.

Greenwood in Tulsa also belongs in a history of community enterprise and lost opportunity. Black Wall Street illustrates economic success built under exclusionary conditions, as well as the devastation of the 1921 massacre. The history deserves dedicated, carefully sourced treatment rather than a slogan.

None of these histories obliges The Way to adopt every belief or tactic of those involved. They are reminders to investigate who participates, who is heard, and what a society chooses to remember.

Colorado, local politics and information

In 1970, Hunter S. Thompson ran for sheriff in Pitkin County under the Freak Power banner. The campaign was irreverent, sometimes deliberately outrageous, and focused attention on local political participation. Thompson lost. The episode nevertheless remains a case study in people contesting established local politics outside familiar packaging.

Local government is where many concrete priorities are decided: water systems, schools, roads, housing, libraries, permits. The Way's longer-term ambition is to make those choices understandable in terms of resources, time, benefits and risks.

Imagine a community deciding whether to renovate a school or modernize a water-treatment plant first. Residents deserve to see what each requires, what happens if either is delayed, and what uncertainties remain. Not merely the budget total; the actual tradeoffs.

The information problem

Local reporting has declined sharply. Medill's 2025 research estimated about 50 million Americans lived in places with limited or no local news. People without broadband, time, transport or research skills face further obstacles.

When the public cannot inspect decisions, narratives can substitute for evidence. That isn't confined to any one political party or kind of institution.

Films such as Starship Troopers satirize propaganda wrapped in the appearance of information. Its invitation—Would you like to know more?—becomes a different proposition if the button actually opens source documents, assumptions, competing analyses, and correction histories.

The same principle applies to the project's own claims. The Way must not become another demand to trust the person at the microphone.

What the research system will do

First, Python scripts collect authoritative public records: the bill as enacted, amendments, recorded votes, dates and the cited public datasets. That establishes what happened, not what it meant.

Next, an analytical model estimates outcomes under disclosed assumptions. Different assumptions can generate different defensible forecasts. We publish the difference.

Then contextual investigation—including LLM-assisted research—searches for explanations, changes during the legislative process, conflicting evidence and developments after a vote. It does not silently replace the primary record.

Qualified experts peer-review methodology and evidence. Public officials who participate can submit documented reasoning and supporting materials. Reviewers' histories and corrections are visible. Community polling and expert review remain separate.

All institutional analyses retain a dated trail: what was believed, what information was available, which model was used, and what later changed. Private personal data are protected, not treated as permanent public property.

Eventually the system may publish multiple evolving Michi measures. A measure of evidence quality is not the same as a forecast of economic impact or a judgment about someone's honesty. Presenting those as separate dimensions matters.

Why raise the floor?

The initial economic philosophy is straightforward: improving conditions for the disadvantaged deserves special attention, while preserving individual freedom to build beyond the minimum.

Raj Chetty and colleagues' work on absolute income mobility estimates that about 90% of people born in 1940 earned more than their parents, compared with about half of people born in the 1980s, adjusted for inflation. It asks us to investigate the mechanisms of changing opportunity.

We want to test whether stable housing, education, access to healthcare and productive credit can create wider benefits. Sometimes a proposal will work; sometimes it will disappoint. Both findings matter.

This isn't a commitment to defend any economic label. The Way can compare private markets, public institutions and cooperative arrangements by explicit outcomes. An ownership model is a tool, not a substitute for evidence.

Taxes, public financing and sustainability

Basic guarantees require financing. A sustainable public lending program has to account for defaults, administration, inflation, supply limits and the real availability of labor and materials.

The Bank of North Dakota demonstrates that public banking can exist in the United States, but it isn't a proof that every zero-interest lending proposal would work. It charges interest and operates under its own structure.

A right to housing, food or healthcare also differs from a loan. People unable to repay still need a defined minimum guarantee. Tax policy, direct services and lending may have different roles.

There may be simpler taxes, better forms of public investment and ways to make budgets much more intelligible. Those are hypotheses for study, not settled answers.

Your way, my way, The Way

Economic research can calculate measurable effects of a policy, including a controversial social policy. It cannot settle every issue of conscience. The Way can publish consequences without claiming to replace voters' judgment.

Likewise, two policy makers may choose different responses to the same research and still act sincerely. Their reasons, votes and outcomes belong in the record.

The organization begins with a founder's ideas but cannot remain the founder’s property. Membership is free. Donations must not buy extra governing votes. Researchers can contest the methods. Members can contest organizational decisions. The public should be able to inspect the documented institutional record.

Have pity for the minority

The industrial group KMFDM's deliberately ungrammatical name, Kein Mehrheit Für Die Mitleid, is conventionally rendered “No pity for the majority.” We're deliberately turning the joke around: Habt Mitleid mit der Minderheit—have pity for the minority.

Not because the majority doesn't count. Because those with the least money, access and influence often face the greatest difficulty making themselves heard.

We don't need everyone to agree with one founder, one expert or one model. We need people to have the tools to inspect what they're told.

The waves will change the course

The dust changed the conversation.

The waves will change the course.

The Way, like water, will carve a new path through politics. A path that can change with evidence and with the people walking it.

The dust reached Washington. The Way will reach everywhere.
