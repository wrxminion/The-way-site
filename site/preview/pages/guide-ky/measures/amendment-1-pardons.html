Amendment · every Kentucky voter

Constitutional Amendment 1: Limits on pardons near a governor's electionNo pardons by a governor in the weeks around a governor's election

Legislative referral (constitutional amendment): 2026 Acts Chapter 12, placed on the ballot by the Legislature as Senate Bill 10 (2026 Regular Session), 2026 Acts Chapter 12 ↗. Passes with a simple majority of the votes cast on it.

Read the full text of the measure: 2026 Acts Chapter 12 (Senate Bill 10), the enacted amendment text (PDF) ↗

In plain words

Today Kentucky's governor can pardon people (forgive a crime) or commute sentences (make a prison sentence shorter) at any time. This amendment would block both during a window that starts 60 days before the election for governor and runs until the next term begins. The rest of the four-year term would not change. (kentuckylantern.com ↗)

Biggest change

  • Adds a period, starting 60 days before the election for governor, when no governor may grant a pardon or commute a sentence. source: legislature.ky.gov (Kentucky General Assembly)
  • Changes the wording of Section 77 so it refers to "the Governor" and "him or her" instead of "He." source: legislature.ky.gov (Kentucky General Assembly)
  • The amendment's own text ends the no-pardon period on "the fifth Tuesday succeeding the election." The ballot question describes the same end point as "when the Governor is sworn in for that term." source: legislature.ky.gov (Kentucky General Assembly)

Who it affects most: People asking a governor for a pardon or a shorter sentence near the end of a governor's term.

Cost to the state: No state cost was estimated. The legislature's local-mandate note says the cost to counties of putting it on the ballot is "minimal" (extra ballot programming and printing).

What your vote means

YES

Governors could not pardon anyone or shorten sentences from 60 days before each governor's election until the next term starts.

NO

Governors keep the power to pardon and shorten sentences at any time during their term.

Our one-line summary. Kentucky does not publish an official "what your vote means" statement; the ballot title above is the official description.

Why people want it, and why others object

Why this is on the ballot

The Legislature put this on the ballot. It changes the state constitution, so only another statewide vote can change it back. It passes with a simple majority of the votes cast on it.

  • Before leaving office in December 2019, Gov. Matt Bevin issued more than 600 pardons and sentence reductions, including for people convicted of murder and of child sexual assault. source: nkytribune.com
  • Sen. Chris McDaniel (R-Ryland Heights) pursued this change for seven years. The 2026 bill, Senate Bill 10, passed the Senate 36-0 on Jan. 20, passed the House 82-12 with a change on April 1, and the Senate agreed 36-1 the same day. source: legislature.ky.gov (Kentucky General Assembly)
  • The 60-day start was picked so early and absentee voters would know about any pardons before they vote. source: kentuckylantern.com

Why supporters want it

  • Sen. Chris McDaniel, the sponsor, says the change adds accountability, that pardons should not be granted out of sight before voters go to the polls, and that governors keep the power for most of each four-year term. source: kentuckylantern.com

Why opponents object

  • Scottie Ellis, a spokesperson for Gov. Andy Beshear, said Bevin clearly abused the pardon power but argued the fix is electing better governors, not limiting pardons. source: kentuckylantern.com
  • Eric King of the ACLU of Kentucky said limiting pardons weakens an important safety valve in an imperfect justice system, and cautioned against changing the constitution because of one person's actions. source: kentuckylantern.com

Who backs each side

  • For: Sen. Chris McDaniel and the 12 Senate co-sponsors listed on SB 10; it passed both chambers with members of both parties voting yes. Against: the ACLU of Kentucky; Gov. Beshear's office questioned the need. No public record found of campaign committees or money for either side. source: legislature.ky.gov (Kentucky General Assembly)
