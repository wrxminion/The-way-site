Measure 1Lets state senators serve three terms in a row instead of two

Legislative referral (constitutional amendment): LR19CA, Legislative Resolution 19CA (2025). Passes with a majority of the votes cast on it, and the yes votes must also be at least 35% of the total votes cast in the election (Nebraska Constitution, Article XVI, Section 1 ↗).

Read the full text of the measure: Legislative Resolution 19CA, final legal text ↗ and Secretary of State approved ballot statement (PDF, scanned) ↗

In plain words

Today a Nebraska state senator can serve two four-year terms in a row, then must sit out four years before running again. Amendment 1 would allow three terms in a row (12 years). The four-year break after that stays the same. (news.ballotpedia.org ↗)

Biggest change

  • Raises the limit from two consecutive four-year terms to three. source: newsfromthestates.com

Who it affects most: State senators now in office and the voters in their districts, who could keep the same senator four more years.

Cost to the state: Unknown. No cost estimate found.

What your vote means

YES

A senator could serve up to 12 years in a row (three terms) instead of 8.

NO

A senator could still serve only 8 years in a row (two terms).

Our one-line summary. Nebraska does not publish an official "what your vote means" statement; the ballot title above is the official description.

Why people want it, and why others object

Why this is on the ballot

The Legislature put this on the ballot. It changes the state constitution, so only another statewide vote can change it back. It passes with a majority of the votes cast on it.

  • Voters set the two-term limit with Initiative 415 in 2000 (55.8% yes). It took effect in 2006. source: news.ballotpedia.org
  • In 2012 voters turned down a similar change to three terms (Amendment 3), with 64.7% voting against it. source: news.ballotpedia.org
  • The Legislature voted 39-10 on May 28, 2025 to put it on the 2026 ballot. source: news.ballotpedia.org

Why supporters want it

  • Sen. Rob Dover, a sponsor, said term limits hit a one-house legislature harder than two-house states, where "there are two chambers, so that when a representative is termed out, they go to the other chamber." source: news.ballotpedia.org
