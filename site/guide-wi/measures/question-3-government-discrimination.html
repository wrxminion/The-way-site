Question 3: Government discrimination and preferencesBan on government discrimination or preference by race, sex, color, ethnicity or national origin

Legislative referral (constitutional amendment): 2025 Assembly Joint Resolution 102, 2025 Assembly Joint Resolution 102 (AJR 102). Passes with a simple majority of the votes cast on it.

Read the full text of the measure: official referendum notice (PDF) ↗

In plain words

State law and the constitution already ban many kinds of discrimination. This amendment also bans "preferential treatment" (giving someone an advantage) based on race, sex, color, ethnicity or national origin in public jobs, public schools and colleges, government contracts and government services. There are exceptions when following it would cost the state eligibility for a federal program or break federal law, and for jobs where sex is a real job requirement (a "bona fide occupational qualification"). (wausaupilotandreview.com ↗)

Biggest change

  • Creates section 27 of article I: governmental entities may not discriminate against, or grant preferential treatment to, any individual or group on the basis of race, sex, color, ethnicity, or national origin in public employment, education, contracting, or administration. source: somerswi.gov
  • Exceptions: where needed to keep eligibility for a federal program or to follow federal law, and a bona fide occupational qualification exception for certain sex-based jobs. source: wausaupilotandreview.com

Who it affects most: Public employers, public schools and universities, people who receive or compete for government jobs, admissions, scholarships and contracts.

Cost to the state: Unknown. No cost estimate was found.

What your vote means

YES

The constitution would ban state and local governments from giving any preference based on race, sex, color, ethnicity or national origin.

NO

Existing laws against discrimination stay, and preference programs remain legal unless a court strikes them down one at a time.

Our one-line summary. Wisconsin does not publish an official "what your vote means" statement; the ballot title above is the official description.

Why people want it, and why others object

Why this is on the ballot

The Legislature put this on the ballot. It changes the state constitution, so only another statewide vote can change it back. It passes with a simple majority of the votes cast on it.

  • Republicans cited the 2023 U.S. Supreme Court decision striking down Harvard's race-conscious admissions. Arizona has a similar question on its November 2026 ballot. source: wausaupilotandreview.com
  • The Legislature gave it final approval on Jan. 21, 2026: Assembly 54-45, Senate 18-15, along party lines (Republicans yes, Democrats no). source: news.ballotpedia.org

Why supporters want it

  • Sen. Stephen Nass (R-Senate District 11): "Preferential treatment to any individual or group is wrong, no matter who it targets or what the reason." source: news.ballotpedia.org
  • Dan Lennington, Wisconsin Institute for Law & Liberty: "We could sue them one at a time, and we have been suing them one at a time." source: wausaupilotandreview.com

Why opponents object

  • Sen. Dora Drake (D-Senate District 4): "you're outlawing accountability when discrimination does occur." source: news.ballotpedia.org
  • Debra Cronmiller, League of Women Voters of Wisconsin, on positions such as diversity staff in government: "In one moment, those positions could all be illegal." source: wausaupilotandreview.com

Who backs each side

  • For: the Republican majority in the Legislature; Wisconsin Institute for Law & Liberty. Against: ACLU of Wisconsin; League of Women Voters of Wisconsin. No public record of campaign money for either side was found in the sources checked. source: wausaupilotandreview.com
