Proposition 133: life sentences for child sex trafficking

What Proposition 133 would change about the penalty for trafficking a minor for sex, how Colorado law treats it today, what the official arguments say on each side, what it would cost, and what is still unknown.

This page gives no recommendation for or against any measure. A sourced first draft: no reviewer has signed off, and each source says how much of it was read. Prepared October 31, 2026.

What the measure does

Proposition 133 is a citizen initiative that changes Colorado statutes. It needs a majority vote to pass. Its short title in the text is the "Children Are Not For Sale Act." S1

It changes section 18-3-504 of the statutes, on human trafficking of a minor for sexual servitude. The Blue Book summarizes two changes: a mandated sentence of life in prison without parole for child sex trafficking convictions, and an expansion of the crime to include buying sexual activity with a minor. The text adds a third way to commit the crime, "knowingly trades anything of monetary value to buy or sell sexual activity with a minor." It also says nothing in the section limits a governor's standing authority of pardon, commutation or clemency, and that the act takes effect January 1, 2027 and applies to offenses committed on or after that date. S1

The official ballot title asks about "expanding human trafficking of a minor for sexual servitude to include knowingly trading anything of monetary value to buy or sell sexual activity with a minor and increasing the penalty to be life in prison without parole or release." S1

Law today

The Blue Book says that under Colorado law, human trafficking of a minor for sexual servitude, or child sex trafficking, occurs when an adult forces a minor, or makes a minor available, for commercial sexual activity in exchange for money or other compensation. It is one of many sex crimes a buyer or seller may be charged with. A minor legally cannot consent to commercial sexual activity, and current law gives legal defenses and protections to people charged with this crime who are themselves victims of sex trafficking. S1

What it would change

The Blue Book says the measure removes judicial discretion in sentencing, so a judge could not weigh the facts of the case, and that buyers and sellers convicted of the crime would receive a life sentence. S1

The text's own findings give the proponents' reasons: that Colorado has seen an alarming increase in human trafficking, especially of minors, that current penalties "with parole possible in as little as eight years" have failed to deter it, and that "it is the measure of a fair and compassionate society to ensure that children are not for sale." Those are the text's statements, not findings by the Legislative Council. S1

Who wrote it and how it reached the ballot

The Blue Book labels this measure as placed on the ballot by citizen initiative. It says the text "was drafted by the proponents of the initiative," and that the proponents "gathered the required amount of petition signatures." S1

A related 2026 law

The legislature passed a separate bill this year on a nearby set of offenses, SB26-015, signed June 3, 2026. According to its official summary as enacted, it renames the offenses involving "child prostitution" to refer to commercial sexual activity with a child, requires a court to sentence people convicted of listed offenses (other than soliciting) to at least the minimum of the presumptive range for the offense level, requires 364 days in county jail as a probation condition for soliciting, removes the spousal exception from the crime of engaging in commercial sexual activity with a child, and makes internet luring of a child for that purpose a class 3 felony. SB26-015 is a separate law. The official summary we read does not mention the section Proposition 133 changes (18-3-504), and the Blue Book does not discuss it. We list it so readers can see what the legislature did on related offenses this year. How the two would interact if both are in force has not been checked.

The arguments on each side

The Blue Book prints one argument for and one against. They are summarized here, not endorsed. S1 Our voter guide page lists who is paying for each side: Proposition 133 in the Voter Guide.

Arguments in the Blue Book
ForAgainst
Child sex trafficking causes serious, long-lasting harm, and supporters say someone who commits it deserves a life sentence rather than a shorter term or probation. They say the punishment is clear and definite and ensures offenders cannot hurt more children, gives victims certainty, deters people from the crime and sends a message that Colorado takes it seriously.Harsher sentences are unlikely to deter trafficking and may instead punish victims. Opponents say many victims of child sex trafficking are pressured or forced to commit crimes to help traffickers, that fear of a life sentence may make victims less likely to report crimes or cooperate with police, and that a judge could no longer consider the facts of the case. They say the measure prioritizes punishment over prevention and redirects resources from other strategies.

Claims about deterrence and about victims' willingness to report are predictions in the Blue Book's wording of each side's argument. We did not test them against research for this draft.

What it would cost

The Blue Book says the measure increases future spending in the Department of Corrections, with costs beginning in about 14 years because it takes years for the first affected offenders to reach the point in their original sentence when they would have been paroled. Initial costs are estimated at $180,000 in budget year 2041-42, growing by that amount for several years (for example $360,000 in 2042-43 and $540,000 in 2043-44), not adjusted for inflation. It says the increase eventually stabilizes as the cost of longer stays is offset by life sentences ending when offenders die or are possibly given a commutation or pardon. Actual costs are likely to be higher with inflation or if more people are convicted, and medical costs will grow as offenders age in prison. Trial courts and public defender agencies would see a minimal workload increase. The measure names no funding source, so the Blue Book assumes the General Fund. County-funded district attorneys would also see a minimal increase in workload. S1

What research says

We have not yet gathered research for this study. Questions it could address include whether longer mandatory sentences deter sex trafficking of minors, how often people charged with child sex trafficking are themselves trafficking victims, how mandatory sentences affect victims' willingness to report, and how many people are convicted under the current statute each year. We will not list a finding until we have read the study and can say who ran it, what place and period it covers, and what it cannot show.

What we do not yet know

We do not know how many people are convicted under the current statute each year or how many would be convicted of the new "buy" offense, since no saved source counts them. We do not know how the defenses and protections for victims described in the Blue Book would work alongside a mandatory life sentence; the Blue Book says current law provides them, but we have not read how the text handles them.

Open work before this could be called a reviewed study: read the full text of section 18-3-504 as it stands now beside the measure's changes; get the Title Board record and proponent names from the Secretary of State; get conviction counts from the courts or the Department of Corrections; gather research on all sides; and get a reviewer who knows Colorado criminal sentencing.
