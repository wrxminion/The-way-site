Amendment 83: a constitutional right to hunt and fish

What Amendment 83 would put in the Colorado Constitution, who sets hunting and fishing rules today, what the official arguments say on each side, and what is still unknown.

This page gives no recommendation for or against any measure. A sourced first draft: no reviewer has signed off, and each source says how much of it was read. Prepared October 31, 2026.

What the measure does

Amendment 83 is a citizen initiative. It would add a new section 13 to article XVIII of the Colorado Constitution, titled "Right to fish and hunt." It needs 55 percent of the vote to pass. S1

The text has four parts. (1) "There is established a right of the people of Colorado to hunt, fish, and take fish and wildlife," including by "traditional methods." The right applies to all species the state manages except nongame species, endangered species and any species that is illegal to hunt under federal law. (2) The policy of the state is that hunting and fishing are "the preferred means of responsibly managing fish and wildlife populations." (3) The section is "not intended to authorize trespass upon private property or to modify any provision of statutes relating to trespass or property rights." (4) The section "does not limit the authority" of a state agency or the General Assembly to regulate hunting, fishing and wildlife management if the law is "necessary for sound scientific wildlife conservation and management, public safety, or to preserve the future of hunting and fishing opportunities for all species statewide." S1

The text does not define "traditional methods."

How hunting and fishing are managed today

The Blue Book says Colorado Parks and Wildlife (CPW) is the state agency responsible for fish and wildlife management and administers hunting and fishing regulations adopted by the CPW Commission. It describes the Commission as a citizen board appointed by the Governor, and says state law requires wildlife and their environment to be "protected, preserved, enhanced, and managed for the use, benefit, and enjoyment of the people and visitors of Colorado." Current rules cover where and when hunting and fishing can occur, license and permit requirements, methods and materials, and limits on how many animals can be hunted, fished or trapped, generally guided by information on populations, species and habitat plans, and recreation needs. S1

CPW's own news releases describe the Commission as made up of 11 governor-appointed members, with seats set aside for different interests. Releases from 2023 and 2024 describe appointees representing sportspersons, agricultural producers, outdoor recreation and parks use, and a nonprofit that promotes conservation and non-consumptive wildlife use. What it might change

The Blue Book says the measure "results in no immediate change to existing state law or regulations." Hunting and fishing continue under existing state and federal rules. But creating a constitutional right "may affect how the legislature or CPW Commission makes policies" and "could lead to changes depending on how courts interpret the language of the measure when reviewing existing or future policies." S1

Two pieces of the text pull in different directions, and a court would have to weigh them. Part (1) creates a right and part (2) sets a state policy favoring hunting and fishing as a management tool. Part (4) keeps state power to regulate when a law is necessary for sound science, public safety or the future of hunting and fishing. Supporters point to part (4) as a safeguard; opponents, in the Blue Book, say vague wording could invite challenges to existing rules. Which reading prevails has not been tested.

Who wrote it and how it reached the ballot

The Blue Book labels this measure as placed on the ballot by citizen initiative. It says the text of the measure "was drafted by the proponents of the initiative," and that the proponents "gathered the required amount of petition signatures." S1

What we do not have: the proponents' names, the filing and Title Board hearing record, earlier drafts of the text, and the signature totals. The arguments on each side

The Blue Book prints one argument for and one against. They are summarized here, not endorsed. The Secretary of State lists the organizations supporting and opposing each measure. S1 Our voter guide page lists who is paying for each side: Amendment 83 in the Voter Guide.

Arguments in the Blue Book
ForAgainst
It gives lasting protection against future attempts to restrict or end hunting and fishing, and supporters say Colorado should join the 24 other states that protect the right in their constitutions. Hunting and fishing are part of the state's heritage and cultural identity and matter to tourism and recreation. License revenue pays for habitat and wildlife management across the state: the Blue Book gives one-third of CPW's annual revenue, about $115 million, as coming from license sales.A state constitution should protect fundamental rights, not recreational activities. Opponents say Colorado is already a leader in wildlife conservation, that the right is unnecessary, and that it promotes one management method over others. They say it may restrict guardrails on hunting, fishing and trapping, and that undefined, vague language could prompt costly legal challenges to rules that voters, lawmakers and CPW have already put in place.

The count of 24 other states and the $115 million figure are given by the Blue Book as part of the supporters' argument. We did not check either against a separate source.

What it would cost

The Blue Book says the measure is not expected to significantly change state spending in the near term, but could raise legal costs as the right is applied. CPW may have more workload and cost for legal advice when writing rules to comply with the measure, and CPW litigation costs would rise if existing or new rules are challenged as unconstitutional. It says the amount cannot be estimated because it depends on private parties and court decisions. S1

We have not yet gathered research for this study. Questions it could address include how other states with such a right have handled challenges to wildlife rules, how license revenue relates to conservation spending, and how hunting and fishing figure in Colorado's economy. What we do not yet know

We do not know how Colorado courts would read "traditional methods," the "preferred means" policy, or the limits in part (4). We have not checked which states have such a provision or how it has been used in court there. We have not verified the Blue Book's revenue and state-count figures.

Open work before this could be called a reviewed study: ask a lawyer who handles Colorado constitutional and wildlife law what the text would do; list the other states' provisions and any court decisions about them; confirm CPW's license revenue from its own budget documents; gather research on all sides; and get a reviewer.
