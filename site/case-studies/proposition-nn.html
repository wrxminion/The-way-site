Proposition NN: keeping TABOR revenue for schools and children

What Proposition NN would let the state keep and spend, who sponsored it and how the legislature voted, how it changes TABOR refunds, what the official arguments say on each side, and what is still unknown.

This page gives no recommendation for or against any measure. A sourced first draft: no reviewer has signed off, and each source says how much of it was read. Prepared October 31, 2026.

What the measure does

Proposition NN is a statute change the legislature referred to voters. It needs a majority vote to pass. S1

The Blue Book says it lets the state keep and spend revenue that the TABOR limit would otherwise require it to refund, up to a new limit that is higher than the current one and based on what the state spends on public education. Retained money must go first to reimburse local governments for property tax exemptions for seniors, veterans with a disability and Gold Star surviving spouses (already required under current law as a refund method), and the rest into a new Children's Account. The new limit continues indefinitely, and revenue above it must still be refunded. S1

What a yes or no vote means

Official wording, Blue Book
VoteWhat the Blue Book says
Yes"allows the state to keep some or all of the revenue that otherwise would be refunded to taxpayers under TABOR. The retained revenue must be spent on K-12 education, programs that support children, and any other purposes."
No"maintains current TABOR refunds and funding levels for K-12 education, programs that support children, and other state purposes."

The official ballot title, written by legislative legal staff, asks whether state investment in K-12 public education should "increase two percent each year for the next ten years" to raise teacher pay, improve teacher retention, lower class sizes and increase career and technical courses, "without raising taxes but instead funded by raising the annual limit on state fiscal year spending only by the amount spent on public K-12 education as a voter-approved revenue change." The title also mentions an annual public report on how the new money is spent. S1

How it works with TABOR

The Blue Book explains that the TABOR limit equals the prior year's limit adjusted for inflation plus population growth, and that revenue above it must be refunded unless voters allow the state to keep it. TABOR refunds are different from the tax refunds owed when someone overpays; those are not affected. S1 For TABOR's history and what research says about it, see our study TABOR: voter control, public revenue, and Colorado's ballot choices.

Under Proposition NN the most the state may keep is the current TABOR limit plus the highest amount it has spent on K-12 education from certain funding sources in previous years. The Blue Book calls this the Proposition NN limit. In the first year that limit would be about $4.6 billion above the current TABOR limit, but the Blue Book estimates the state would actually keep about $500 million, the amount it expects to collect above the TABOR limit. State revenue is expected to stay well below the new limit and not exceed it for at least the next five to ten years; beyond that the projections are uncertain. S1

The Blue Book gives three scenarios. (1) If revenue exceeds the new limit, refunds are reduced, which is not expected soon. (2) If revenue is above the current TABOR limit but below the new one, refunds are eliminated, which it calls the most likely scenario for the next several years unless there is a recession. (3) If revenue falls below the current TABOR limit, as in a recession, the measure has no effect on refunds because none would be paid. S1

Where the money goes

The Blue Book describes the Children's Account in this order. First, money pays for increased teacher pay and retention, career and technical courses, and smaller class sizes, distributed to school districts and charter schools, which must post downloadable information online on how they spent it. This amount grows each year for ten years then stays flat: about $110 million in the first year and $220 million in the second, possibly $1 billion to $2 billion per year by year ten, paid only if enough revenue is retained. Second, in the first ten years, if that spending is under half the account, the measure requires extra spending on disability services, school services and more instructional hours so at least half the account goes to K-12 overall (about $130 million in year one and $20 million in year two). Third, in the first ten years, what remains goes to programs that support children, prioritizing full-day preschool and child care (about $240 million and $230 million in the first two years), with the legislature deciding how. After ten years the remaining money may be spent on any purpose the legislature decides. S1

The bill's official summary adds that the state auditor must publish a report on the excess revenue retained and how it was spent, that the funding increase is built from a "positive factor" of 2 percent a year that compounds for ten years, and that the act protects the expanded earned income tax credit, the family affordability tax credit and the affordable housing financing fund from being harmed by voter approval. S2

Who sponsored it and how the legislature voted

This is the one Colorado measure on the ballot placed there by the legislature. The text was "referred to the voters because it passed by a majority vote of the state senate and the state house of representatives." S1 The measure is bill SB26-135.

  • Prime sponsors on the bill page: Senators Jeff Bridges and Cathy Kipp, and Representatives Jennifer Bacon and Meghan Lukens. The page lists many additional sponsors in both chambers. S2
  • Committees named on the page: Senate Finance, Senate Appropriations and House Appropriations. S2
  • Recorded votes on the bill page: Senate third reading, April 27, 2026, 23 yes and 12 no. House third reading, May 9, 2026, 42 yes, 21 no and 2 not voting. After House amendments, the Senate repassed it on May 12, 2026 by 23 yes and 12 no. S2

The bill page also lists 27 amendments and several amendment votes (for example two House floor amendments, H.001 and H.002, that lost). We have not read those amendments, so this page does not say how the final text differs from the introduced bill. Individual members' votes are on the linked vote records, which we have not saved.

If Proposition 137 also passes

The Blue Book says Proposition 137 reduces the amount of revenue that counts against the TABOR limit, and does not change the Proposition NN limit, but if 137 passes the amount the state actually keeps and spends under NN "may be reduced or eliminated." S1 Our study of Proposition 137 will cover that measure.

The arguments on each side

The Blue Book prints two arguments for and two against. They are summarized here, not endorsed. The Secretary of State lists the organizations supporting and opposing each measure. S1 Our voter guide page lists who is paying for each side: Proposition NN in the Voter Guide.

Arguments in the Blue Book
ForAgainst
It raises teacher pay, reduces class sizes and improves career and technical education in every district. Supporters cite a state study finding a $3.5 billion school funding shortfall, about $4,000 per pupil, and say the measure redirects money the state already collects, with accountability for how it is spent.It is essentially a tax increase that permanently eliminates or reduces TABOR refunds. Opponents call refunds a taxpayer right, say losing them is a significant hit to stretched households, and say TABOR was put in the constitution to guard against unchecked government growth.
It frees up money that should already be supporting children. Supporters say the cost of educating and caring for a child keeps rising, the current limit does not keep pace, and that permanent relief is overdue, using existing revenue for child care, universal preschool and other early childhood needs.It eventually hands the state over $5 billion per year. Opponents say school funding rose 25 percent over five years while enrollment declines, and that government should first show it has exhausted ways to cut waste, remove redundant programs and deliver services more efficiently.

The $3.5 billion shortfall, the 25 percent funding increase and the $5 billion figure are the Blue Book's wording of each side's argument. We did not check them against separate sources. The Blue Book's own estimate of the amount retained is about $500 million in the first year.

Cost and refunds

The Blue Book says the measure reduces the amount refunded to taxpayers by $329.9 million in budget year 2026-27 and $521.0 million in 2027-28, and eliminates TABOR refunds in both years, with varying amounts in later years. Its example table shows estimated refunds that would go to $0 under the measure for single and joint filers at adjusted gross incomes from $50,000 to $500,000 (for 2027 under current law, $21 to $65 for a single filer and $42 to $130 for a joint filer). It says the effect could be none in some years and more than $1,000 per taxpayer in others, depending on how much the state collects above the current limit. S1

On spending, the Blue Book estimates a net increase of $329.9 million in 2026-27 and $521.0 million in 2027-28. In 2026-27 that is $241.5 million for K-12 education and $241.5 million for programs that support children, less $153.1 million that would otherwise be available for general government purposes. In 2027-28 it adds $206.1 million for property tax reimbursements, $234.0 million each for K-12 and children's programs, minus the same $153.1 million, plus $20,000 for the Office of State Auditor. State funding for school districts rises by an estimated $241.5 million and $234.0 million. S1

What research says

We have not yet gathered research for this study. The measure's own legislative declaration cites two commissioned school funding adequacy studies (by the American Institutes for Research and by Augenblick, Palaich and Associates) delivered to the legislature in January 2025, and asserts that research shows school funding raises graduation rates and lifetime wages. We have not read those studies and do not repeat their findings. Other questions research could address are how school spending affects outcomes, what the evidence says about early childhood programs, and how states that keep revenue above refund limits have spent it. The TABOR study lists research on TABOR itself.

What we do not yet know

Revenue forecasts past budget year 2027-28 are not available, so the long-run effect on refunds is uncertain, as the Blue Book says. We do not know how much of the retained money would go to teacher pay versus other uses in later years, since amounts depend on collections. We have not read the adequacy studies or the bill's amendments.

Open work before this could be called a reviewed study: read the adequacy studies cited in the measure; read the bill's amendments and the final signed text; get the individual roll call records; gather research on all sides; and get a reviewer who knows school finance. No reviewer has signed off.
