Amendment 87: a graduated state income tax

What Amendment 87 would change about Colorado's flat income tax, what it would do to taxes at different incomes, how it reached the ballot, what the official arguments say on each side, and what is still unknown.

This page gives no recommendation for or against any measure. A sourced first draft: no reviewer has signed off, and each source says how much of it was read. Prepared October 31, 2026.

What the measure does

Amendment 87 is a citizen initiative that changes both the Colorado Constitution and the state statutes. It needs a majority vote to pass. S1

Per the Blue Book, it removes the constitutional requirement that income be taxed at a single flat rate and creates a graduated tax starting in 2027, with six brackets. It lowers taxes on taxable income below $500,000 and raises them above $500,000. The extra money must be spent on K-12 education, health care, and early childhood care and education. It must add to, not replace, spending on those services, and the legislature decides which programs get it. The extra revenue is exempt from the state's constitutional revenue limit (TABOR), and the state must produce an annual report on how much extra revenue was collected and how it was spent. S1

No"keeps the current constitutional requirement for a flat state income tax rate."

The official ballot title begins "Shall state taxes be increased $2.7 billion annually," a figure the Blue Book explains is the maximum possible change in state revenue for budget year 2027-28 allowing for forecast error, not its main estimate (see "Cost and revenue" below). S1

The Blue Book's worked example: a taxpayer with $125,000 of taxable income pays 3.7 percent on the first $25,000 ($925), 4.2 percent on the next $75,000 ($3,150) and 4.4 percent on the last $25,000 ($1,100), for $5,175, which is $325 less than the $5,500 owed now. S1

Where TABOR fits

Two different TABOR rules are involved, and the Blue Book separates them. The measure removes the TABOR requirement that income be taxed at one rate. Separately, it exempts the extra revenue from the TABOR revenue limit, so it would not count toward refunds. The Blue Book says the measure has no effect on current TABOR refunds or how they work: it expects $15 billion of income tax revenue in 2028 under current law and about $2 billion more under the measure, the added $2 billion being outside the limit. S1

For the history of TABOR, how it has been changed, and what research says about it, see our study TABOR: voter control, public revenue, and Colorado's ballot choices.

If Amendment 87 and Proposition 136 both pass

Proposition 136 limits the state income tax rate at the current 4.4 percent. Amendment 87's rates above $500,000 are higher than that. The Blue Book says that when parts of voter-approved measures conflict, the outcome is generally decided by the measure that got more votes, but that if both pass, "the exact outcome is unclear" and the legislature or a court will have to resolve it. S1 Our study of Proposition 136 will cover its own text.

The arguments on each side

The Blue Book prints three arguments for and three against. They are summarized here, not endorsed. The Secretary of State lists the organizations supporting and opposing each measure. S1 Our voter guide page lists who is paying for each side: Amendment 87 in the Voter Guide.

Arguments in the Blue Book
ForAgainst
It cuts taxes for 97 percent of taxpayers, including most households and small businesses, and raises them only on top earners who can afford it. Supporters say a graduated tax is more equitable because a dollar of tax is a bigger hardship at $30,000 than at $1 million.It is a multibillion-dollar tax increase, and higher rates on high incomes will harm the economy by making Colorado less competitive for businesses and perhaps pushing high earners to leave. Because the brackets do not rise with inflation, more taxpayers will pay the higher rates each year.
It funds critical services when more money is needed. Supporters point to federal policy changes, underfunded public schools and likely Medicaid cuts.It attacks the fair and predictable system TABOR guarantees. Opponents say the flat tax is fair because everyone has equal access to services, high earners already pay more, and low earners qualify for more deductions and credits, and that the state should prioritize the money it already collects.
A graduated tax is a proven approach: supporters say the federal government and 27 other states use one, and that sales and property taxes weigh more heavily on lower-income households.It would permanently expand government, increase careless spending and hinder growth. Opponents say policy should instead lower taxes for everyone or fund specific proven programs.

The "97 percent" and "27 other states" figures are the Blue Book's wording of the supporters' arguments. Neither is the Legislative Council's own finding, and we did not check them against separate sources.

Cost and revenue

The Blue Book estimates the measure raises income tax revenue by $958.4 million in budget year 2026-27 (a half-year effect of tax year 2027) and $1.972 billion in 2027-28, more in later years depending on income and population growth. The money goes into a new Colorado Future's Account created by the measure and is exempt from TABOR. The $2.7 billion in the ballot title is the maximum possible change for 2027-28 allowing for forecast error. Setting up the tax change would cost the Department of Revenue about $98,000 once. S1

By income, the Blue Book's Table 3 estimates the average change in tax burden for taxpayers in the $1,000,000-or-more federal adjusted gross income category at +$26,085, with a total of about $1.208 billion from 46,328 taxpayers, and average decreases between $1 and $325 in every category below $1,000,000, including an average decrease of $284 in the $500,000 to $999,999 category. It notes these ranges use federal adjusted gross income, not the taxable income used in Tables 1 and 2, so the two sets of ranges do not line up. S1

What research says

We have not yet gathered research for this study. Questions it could address include whether higher state income tax rates on high earners lead them to leave a state, how spending on schools, health care and child care changes outcomes, and how graduated and flat state income taxes compare in revenue stability. The TABOR study cites research on TABOR itself. We will not list a finding here until we have read the study and can say who ran it, what place and period it covers, and what it cannot show.

What we do not yet know

The revenue figures are forecasts. Whether people or businesses would change where they live or report income is not measured in any source we read. We do not know how a conflict with Proposition 136 would be resolved if both pass.
