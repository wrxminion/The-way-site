Proposition 136: income tax rate limit

What Proposition 136 would put in statute about the state income tax rate, what happens if it and Amendment 87 both pass, what the official arguments say, and what is still unknown.

This page gives no recommendation for or against any measure. A sourced first draft: no reviewer has signed off, and each source says how much of it was read. Prepared October 31, 2026.

What the measure does

Sourcing note: this page so far rests on the Colorado Blue Book alone. We have not yet read the Title Board record, the proponents' filings, or any outside reporting.

Proposition 136 is a citizen initiative that changes Colorado statutes. It needs a majority vote to pass. The text adds section 39-22-102.5, "Maximum Tax Rate": starting January 1, 2027, neither the individual income tax rate nor the corporate income tax rate "may exceed 4.4% of a taxpayer's federal taxable income." It takes effect on proclamation of the Governor and applies to the tax year beginning January 1, 2027 and all later years. S1

The Blue Book says the measure does not change the current 4.4 percent rate; it sets that rate as the maximum. It also says the measure does not change how the rate can be adjusted: the legislature or a citizen initiative may lower the rate, and the Colorado Constitution already requires voter approval for any tax rate increase. S1

No"keeps the current state income tax rate of 4.4 percent without establishing a new limit in statute."

If Amendment 87 also passes

The Blue Book says Amendment 87 would create a graduated income tax, and that its proposed rate for incomes over $500,000 would be higher than the 4.4 percent limit in Proposition 136. It says that when parts of voter-approved measures conflict, the outcome is generally decided by the measure that receives more votes, but "if both measures are approved by voters, the exact outcome is unclear" and the legislature or a court would resolve any conflict. S1 See our study of Amendment 87.

Who wrote it and how it reached the ballot

The Blue Book labels this measure as placed on the ballot by citizen initiative. It says the text of the measure "was drafted by the proponents of the initiative," and that the proponents "gathered the required amount of petition signatures." S1

The arguments on each side

The Blue Book prints one argument for and one against. They are summarized here, not endorsed. S1

Arguments in the Blue Book
ForAgainst
The measure reinforces voter support for keeping the current rate. Supporters say the rate, "overwhelmingly approved by voters in 2022," is fair and keeps Colorado's tax rate among the lowest in the country.The initiative is designed to put Amendment 87 into legal jeopardy, causing confusion and a possibly costly legal battle. Opponents say that after the 2026 election the measure has no effect, since raising rates already requires voter approval and any such approval would override it.

These are the sides' claims in the Blue Book's wording. We have not checked them, including the 2022 vote and the "among the lowest" comparison.

What it would cost

The Blue Book says the measure limits the rate to no higher than the current 4.4 percent, results in no change to state or local revenue or spending, and so has no fiscal impact. S1

What we do not yet know

We do not know who proposed the measure or how its text changed, how a court or the legislature would resolve a conflict with Amendment 87 if both pass, or whether the measure's effective date wording would matter in that fight.
