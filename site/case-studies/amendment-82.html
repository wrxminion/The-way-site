Amendment 82: a constitutional right to buy and sell natural gas

What Amendment 82 would put in the Colorado Constitution, how the state and cities regulate natural gas today, what the official arguments say on each side, and what is still unknown.

This page gives no recommendation for or against any measure. A sourced first draft: no reviewer has signed off, and each source says how much of it was read. Prepared October 31, 2026.

What the measure does

Amendment 82 is a citizen initiative. It would add a new section 17 to article XVIII of the Colorado Constitution, titled "Right to natural gas." It needs 55 percent of the vote to pass. S1

The whole text is two sentences. First: "Consumers have the right to purchase natural gas for cooking or heating in homes or businesses throughout the state." Second: "Distributors and utilities have the right to sell natural gas for homes or businesses." S1

The text does not say whether the rights can be limited, by whom, or for what reasons. It does not mention building codes, appliance rules, emissions rules or utility plans.

The official ballot title asks: "Shall there be an amendment to the Colorado Constitution creating new law granting the right for consumers to purchase natural gas for cooking or heating in homes or businesses and for distributors and utilities to sell natural gas to consumers?" S1

How natural gas is regulated today

Natural gas is a fossil fuel made mostly of methane. In homes and businesses it can power furnaces, water heaters, clothes dryers, stoves and ovens. The Blue Book notes that electricity and propane are other common household energy sources in Colorado for the same appliances. S1

The Blue Book describes three layers of regulation. The Public Utilities Commission (PUC) regulates investor-owned gas utilities, including rates, safety and clean heat plans. The Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment regulates air pollution and sets emissions standards for natural gas appliances. Local governments may regulate the installation and repair of gas service, delivery, pipelines and appliances, and the Blue Book says several municipalities have restricted gas appliances in new construction or certain renovations. Its example is the Town of Crested Butte, which it says recently updated its building code to require that new residential buildings use electricity for heating, water heating and other appliances. S1

Clean heat plans come from a 2021 state law, Senate Bill 21-264. The PUC's fact sheet says it requires investor-owned gas utilities with more than 90,000 customers to carry out plans that meet greenhouse gas targets: a 4 percent reduction by 2025 and 22 percent by 2030, measured from a 2015 baseline. The law also sets a cost cap of 2.5 percent of annual gas bills, which the Commission can exceed only if it finds the plan is in the public interest, protects income-qualified customers and has benefits that exceed costs. S2 S3

On December 1, 2025, the PUC issued a written decision setting a 41 percent reduction target for 2035, again from 2015. It postponed targets for 2040, 2045 and 2050 until 2032 and did not cap how much recovered methane (for example gas captured at landfills) can count toward the targets. The rule applies to three utilities: Atmos Energy, Black Hills Energy and Xcel Energy. Utilities may meet the targets through efficiency, electrification, recovered methane, green hydrogen, thermal energy and pyrolysis of tires, and customers take part voluntarily through rebates and incentives. S2

The fact sheet is dated December 15, 2025 and says requests for rehearing were due December 22 and would be decided at a January meeting. We did not find the outcome of those requests, so we do not say whether the December decision stands unchanged.

What it might change

The Blue Book's own answer is hedged: by establishing the right in the constitution, the measure "may limit the ability for state or local governments to enforce existing or future regulations related to natural gas." S1

That means no source we read can say which rules would survive. A constitutional right with no stated limits has to be interpreted by courts, and no court has done so for this text. The fiscal analysis points the same way: it expects the Department of Local Affairs to revise technical assistance on building codes "that conflict with the right," and expects local governments with gas restrictions to spend on legal services to bring their codes in line. S1

What could be affected, according to the Blue Book's own descriptions, are local building-code rules like Crested Butte's, and possibly the PUC and air-quality rules. What would not obviously change is the right of a customer to choose electricity, propane or any other fuel; the text says nothing about that.

Who wrote it and how it reached the ballot

The Blue Book labels this measure as placed on the ballot by citizen initiative. It says the text of the measure "was drafted by the proponents of the initiative," and that the proponents "gathered the required amount of petition signatures." S1

What we do not have: the proponents' names, the filing and Title Board hearing record, earlier drafts of the text, and the signature totals. None of these is in the sources we saved, so this page does not name anyone. Where to get them: the Secretary of State's initiative file for this measure and its Title Board record, and the Secretary of State's list of organizations supporting and opposing each 2026 measure, which the Blue Book gives at coloradosos.gov/pubs/elections/Initiatives/ballot/contacts/2026.html. We have not read these.

The arguments on each side

The Blue Book prints one argument for and one against. They are summarized here, not endorsed. The Secretary of State lists the organizations supporting and opposing each measure. S1 Our voter guide page lists who is paying for each side: Amendment 82 in the Voter Guide.

Arguments in the Blue Book
ForAgainst
Natural gas is affordable and widely available and heats homes and businesses reliably, including in extreme cold. Gas appliances are proven technology and, supporters say, most Colorado households rely on gas as their primary heating source. The measure would ensure continued access "without interference from state or local governments."Burning natural gas emits greenhouse gases and contributes to air pollution, and opponents say it should not be protected in the constitution. They say doing so would limit efforts to cut emissions, improve air quality and adopt options such as wind, solar and geothermal, and that local communities should be able to decide on public health, energy needs and climate goals.

The For argument's claim about how many households use gas as their main heating source is the Blue Book's wording of the supporters' argument. We did not check it against a household energy survey.

What it would cost

The Blue Book says the measure may minimally increase state expenditures, mainly in the Department of Local Affairs for revised technical assistance to local governments. It may also add workload and legal services for agencies that regulate or monitor gas utility emissions or take part in those cases: the PUC, the Departments of Natural Resources and of Public Health and Environment, and the Colorado Energy Office. It gives no dollar figure. S1

For local governments, it says those that have or are considering building codes that regulate, prohibit or restrict natural gas may have higher legal and compliance costs to conform their codes with state law and to change permitting, inspection and compliance work. It gives no dollar figure. S1

What research says

We have not yet gathered research for this study. Questions it could address include how gas and electric heating compare on cost to households in Colorado's climate, how reliable each is in extreme cold, how gas appliance use affects indoor and outdoor air quality, and how much local gas rules have changed what gets built. We will not list a finding until we have read the study and can say who ran it, what place and period it covers, and what it cannot show.

What we do not yet know

Which existing or future rules a constitutional "right" would override is not settled by any source we read. The Blue Book says only that the measure "may" limit enforcement. We do not know how many Colorado local governments have gas restrictions beyond the Crested Butte example the Blue Book gives. We do not know the dollar costs the fiscal analysis leaves out.

Open work before this could be called a reviewed study: ask a lawyer who handles Colorado constitutional and utility law what the text would do; list every Colorado local gas ordinance and date it; get household energy data from a named public source; read the final outcome of the PUC rehearing requests; gather research on all sides; and get a reviewer. No reviewer has signed off.
