Amendment 86: changing congressional maps between censuses

What Amendment 86 would require before Colorado's congressional districts could change outside the ten-year schedule, how the 2018 and 2021 process worked, what the official arguments say on each side, and what is still unknown.

This page gives no recommendation for or against any measure. A sourced first draft: no reviewer has signed off, and each source says how much of it was read. Prepared October 31, 2026.

What the measure does

Amendment 86 is a citizen initiative. It needs 55 percent of the vote to pass. It repeals and reenacts the article V sections that create Colorado's Independent Congressional Redistricting Commission (sections 44 through 44.6), and adds a new subsection (1.5) to section 44. S1

Subsection (1.5) is the new rule. In its words: "Upon adoption and approval of the final plan, no plan may be modified for the 2028 congressional election or thereafter except with the approval of the congressional commission and adoption by the Colorado Supreme Court. In reviewing the plan, the Commission must hold at least three public meetings and must not adopt any plan that has the effect of dividing communities of interest or purposefully favoring one political party." S1

The official ballot title describes the rest as "reenacting the current process for congressional redistricting in the Colorado Constitution." S1 We have not compared the reenacted sections word for word with the current constitution, so this page does not say whether they differ in any way other than subsection (1.5).

The Blue Book summary says the measure bars off-cycle changes that "unnecessarily divide" communities of interest. The measure's text says a plan must not have "the effect of dividing communities of interest," with no "unnecessarily." The text, not the summary, is what would become law. The text defines a community of interest as a group sharing substantial interests that may be the subject of federal legislative action, in a reasonably proximate population, and says it does not include relationships with political parties, incumbents or candidates. S1

The official ballot title asks: "Shall there be an amendment to the Colorado Constitution concerning congressional redistricting, and, in connection therewith, reenacting the current process for congressional redistricting in the Colorado Constitution and prohibiting modifications to a final map unless at least three public meetings are held, the modifications do not have the effect of dividing communities of interest or purposefully favoring one political party, and are approved by the congressional redistricting commission and the Colorado Supreme Court?" How districts are drawn today

The Blue Book says Colorado has eight congressional districts, each electing one member of the U.S. House. Using the 2020 census population of 5.8 million, the required district size is 721,714 people. Districts are normally redrawn after each census, every ten years. S1

Before 2018 the General Assembly drew the congressional map. The 2018 Blue Book says the process had ended in court action the last four times it was used, and that Colorado then had seven seats. S2 In 2018 voters approved Amendment Y, which created the commission. Under Amendment Y as the 2018 Blue Book described it: a 12-member commission with equal numbers from the two largest parties and from unaffiliated voters, which amends maps drawn by nonpartisan legislative staff; at least 8 of 12 commissioners, including at least 2 unaffiliated, must approve a final map; the Colorado Supreme Court must approve it unless it finds the commission abused its discretion in applying the required criteria; and the court must approve a map by December 15 of the redistricting year. S2

The Blue Book for this year says the last commission formed and finished its work in 2021 and the next will meet in 2031. S1 On November 1, 2021 the Colorado Supreme Court, in In re Colorado Independent Congressional Redistricting Commission, 2021 CO 73, concluded the commission "did not abuse its discretion in applying the criteria" and approved the plan "for Colorado's congressional districts for the ensuing decade." S3

What current law does not cover, according to the Blue Book, is a change between those ten-year cycles. It says the current process "is designed for redrawing congressional and legislative districts immediately following the census and does not establish a process specifically for off-cycle redistricting," and that since 2025 at least ten states have changed their congressional maps off-cycle. S1 We did not check that count against a separate source.

What it might change

The Blue Book says that if an off-cycle change were ever proposed, the measure requires the commission to approve it after at least three public hearings, and the Colorado Supreme Court to adopt it. The measure also bars off-cycle changes that intentionally favor a political party or unnecessarily divide communities of interest, and any such effort would have to use population figures from the most recent census or another data source. S1

The text's start date is the 2028 congressional election. Today, no source we read says who in Colorado could propose an off-cycle map or by what route. The measure sets conditions on any plan change but does not create a way to start one. How that would work with the legislature's or voters' powers, and whether a citizen initiative could still change maps by a vote of the people, is a legal question we have not answered.

Who wrote it and how it reached the ballot

The Blue Book labels this measure as placed on the ballot by citizen initiative. It says the text of the measure "was drafted by the proponents of the initiative," and that the proponents "gathered the required amount of petition signatures." S1

What we do not have: the proponents' names, the filing and Title Board hearing record, earlier drafts of the text, and the signature totals. None of these is in the sources we saved, so this page does not name anyone. Where to get them: the Secretary of State's initiative file for this measure and its Title Board record, and the Secretary of State's list of organizations supporting and opposing each 2026 measure, which the Blue Book gives at coloradosos.gov/pubs/elections/Initiatives/ballot/contacts/2026.html. The arguments on each side

The Blue Book prints one argument for and one against. They are summarized here, not endorsed. The Secretary of State lists the organizations supporting and opposing each measure. S1 Our voter guide page lists who is paying for each side: Amendment 86 in the Voter Guide.

Arguments in the Blue Book
ForAgainst
It protects public participation in any off-cycle changes and ensures review through a fair, transparent process. Requiring commission review and barring districts that favor a single party keeps partisan politics out of the process and affirms the voters' intent in creating the commission, which supporters say voters "overwhelmingly approved" in 2018. They say politicians elsewhere have tried to change districts in their favor with limited public input.If voters ever want new districts in an off-cycle year, the measure stands in their way, whether the cause is partisan redistricting in other states, changed political priorities or other concerns. Opponents call it an unnecessary constitutional barrier. They also say that if voters feel the commission's districts no longer meet the state's needs, the same commission should not be part of the process, and voters should always have the final word.

The word "overwhelmingly" is the Blue Book's wording of the supporters' argument. We did not look up the 2018 vote total for this page.

What it would cost

The Blue Book says the measure increases costs in the Legislative Department by about $370,000 in any year the commission must convene and review changes to congressional maps outside its usual schedule. The costs are for staffing the commission, reimbursing commissioners and hiring legal counsel. The Judicial Department would have a small workload increase to review any new maps. Because the commission meets only if a change is proposed, the Blue Book's wording means no cost in a year with no proposal. S1

What research says

We have not yet gathered research for this study. Questions it could address include how independent commissions compare with legislatures on competitiveness and on how long map fights last, how often states have redrawn congressional maps between censuses and by what process, and what courts have held about mid-decade redistricting. We will not list a finding until we have read the study and can say who ran it, what place and period it covers, and what it cannot show.

What we do not yet know

We do not know whether any off-cycle change is being proposed in Colorado. We do not know how a court would read "the effect of dividing communities of interest" without the word "unnecessarily," or how the measure would interact with any other way of changing districts. We have not checked the Blue Book's count of other states.

Open work before this could be called a reviewed study: compare the reenacted sections 44 through 44.6 with the current constitution line by line; ask a lawyer who handles Colorado election law what route could start an off-cycle change; look up the 2018 Amendment Y vote from the Secretary of State; gather research on all sides, including on independent commissions; and get a reviewer.
