Florida Amendment 3: a bigger homestead exemption and a lower assessment cap

What Amendment 3 would change about homestead property tax and other property, why a judge ordered its ballot language rewritten, who sponsored it, how each chamber voted, and what each side says.

This page gives no recommendation for or against any measure. A sourced first draft: no reviewer has signed off, and each source says how much of it was read. Prepared October 31, 2026.

What the measure does

Sourcing note: the Florida Senate bill page and the enrolled resolution text were read in full through a tool that cannot quote long passages, so ballot wording below is shown only in short excerpts or described. Arguments come from news reports, attributed. We could not retrieve the House and Senate staff analyses or any official fiscal estimate.

Amendment 3 is a legislatively referred constitutional amendment on Florida's November 3, 2026 ballot. It is Committee Substitute for House Joint Resolution 1-F, passed in the legislature's June 2026 special session, originally titled "Save Our Homes from Excessive Property Taxes." The Senate page summarizes it as proposing to revise the limit on annual assessment increases for specified real property and to increase the homestead exemption. S1

From the enrolled resolution, as we read it, it amends Article VII, Sections 4, 6 and 9 and adds a schedule provision, taking effect January 1, 2027: S2

  • Homestead exemption (non-school taxes): raised to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028, with inflation adjustments starting in 2029. The school-tax exemption stays at $25,000. Counties and cities could extend exemptions up to the full assessed value by general law; special districts could by referendum.
  • Cap on other property: the annual limit on assessment increases for non-homestead real property and for residential property of nine units or fewer (non-school levies) would fall from 10 percent to 5 percent. The homestead cap (the lower of 3 percent or inflation) is unchanged.
  • New residents: people who had not kept a permanent Florida residence on December 31, 2026 would get a $50,000 non-school exemption, rising to the full homestead amount after five years. Local governments could shorten the wait by a two-thirds vote for a critical local need, starting in 2030.
  • Local spending: counties and cities could use property tax revenue only for listed purposes, including public safety, education, infrastructure, natural resources, debt service on existing obligations, retirement obligations and government operations, except what general law prohibits.

The text we read does not mark struck and added words, and the ballot summary in the original resolution described a full-elimination schedule that the operative text does not itself contain (the court case below turned partly on this kind of gap). We have not checked these descriptions against the official marked text.

What a vote means

A yes vote adds the changes above to the constitution; a no vote leaves the current exemption and cap rules. WFLX reports it needs at least 60 percent voter support to pass. S3

How the ballot language was rewritten

Three consolidated lawsuits challenged the legislature's ballot title and summary. WFLX reports that on August 3, 2026, Leon County Circuit Judge David Frank ruled them "clearly and conclusively defective," biased, inaccurate and misleading, and ordered them rewritten while leaving the amendment on the ballot. Plaintiffs it names include the nonprofit Save Our Voters From Misleading Ballot Language, several former mayors, former Republican state Senator Jeff Brandes and former Democratic U.S. Representative Al Lawson Jr. The judge said the title read more like a slogan than a neutral description, that "Save Our Homes" could confuse voters because it is already the name of the existing 3 percent homestead cap, which the amendment does not change, and that the summary overstated effects (for example, saying $250,000 when the 2027 figure is $150,000). He wrote that the court was ruling on fairness and accuracy, not on whether the policy is good. S3

Attorney General James Uthmeier rewrote the title and summary. Central Florida Public Media reports the new title as "Increased Homed Exemption; Lower Cap on Increases in Non-Homestead Property Assessments" (the article's spelling, which we have not checked against the official ballot) and says Uthmeier said Florida law lets him revise only the title and summary, not the amendment, and criticized the amendment as passed. WJHG reports the new summary says the amendment "increases the homestead exemption, for all non - school taxes to $150,000 in 2027" and "reduces the annual cap on assessment increases for non-homestead properties from 10% to 5%," lists the spending purposes, and says the increased exemption for new residents begins in the fifth year "to the extent permitted by the U.S. Constitution." WJHG reports no appeal as of August 14. We have not read the official final ballot text. S5 S6

Who wrote it and how the legislature voted

  • Sponsors: the House State Affairs Committee and Representative Overdorf. S1
  • House, June 2, 2026 (special session): five floor amendments failed; committee substitute passed, 75 yeas and 26 nays. S1
  • Senate, June 2, 2026: substituted for CS/SJR 2-F and passed, 30 yeas and 9 nays. S1

Counts are from the Senate bill page. We have not read the roll call pages.

Arguments

No official statement of arguments is saved. These are news-reported statements, attributed, and we have not checked them. S4 S3

  • Supporters. WLRN quotes Florida Realtors CEO Margy Grant: the amendment is "ultimately about giving Florida homeowners actual meaningful tax relief." Governor Ron DeSantis backs it, WLRN says, without a quote in that article. Florida Realtors has put $18 million into the Vote Yes on 3 committee, which WLRN says has covered all of the campaign's funding so far. S4
  • Opponents. WLRN quotes Polk County Sheriff Grady Judd calling it "a train wreck" and saying "Amendment 3 doesn't protect law enforcement, firefighters or emergency services, it defunds them." It names sheriffs, police chiefs and fire chiefs (through state associations), the environmental groups 1000 Friends of Florida and the Sierra Club, and economic development councils as opponents. Critics say the cuts would reduce local spending by "billions of dollars" in the first five years; WLRN gives no figure or source for that. S4

What it would cost and save

We have not read an official fiscal estimate. Estimates in news reports: Florida Realtors' CEO says the average homeowner would save about $1,000 a year in 2027 and $1,800 in 2028. Florida TaxWatch, a nonprofit research group (not an official state body), estimates savings of, for example, $1,113 in 2027 and $2,242 in 2028 for Orlando, with ClickOrlando noting it does not explain how the figures were derived. The size of the loss to local governments is claimed as "billions" by critics and is not given in a figure we could source. S4 S7
