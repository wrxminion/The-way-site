Florida Amendment 2: farm equipment exempt from property tax

What Amendment 2 would exempt from local property tax, who sponsored it, how each chamber voted, what each side says, and what is still unknown.

What the measure does

Sourcing note: the Florida Senate bill page and the enrolled resolution text were read in full through a tool that cannot quote long passages, so ballot wording below is shown only in short excerpts or described. Arguments come from news reports, attributed. We could not retrieve the House and Senate staff analyses or any official fiscal estimate.

Amendment 2 is a legislatively referred constitutional amendment on Florida's November 3, 2026 ballot. It is Committee Substitute for House Joint Resolution 1215 of the 2025 session, "Ad Valorem Tax Exemption." The Senate's summary says it proposes to exempt certain tangible personal property from ad valorem (property) taxation. S1

From the enrolled resolution, as we read it: it adds a new subsection (h) to Article VII, Section 3 exempting tangible personal property (business belongings you can touch that are not land or buildings, such as tractors and tools) that is habitually located or typically present on agricultural land, is used in producing agricultural products or for agritourism, and is owned by the landowner or the person leasing the land. The legislature would set conditions and definitions by general law. A schedule provision says it first applies to assessments for tax years beginning January 1, 2027. S2

What a vote means

The ballot title is "EXEMPTION OF TANGIBLE PERSONAL PROPERTY ON AGRICULTURAL LAND FROM TAXATION," and the ballot summary describes the exemption above. A yes vote adds it to the constitution; a no vote leaves such property subject to local property tax. Our Florida guide page lists it as needing at least 60 percent of the vote; we have not rechecked that against the Division of Elections. S2

Who wrote it and how the legislature voted

  • Sponsors: the House State Affairs Committee and Representative Danny Alvarez, with co-introducers Barnaby, Chamberlin, Fabricio, J. Lopez, Maggard, Owen, Salzman and Yarkosky. S1
  • Senate companion: Senate Bill 318 was identical and died on the calendar on June 16, 2025 after the House version passed. Floridian Press reports that Senator Jay Collins and Representative Alvarez filed the measure with Agriculture Commissioner Wilton Simpson; the Senate page does not name the Senate sponsor. S1 S3
  • House, April 25, 2025: committee substitute read a second and third time and passed, 110 yeas and 1 nay. S1
  • Senate, May 2, 2025: withdrawn from Appropriations and passed, 37 yeas and 0 nays. S1
  • Signed by the presiding officers and filed with the Secretary of State on June 18, 2025. S1

Counts are from the Senate bill page. We have not read the roll call pages.

Arguments

No official statement of arguments is saved. These are news-reported statements, attributed, and we have not checked them. S3 S4

  • Supporters. Agriculture Commissioner Wilton Simpson called the amendment "a pivotal step towards securing the permanent future of agriculture in Florida" (January 2024 press release) and said "Food security is a national security issue." Representative Alvarez said "The tax we are eliminating is flat out wrong," and told News 13 that people have already paid for the equipment before the tax applies. S3 S4
  • Opponents. Representative Anna Eskamani (D-Orlando) cast the one "no" vote in the House. She said the measure "would have a $30 million fiscal impact for local governments, with no guardrails to prevent windfalls," and "would also mostly benefit a handful of giant agribusinesses with multiple TPP accounts." She told News 13 "you'll see large actors tap into this tax break multiple times." Floridian Press says other opponents make the same two points but names none. S3 S4

What it would cost

News 13 reports that state economists estimate local governments could lose about $31 million a year if voters approve it. Floridian Press cites Representative Eskamani's figure of $30 million. We have not read the staff analyses or the economists' estimate itself, so we cannot say how the figure was built. S3 S4

What we do not yet know

We do not know how the legislature would define the conditions by general law, how much of the exemption would go to large versus small operations (the opponents' claim has not been tested in the sources we read), or the exact dollar effect by county.
