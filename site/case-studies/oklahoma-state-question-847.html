Oklahoma State Question 847: Property Valuation Caps and the Senior Freeze

What State Question 847 would change about how fast property values can rise for tax purposes and about the senior freeze, who wrote it, how each chamber voted, how the ballot title was rewritten, and what is still unknown.

This page gives no recommendation for or against any measure. A sourced first draft: no reviewer has signed off, and each source says how much of it was read. Prepared October 31, 2026.

What the measure does

State Question 847 (Legislative Referendum 381) is a constitutional amendment the Oklahoma Legislature referred to voters on the November 3, 2026 ballot. It amends Article X, Sections 8B and 8C of the Oklahoma Constitution. Section 8B limits how much the taxable "fair cash value" of real property can rise each year. Section 8C is the "senior freeze." S1 S2

The Attorney General's final ballot title says that, for tax year 2027 and after, the annual limit would be 4% for most real property (now 5%) and 1.75% for homesteads and agricultural land (now 3%). S1

For homeowners age 65 or older, the final title says Section 8C currently prohibits any increase in the fair cash value of a qualifying homestead if income does not exceed certain limits. The measure would eliminate the income cap and tie the annual limit to the prior year's gross household income compared with the median income for the county or metropolitan area, as estimated by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development. At or below the median, no increase. Over 100% up to 120%, 0.35%. Over 120% up to 140%, 0.7%. Over 140% up to 160%, 1.05%. Over 160% up to 180%, 1.4%. Over 180%, 1.75%. S1

The final title says the measure "would have a fiscal impact on the state, but the exact impact is unknown." S1

What a vote means

The ballot reads "Shall the proposal be approved?" with "For the proposal - YES" and "Against the proposal - NO." A yes vote adopts the changes above, beginning with tax year 2027. A no vote leaves the current limits in place. The measure changes limits on how fast taxable value can grow. It does not set tax rates, so what happens to any one tax bill also depends on local assessments, rates and levies. S1 S6 S7

How the ballot title was written

The resolution as passed carried a ballot title written by the legislature. The saved Attorney General letters show that on May 8, 2026 he found it did not comply with state law because it did not explain the income measurement drawn from federal law, did not explain that the income cap is eliminated, and did not state the fiscal impact. He then sent a preliminary title on May 22, 2026 and a final title letter dated June 3, 2026. The Secretary of State certified the final title to the State Election Board and the Governor on June 4, 2026. S1

This is the same rewrite process described in our State Question 845 study. We have not compared the preliminary and final titles line by line for 847. S1

Who wrote it and how the legislature voted

The measure is Senate Joint Resolution 39 of the 2025-2026 legislature. S2 S3

  • Authors: Senator Paxton is the Senate author, with Senators Burns and McIntosh. Representative Kyle Hilbert, who is now Speaker of the House, is the principal House author, with Representatives Wilk, Eaves, Townley, Harris, Norwood, Boles, Manger, Roberts, Hildebrant, Chapman, Wolfley, Grego and Hardin. S3 S6
  • Senate Rules Committee, March 4, 2026: "do pass as amended by committee substitute," 16 ayes and 2 nays. Nays: Boren, Hicks. S5
  • Senate passage, March 26, 2026: passed 38 to 8. A vote on advancing to third reading was 32 to 13. S3
  • House Rules Committee, April 6, 2026: "do pass," 10 yeas and 2 nays. Nays: Fugate, Hefner. S4
  • House passage, April 15, 2026: amended by a floor substitute and passed 85 to 9. A vote on the special election provision was 80 to 14. S3
  • Senate final vote, April 27, 2026: House amendments adopted 27 to 19; measure passed 40 to 8. The bill history also records "Special Election failed" at 26 to 21. S3
  • Filed with the Secretary of State April 28, 2026, and designated State Question 847 on May 1, 2026. S1 S3

These counts are from the legislature's bill history page and the committee reports. The roll call pages (each member's vote) are not saved. We do not interpret the "Special Election failed" entries. The question is on the November 3 general election ballot. S7

Arguments

We have no official statement of arguments saved for SQ 847. The only descriptions saved are a news explainer. News 9 reports the following, and we repeat it as their report of what each side says, not as our finding. S6

  • Supporters, including lawmakers who backed the measure, say lower valuation caps protect property owners as values rise, and that the senior provisions extend protection to older homeowners who earn more than the current freeze allows. Speaker Hilbert is quoted as saying "Seniors often live on a fixed income, so tax relief for them ensures they are not priced out of their homes," and that the tiered freeze "does not disincentivize work." The Oklahoma Farm Bureau supports the measure and has said lower caps could add protection for agricultural owners.
  • Opponents, including the Oklahoma Policy Institute and some Democrats, say the measure could slow the growth of property-tax revenue for local governments and schools, could affect their ability to finance existing obligations, and could shift some burden toward newer owners when capped property is transferred. Representative Ellen Pogemiller (D-88) is quoted: "Lower caps will mean slower growth. Which means less revenue for schools, CareerTechs, and roads."

News 9 notes these are arguments of the two sides and not conclusions in the official ballot language. We have not checked the factual claims on either side. Sources to add: floor debate on SJR 39, statements from county assessors and the Oklahoma Tax Commission, and the Oklahoma Policy Institute's own analysis.

What it would cost

The Attorney General's final title says the measure would have a fiscal impact on the state but that the exact impact is unknown. News 9 reports that the Oklahoma Policy Institute says the effect is hard to model because county assessor offices do not collect all the age and income information needed to project the senior provisions. The Senate bill page lists fiscal impact statements for SJR 39, but they are not saved, so we have not read them. S1 S3 S6

What research says

We have not yet gathered research for this study. Questions it could address include how assessment limits have worked in other states, who benefits from them, and how they affect local revenue. We will not list a finding until we have read the study and can say who ran it, what place and period it covers, and what it cannot show.

What we do not yet know

We do not know the dollar effect on state or local budgets, how many Oklahoma homeowners are 65 or older and in each income tier, or how county assessors would collect the income information. We have not read the enrolled text of the resolution against the current Sections 8B and 8C line by line, and we have not compared the preliminary and final ballot titles.

Open work before this could be called a reviewed study: get the Senate fiscal impact statements; read the roll call pages and floor debate; get the current text of Article X Sections 8B and 8C; gather named arguments from both sides directly; and get a reviewer who knows Oklahoma law.
