Proposition 135: surgery on minors and sex or gender

What Proposition 135 would prohibit and stop insurance and public funds from paying for, what is known about how often these surgeries happen, what Colorado law and a new federal rule say, what the official arguments say on each side, and what is still unknown.

This page gives no recommendation for or against any measure. A sourced first draft: no reviewer has signed off, and each source says how much of it was read. Prepared October 31, 2026.

What the measure does

Proposition 135 is a citizen initiative that changes Colorado statutes. It needs a majority vote to pass. Its short title in the text is the "Protect Children From Irreversible Sex Change Surgery Act," and it would take effect January 1, 2027, applying to violations on or after that date. S1

The text adds section 12-30-125 to the statutes. A health-care professional or other person "shall not knowingly perform, prescribe, administer, or provide any surgery to a minor for the purpose of altering biological sex characteristics." No state or federal funds, Medicaid reimbursements or insurance coverage may be used to pay for the prohibited surgeries. A minor is anyone under 18. The definition of "health-care professional" covers people licensed in Colorado or any other state in medicine, chiropractic, nursing, physical therapy, podiatry, dentistry, pharmacy, optometry, psychiatry, psychology, mental health therapy or other healing arts. S1

What a yes or no vote means

Official wording, Blue Book
VoteWhat the Blue Book says
Yes"prohibits surgeries on minors that would alter their biological sex characteristics as a treatment in response to a minor's perception of sex or gender, and prohibits the use of government funds or health insurance to pay for the surgeries."
No"maintains Colorado law, which protects access to surgery altering minors' biological sex characteristics and allows public and private health insurance programs to pay for these surgeries."

What is and is not covered

The text defines "altering biological sex characteristics" as "treatment in response to a minor's perception of sex or gender." It does not include treatment for people born with a medically verifiable disorder of sex development, treatment for acquired physical or chemical abnormalities, or male circumcision. S1

The Blue Book adds that the measure lists no specific procedures; the ban depends on the reason a surgery is performed. It says the ban includes gender-affirming surgeries, a range of procedures that may be provided to transgender people to match their physical appearance to their gender identity, such as altering, removing or reconstructing sexual and reproductive organs, modifying body shape, changing facial appearance or altering vocal characteristics. It also says the measure "may also limit other surgeries for minors, including non-transgender minors, if the treatment is interpreted as being in response to the minor's sex or gender perception." Non-surgical gender-related treatments, such as social transition and counseling, are not restricted by the measure. S1

The text does not mention medicines such as puberty blockers or hormones; it addresses surgery only. The Blue Book likewise discusses only surgery.

Law and coverage today

The Blue Book says that in Colorado today, medically necessary gender- and sex-based surgeries may be covered by private health insurance or a public health program, or paid for out of pocket. S1 In 2025 the legislature passed HB25-1309, "Protect Access to Gender-Affirming Health Care," signed May 23, 2025. According to its official summary as enacted, it requires health benefit plans to cover gender-affirming health care, defined broadly as supplies, care and services relating to the treatment of gender dysphoria, and bars plans from denying or limiting medically necessary gender-affirming care as determined by the provider. Its prime sponsors were Representatives Kyle Brown and Brianna Titone and Senators Lisa Cutter and Julie Gonzales. The bill page shows the Senate third reading vote as 23 yes and 12 no and the House third reading as 40 yes, 20 no and 5 not voting. S2

The HB25-1309 vote table has several rows for the House (third reading, a "previous question" motion, and later concurrence and repass votes). We report only the third-reading rows labeled as the bill. We have not read the roll calls. The act's coverage rules cover care broadly, not only surgery, and we have not checked how it applies to minors.

A new federal rule also bears on funding. A law firm's summary (Groom Law Group) reports that on August 11, 2026 the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services finalized a rule limiting federal Medicaid funding for people under 18, and federal CHIP funding for people under 19, for certain gender-affirming medical interventions for minors, which CMS calls "sex-rejecting procedures." The rule takes effect October 13, 2026, allows up to six months of continued federal funding for children already receiving hormone therapy, leaves mental health coverage in place, and does not bar states from using state-only funds. S3 We have not read the rule itself, and the summary does not say which interventions it lists, so we do not say whether it covers surgery. The summary also notes legal challenges could delay implementation.

How often it happens

The Blue Book says it is unknown how many minors in Colorado or the United States receive the surgeries the measure would prohibit. It cites one study from Harvard University researchers using 2019 health insurance claims: an estimated 2.1 youths per 100,000 aged 15 to 17 who identified as transgender or gender diverse had at least one gender-affirming surgery; 0.1 per 100,000 (one in a million) among those aged 13 to 14; and no cases at age 12 and younger. It says the three large pediatric surgical providers in Colorado, Children's Hospital Colorado, Rocky Mountain Children's and Denver Health, report that they do not perform gender- or sex-based surgeries on transgender patients under 18. S1 We did not locate or read the study itself.

The arguments on each side

The Blue Book prints two arguments for and two against. They are summarized here, not endorsed. S1 Our voter guide page lists who is paying for each side: Proposition 135 in the Voter Guide.

Arguments in the Blue Book
ForAgainst
Minors lack the maturity to make permanent, life-altering medical decisions. Supporters compare it to other age-restricted rights and say surgery should require a heightened level of maturity and informed decision-making, with less invasive, non-surgical care used until a person is an adult.The measure is an overly broad ban that interferes with needed care for transgender and non-transgender minors. Opponents say the narrow exceptions could limit surgeries for a range of reasons, medical and cosmetic, that a ban based on a patient's purpose creates uncertainty for patients, doctors and insurers, and that insurers and doctors might pull back for fear of legal consequences.
More long-term data is needed to know whether gender-affirming surgery is a necessary option before adulthood. Supporters say some minors may regret irreversible procedures, that surgery carries risks, and that other states have limited gender-related treatment options for minors.The measure delays medically recommended care even when parents, patients and providers agree it is needed. Opponents say care should be individualized under established standards, that transgender youth already face stigma, that restricting care may worsen mental health outcomes including anxiety, depression, isolation, eating disorders and risk of self-harm, and that medical decisions should be left to patients, families and providers, "not legislated at the ballot box."

The claims about regret, long-term outcomes and mental health effects are the sides' assertions in the Blue Book's wording. We did not test them against research for this draft. The text's own findings also claim that several European countries "have halted surgery"; we did not check that claim.

What it would cost

The Blue Book says the measure would minimally increase workload and cost for the Department of Regulatory Agencies, which regulates health care professionals and insurers, to update rules and handle complaints. Other state and local agencies that employ health care professionals, such as the Department of Human Services and county hospitals, would have added workload to update policies, and legal costs may rise to respond to allegations of noncompliance. Assuming compliance, the impact is expected to be minimal. State spending on health programs and employee insurance might decrease slightly to the extent Medicaid covers these surgeries for minors now, and private coverage changes might minimally lower premiums for state and local governments. Any savings are expected to be minimal. S1

What research says

We have not yet gathered research for this study beyond the one claims-data study the Blue Book cites. Questions research could address include how often these surgeries occur in minors, what is known about satisfaction and regret over time, how outcomes compare with other treatments, and what the systematic reviews and national medical groups conclude. These are contested areas, with different groups reading the evidence differently; we will not list a finding until we have read the study and can say who ran it, what place and period it covers, and what it cannot show.

What we do not yet know

We do not know how many minors in Colorado would be affected, since the Blue Book says the number is unknown. We do not know how a court or insurers would read the "in response to a minor's perception of sex or gender" test for surgeries other than gender-affirming ones. We do not know how the federal rule's final text and any court challenge will affect Medicaid coverage in Colorado, or whether it covers surgery.

Open work before this could be called a reviewed study: get the Title Board record and proponent names from the Secretary of State; read the CMS final rule in the Federal Register; read the Harvard claims study; read the HB25-1309 roll calls and the signed act; gather research on all sides, including systematic reviews and statements by medical groups; and get a reviewer who knows pediatric medicine and health law.
