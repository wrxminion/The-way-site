Proposition 137: sporting goods sales tax for conservation

What Proposition 137 would do with existing sales tax on sporting goods, how it changes TABOR refunds, what the official arguments say on each side, and what is still unknown.

This page gives no recommendation for or against any measure. A sourced first draft: no reviewer has signed off, and each source says how much of it was read. Prepared October 31, 2026.

What the measure does

Sourcing note: this page so far rests on the Colorado Blue Book alone. We have not yet read the Title Board record, the proponents' filings, or any outside reporting.

Proposition 137 is a citizen initiative that changes Colorado statutes. It needs a majority vote to pass. The text adds section 24-77-110, which has the people of Colorado find that more conservation funding is needed without raising taxes, and, through a voter-approved revenue change, lets the state keep and spend part of its sales tax revenue from sporting goods and equipment for conservation, wildfire risk reduction and outdoor recreation. The official ballot title also says the amount can be reduced if needed to preserve funding for certain tax credits. S1

The Blue Book says the measure does not change the price of sporting goods or the amount of tax collected. Sporting goods include bicycles, golf equipment, hunting and fishing equipment, camping equipment, boats, recreational vehicles and campers, and rentals of recreational, sports and fitness equipment. The amount directed would be calculated by Legislative Council Staff. S1

What a yes or no vote means

Official wording, Blue Book
VoteWhat the Blue Book says
Yes"directs existing sales tax revenue collected from sales of sporting goods to be spent on certain conservation, wildfire risk mitigation, and outdoor recreation programs, and exempts it from state revenue limitations."
No"keeps the sales tax revenue collected from sporting goods in the state's general budget and subject to the state's revenue limit, with the revenue either spent on priorities decided annually by the legislature or refunded to taxpayers when required."

Where the money would go

The Blue Book lists these uses: conservation programs for parks, open spaces and wildlife; wildfire risk mitigation, forest management and restoration, and a fund for damages from prescribed fires; watershed restoration; outdoor education and recreation; and outdoor access and equity improvements. It estimates about $175 million for state budget year 2027-28, in addition to roughly $114 million those efforts now receive. S1

  • Great Outdoors Colorado (GOCO), 47.5 percent, about $83.1 million. GOCO is set up in the state constitution, is funded by the lottery at about $90 million a year, and currently receives no state tax funding. S1
  • Wildfire risk mitigation, forest management and watershed restoration, 47.5 percent, about $83.1 million, including a one-time $10.0 million in 2027-28 for damages from prescribed fires. These efforts now receive about $16.4 million a year for wildfire and forest work and about $5 million for watershed and post-wildfire flood projects. S1
  • Outdoor recreation, economic development and equity programs, 5 percent, about $8.7 million, now funded at about $800,000 a year. S1

The Blue Book's spending table splits the money among GOCO (up to $83.1 million), the Colorado State Forest Service, the Department of Natural Resources, the Department of Public Safety ($10.0 million in 2027-28 only), Colorado Parks and Wildlife and the Office of Economic Development and International Trade, up to $175.0 million in 2027-28 and $180.2 million in 2028-29. In some years the full amount would not be directed, which would happen if exempting the revenue from the limit would reduce two state tax credits for low- and moderate-income households. S1

TABOR refunds and other measures

The Blue Book explains that TABOR limits state revenue to an amount adjusted for inflation and population growth, and revenue over the limit is refunded unless voters approve keeping it. Under this measure the directed sales tax revenue would be exempt from the limit. In years when revenue is over the limit, it reduces TABOR refunds by that amount; the Blue Book says it expects refunds to fall by $175.0 million in tax year 2027, based on June 2026 forecasts, and expects it to eliminate the six-tier sales tax TABOR refund in at least 2027-28, by between $26 and $83 for a single filer and $52 and $166 for joint filers, depending on income. In years when revenue is below the limit, refunds are not affected but less money is available for other priorities. S1

The Blue Book also says that if Proposition NN passes too, Proposition 137 would reduce the money the state actually keeps and spends under NN, because 137 reduces the revenue counted under the limit. See our study of Proposition NN. S1

Who wrote it and how it reached the ballot

The Blue Book labels this measure as placed on the ballot by citizen initiative. It says the text of the measure "was drafted by the proponents of the initiative," and that the proponents "gathered the required amount of petition signatures." S1

What we do not have: the proponents' names, the filing and Title Board hearing record, earlier drafts of the text, and the signature totals. None of these is in the sources we saved, so this page does not name anyone. The arguments on each side

The Blue Book prints two arguments for and two against. They are summarized here, not endorsed. S1

Arguments in the Blue Book
ForAgainst
Without raising tax rates or changing the price of sporting goods, the measure directs needed money to recreation access, land protection, wildfire mitigation and watersheds. Supporters say competing budget priorities squeeze the state, federal support is unreliable, and the state has not kept up with needed investments. They note GOCO has supported conservation statewide for over 30 years.The measure is a tax increase in disguise because it reduces or eliminates future TABOR refunds. Opponents say keeping revenue under the limit is a critical taxpayer protection, that every dollar retained is a dollar less in refunds or in spending on priorities such as health care and education.
Colorado's outdoor spaces are under strain from visitors, drought and wildfire. Supporters say the measure is a user-based model: people who buy or rent sporting goods often use them outdoors, so those who benefit most from the outdoors help sustain it.The measure sends more money to organizations and government entities with specific interests and limited accountability to the legislature or voters. Opponents say many of these programs already have dedicated funding, and that legislators already have the power to spend on these needs and should keep flexibility to balance priorities.

These are the sides' claims in the Blue Book's wording. We have not checked them, including the dollar claims about existing funding or the "user-based" description.

What it would cost

The Blue Book says the measure moves an estimated $175.0 million in 2027-28, and $180.2 million in 2028-29 and later years, from the state General Fund to these programs, and reduces TABOR refunds in years when revenue exceeds the limit. In years when revenue is below the limit, it reduces state money available for other uses, with health care, education and human services the largest, though the exact effect depends on legislative budgeting. Some money may not be spent in the year received. S1

What we do not yet know

We do not know who proposed the measure, how the text changed, or which of the text's findings (for example the wildfire figures it states) hold up. We have not read the full text of section 24-77-110 beyond its opening findings, including how the reduction for tax credits is worked out.
