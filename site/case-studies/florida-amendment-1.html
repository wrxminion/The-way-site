Florida Amendment 1: a bigger budget stabilization fund

What Amendment 1 would change about the budget stabilization fund, who sponsored it, how each chamber voted, what each side says, and what is still unknown.

What the measure does

Sourcing note: the Florida Senate bill page and the enrolled resolution text were read in full through a tool that cannot quote long passages, so ballot wording below is shown only in short excerpts or described. Arguments come from news reports, attributed. We could not retrieve the House and Senate staff analyses or any official fiscal estimate.

Amendment 1 is a legislatively referred constitutional amendment on Florida's November 3, 2026 ballot. It is House Joint Resolution 5019 of the 2025 session, titled "Budget Stabilization Fund." The Senate's summary says it proposes to increase the amount of funds that may be retained in the fund, require an annual transfer to the fund if there is no revenue shortfall, and allow the legislature to withdraw funds periodically for critical state needs. S1

From the enrolled resolution, as we read it: the cap on the fund's balance would rise from 10 percent to 25 percent of general revenue collections. By June 30 each year the legislature would have to transfer the lesser of $750 million or the amount needed to bring the balance to 25 percent. The transfer could be skipped in specified cases: a withdrawal for a revenue shortfall or emergency, a withdrawal for a critical state need, or a critical state need that requires spending above the transfer amount. A critical-need withdrawal or skip takes a two-thirds vote in a separate bill, and the skip can happen no more than once every five years. The fund could not be drawn below 10 percent of revenue collections for a nonrecurring critical state need. S2

The text we read does not mark which words are struck and which are added, so the old and new wording could not be told apart. We have not checked these descriptions against the official marked text. WUSF reports the fund was last used in 2008, during the Great Recession. S3

What a vote means

The ballot title is "BUDGET STABILIZATION FUND" followed by the words "Proposing an amendment to the State Constitution to increase the amount of funds ...", and the ballot summary describes the changes above. A yes vote adds them to the constitution; a no vote leaves the current rules. WUSF reports it needs 60 percent of the vote to pass. S3

Who wrote it and how the legislature voted

  • Sponsor: Representative McClure filed the joint resolution; the Senate companion, Senate Bill 1908, was filed by Senator Hooper and was laid on the table once the House resolution passed. S1
  • House, June 16, 2025: read a second time, an amendment by McClure adopted, passed as amended, 100 yeas and 1 nay. S1
  • Senate, June 16, 2025: withdrawn from Appropriations and passed, 29 yeas and 4 nays. S1
  • Signed by the presiding officers and filed with the Secretary of State on June 17, 2025. S1

Counts are from the Senate bill page. We have not read the roll call pages.

Arguments

No official statement of arguments is saved. These are from one news report, News Service of Florida via WUSF, September 15, 2026, and we have not checked them. We have not found a second news report on the two sides yet. S3

  • Supporters. The Republican Party of Florida endorsed it, saying "This amendment will help strengthen Florida's rainy day fund" and arguing it would help the state handle storms and downturns without tax increases. Legislative leaders back it as a safeguard against a severe downturn that could force tax or fee increases. The report quotes then-House Speaker Daniel Perez in June 2025: "The state of Florida was not prepared for that recession."
  • Opponents. Governor Ron DeSantis called it a "foolish constitutional amendment proposal" in a post on X and recommends a no vote. The report says he argues the fund is already at its maximum and that extra revenue should go to tax cuts, local grants for homestead exemptions and removing tolls.

What it would cost

The amendment would require up to $750 million a year to be moved into the fund until it reaches 25 percent, which the legislature could skip in the cases above. We have not read a staff analysis or an official fiscal estimate of what that would mean for the rest of the budget; the House Budget Committee analysis is listed on the bill page but failed to load.
