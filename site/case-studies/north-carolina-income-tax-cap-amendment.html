North Carolina income tax cap amendment: a 3.5% constitutional limit

What the 3.5 percent cap would change, who sponsored it, how each chamber voted, what the official fiscal note says, what each side says, and what is still unknown.

This page gives no recommendation for or against any measure. A sourced first draft: no reviewer has signed off, and each source says how much of it was read. Prepared October 31, 2026.

What the measure does

Sourcing note: bill text, fiscal notes, sponsors and vote counts below come from the legislature's own pages, and the ballot wording is also checked against the State Board of Elections referendum list dated September 2, 2026. We have not read the Board's explanation of the amendments. Saved copies of these files are kept with our research.

This is a legislatively referred constitutional amendment on North Carolina's November 3, 2026 ballot. It is Senate Bill 1080 of the 2025-2026 session, short-titled "Lower Taxes for All NC," ratified as Session Law 2026-4. In the third edition of the bill, Article V, Section 2(6) says "The rate of tax on incomes shall not in any case exceed" a limit that the rendered text shows changed from "seven" to "three and one-half" percent, while the provision allowing personal exemptions and deductions so that only net incomes are taxed is unchanged. The legislature's Fiscal Research Division says the cap covers both personal and corporate income and would apply to taxable years beginning on or after January 1, 2027. S1 S2 S3

The Fiscal Research Division's note (May 19, 2026) explains that state law lowers the personal income tax rate by half a percentage point each time general fund revenue beats a set trigger. It says the May 2026 revenue forecast beats the triggers for 2025-26 and 2026-27, so the rate is projected to fall from 3.99 percent in 2026 to 3.49 percent in 2027 and 2.99 percent in 2028 and after, with one more possible half-point cut, to 2.49 percent, around 2033 if later triggers are hit. It adds that the corporate rate is already below the cap (2 percent in 2026 and 2027, 1 percent in 2028 and 2029, 0 percent in 2030 and after). Other reports give different paths: WSOC reports 3.24 percent in 2030 and 2.99 percent from 2033, and WUNC says the July budget drops the rate to 3.49 percent for 2027 through 2029. The note's table does not show those paths, it predates the July budget, and we have not read the budget law. The amendment sets a ceiling and does not itself change the rate. S3 S4 S5

What a vote means

The ballot question, word for word from the third edition of the bill: "Constitutional amendment to keep the State income tax rate from being raised higher than three and one-half percent (3.5%)," with boxes for FOR and AGAINST. The State Board of Elections list, dated September 2, 2026, shows the same wording under "Maximum income tax rate of 3.5%." A yes vote adds the 3.5 percent ceiling to the constitution; a no vote leaves the constitutional ceiling at 7 percent. News reports say it needs a simple majority of votes cast on it and that the governor has no veto over it. S2 S4 S5 S8

The Board's list has exactly three statewide questions for November 3, 2026: photo ID, this cap and the property tax limit. A 5 percent cap proposal (Senate Bill 920), which Ballotpedia describes on a separate page, is not on it. S8

Who wrote it and how the legislature voted

  • Primary sponsors: Senators Michael Lee, Benton Sawrey and Lisa Barnes, as the bill page and the fiscal note list them; many other senators are co-sponsors. S1 S3
  • Senate, May 19, 2026, second reading (roll call S-534) and third reading (S-535): 30 yes, 18 no, 1 excused absence. S1
  • House, May 20, 2026, second reading (H-663) and third reading (H-664): 73 yes, 46 no, 1 excused absence. S1
  • Three amendments to the bill were offered in the Senate on May 19: one was ruled out of order and two were tabled 30 to 18. We have not read them. S1
  • Carolina Public Press, in a report carried by WFAE, says the measure passed mostly along party lines and that Representatives Carla Cunningham and Nasif Majeed, both recently unaffiliated Mecklenburg lawmakers, supplied the votes Republicans needed. S6

Ballotpedia's news site reports the House vote as 71 to 46. The legislature's bill page shows 73 to 46 on both readings, and the saved roll call transcripts (H-663 and H-664) show 73 to 46 too, so we use that. We checked each total in the transcripts for H-663, H-664, S-534 and S-535 but have not gone through how individual members voted. S1 S7

Arguments

No official statement of arguments is saved. These are news-reported statements, attributed, and we have not checked them.

  • Supporters. WFAE quotes Senator Lisa Barnes: "We have seen the challenges created by past tax-and-spend policies," arguing for a firm fiscal limit. WUNC quotes Senator Michael Lee (R-Hanover) saying the measure reduces the tax burden on residents. WSOC reports supporters say it puts a lower limit on how much the state could tax income. S4 S5 S6
  • Opponents. WUNC quotes Representative Marcia Morey (D-Durham) warning that the cap would force cuts to services. WFAE reports Down Home North Carolina and the Carolina Federation oppose it; Carolina Federation co-director Jenn Frye is quoted objecting to "Locking these kinds of changes into our state Constitution," arguing it removes future lawmakers' power to adjust policy. WSOC reports opponents say it would limit future lawmakers' ability to adjust tax rates and state revenue. S4 S5 S6

What it would cost

The Fiscal Research Division's note estimates no fiscal impact for the state. It gives three reasons: the ballot outcome is unknown, the personal income tax rate is projected to fall below 3.5 percent anyway, and the corporate rate is already below the cap. It gives no dollar figure for local governments and says the extra ballot cost of a second referendum is minimal and could be absorbed by existing local boards of elections. It also says that if revenue in 2025-26 came in below the $33,042 million trigger, the 3.99 percent rate would apply in 2027 and the cap could then bind and require a reduction. WFAE says the same: no dollar estimate, and reversing the cap would take another amendment. S3 S6

What we do not yet know

We do not know the fiscal effect over time if revenue falls short of the triggers, which rate schedule the July budget law actually sets after 2027 (the fiscal note, WSOC and WUNC differ), or how the cap would interact with the corporate rate path.
