North Carolina income tax cap amendment: a 3.5% constitutional limit

What the 3.5 percent cap would change, who sponsored it, how each chamber voted, what each side says, and what is still unknown.

This page gives no recommendation for or against any measure. A sourced first draft: no reviewer has signed off, and each source says how much of it was read. Prepared October 31, 2026.

What the measure does

Sourcing note: the North Carolina legislature and State Board of Elections sites are blocked from where this draft was written. Sponsors and vote counts come from the legislature's bill pages as retrieved. We could not retrieve the bill PDF for this measure, so the exact ballot question wording is taken from news reports and is marked as such.

This is a legislatively referred constitutional amendment on North Carolina's November 3, 2026 ballot. It is Senate Bill 1080 of the 2025-2026 session, short-titled "Lower Taxes for All NC," ratified as Session Law 2026-4. News reports say it would lower the maximum income tax rate the constitution allows from 7 percent to 3.5 percent. Ballotpedia's news site reports the same 7 percent starting point. S1 S3 S4 S5

News reports say the actual rate is 3.99 percent for 2026 and that state law already schedules reductions: WSOC reports 3.49 percent in 2027, 3.24 percent in 2030 and 2.99 percent beginning in 2033, while WUNC says the July budget drops the rate to 3.49 percent for 2027 through 2029. The two reports differ after 2027, and we have not read the budget law. The amendment sets a ceiling and does not itself change the rate. S3 S4

A different Ballotpedia page describes a North Carolina amendment lowering the cap from 7 percent to 5 percent (Senate Bill 920, Senate vote December 2, 2024, 30 to 19). A bill tracker, Plural Policy, shows no House floor vote on it: after the Senate passed it, the House took it up on first reading December 9, 2024 and referred it to the Rules committee, and the page records nothing later. We could not confirm its final status, and we did not see the State Board of Elections list. We treat Senate Bill 1080 as the measure on the ballot because the legislature's bill page shows it chaptered as Session Law 2026-4 and WUNC quotes the 3.5 percent ballot wording. S2 S6

What a vote means

WUNC quotes the ballot wording as "Constitutional amendment to keep the State income tax rate from being raised higher than three and one-half percent (3.5%)." We did not find the wording on the legislature's pages. A yes vote adds the 3.5 percent ceiling to the constitution; a no vote leaves the constitutional ceiling at 7 percent. News reports say it needs a simple majority of votes cast on it and that the governor has no veto over it. S3 S4

Who wrote it and how the legislature voted

  • Primary sponsor on the bill page: Senator Barnes, with Senators Lee, Sawrey and many others listed as co-sponsors. S1
  • Senate, May 19, 2026, second and third readings: 30 yes, 18 no, 1 excused absence. S1
  • House, May 20, 2026, second and third readings: 73 yes, 46 no, 1 excused absence. S1
  • Three amendments to the bill were offered in the Senate on May 19: one was ruled out of order and two were tabled 30 to 18. We have not read them. S1

Ballotpedia's news site reports the House vote as 71 to 46 and says both chambers split along party lines. The legislature's own bill page shows 73 to 46, so we use that. We have not read the roll call pages. S1 S5

Arguments

No official statement of arguments is saved. These are news-reported statements, attributed, and we have not checked them.

  • Supporters. WUNC quotes Senator Michael Lee (R-Hanover) saying the measure reduces the tax burden on residents. WSOC reports supporters say it puts a lower limit on how much the state could tax income. S3 S4
  • Opponents. WUNC quotes Representative Marcia Morey (D-Durham) warning that the cap would force cuts to services. WSOC reports opponents say it would limit future lawmakers' ability to adjust tax rates and state revenue. S3 S4

What it would cost

We have not read a fiscal note for this amendment. Because the rate paid now (3.99 percent in 2026) and the scheduled 2027 rate (3.49 percent) are both under the proposed ceiling, the reports we read describe the cap as a limit on future increases, not a cut. How much revenue it could cost in later years depends on future rates, which no source we read estimates. S3 S4

What we do not yet know

We do not know the fiscal effect over time, which rate schedule the budget law actually sets after 2027 (the two reports differ), or what the constitution says today beyond the 7 percent figure as reported.
