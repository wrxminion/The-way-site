Proposition 132: stronger penalties for fentanyl crimes

What Proposition 132 would change about fentanyl sentences, how today's penalties are set, what the official arguments say on each side, what it would cost, and what is still unknown.

This page gives no recommendation for or against any measure. A sourced first draft: no reviewer has signed off, and each source says how much of it was read. Prepared October 31, 2026.

What the measure does

Proposition 132 is a citizen initiative that changes Colorado statutes. It needs a majority vote to pass. S1

The Blue Book says it increases penalties for possession and distribution of fentanyl and related synthetic opioids, including selling, transferring or sharing any amount, and eliminates the chance of reduced penalties for certain possession and distribution offenses that involve mitigating circumstances. It does this by expanding felony charges, imposing longer prison sentences and limiting judicial discretion. The text rewrites the distribution statute (18-18-405) and adds a new category, the "level 4 treatment-mandated drug felony," a level 4 drug felony that can be vacated after a person successfully completes court-ordered drug treatment. S1

What a yes or no vote means

Official wording, Blue Book
VoteWhat the Blue Book says
Yes"makes possession of any amount of fentanyl a felony, requires a mandatory minimum 8-year prison sentence for any quantity of fentanyl distribution, and eliminates options to reduce penalties for certain possession and distribution offenses."
No"keeps minor possession charges of fentanyl a misdemeanor, maintains tiered felony penalties for fentanyl distribution based on drug quantity, and keeps options to reduce penalties for certain possession and distribution offenses."

The official ballot title asks about "increasing the felony classifications of drug-related crimes for distribution, manufacturing, dispensing, sale, or possession of fentanyl and certain synthetic opioids; creating mandated treatment for certain drug felony violations based on possession amount; and changing sentencing provisions to narrow or eliminate exemptions." Penalties today

The Blue Book describes fentanyl as a potent synthetic opioid used medically for pain relief, illegal to possess or distribute without a prescription, and the leading cause of drug overdose deaths in both Colorado and the United States because it can be fatal in very small amounts and is often mixed into other illicit drugs without the user's knowledge. It says the measure also covers a class of related synthetic opioids. S1

Penalties now depend on drug type, quantity and whether the offense was for personal use or distribution. The weight counts the whole substance, meaning any mixture containing fentanyl, not just the fentanyl in it. A mandatory prison sentence of 8 to 32 years applies to people who distributed to a minor, distributed more than 50 grams of a substance, or distributed at least 4 grams of a substance that caused a death. State prison is prohibited for people who possessed under 1 gram with no intent to distribute and no multiple past fentanyl convictions; possessed 1 to 4 grams with no intent to distribute and showed evidence they did not know the substance contained fentanyl; or distributed under 4 grams and qualified for "Good Samaritan" protections for reporting an overdose and staying until help arrived. In other cases a judge sets a sentence within a range and may consider the circumstances, including whether the person knew fentanyl was present, criminal history, risk to public safety and acceptance of responsibility, and may reduce a penalty for completing treatment. The Blue Book adds that possession of any amount becomes a felony, but a person with under one gram who completes court-ordered drug treatment may have the felony reduced to a misdemeanor, while people charged with felony possession of more than one gram lose that option. Courts must order treatment for anyone charged with possessing under one gram, regardless of the circumstances. Judges could not consider mitigating factors such as a reasonable belief that the substance did not contain fentanyl. The measure keeps "Good Samaritan" reductions for other low-level possession offenses but removes them for distribution. S1

Earlier drug laws in the record

Two earlier Colorado laws on drug penalties are saved with this study. The Blue Book does not name them, and we have not verified how they relate to the current fentanyl penalties it describes, so they are listed here only as background.

  • HB19-1263, signed May 28, 2019, prime sponsors Representatives Leslie Herod and Shane Sandridge and Senators Vicki Marble and Pete Lee. According to its official summary, it made possession of 4 grams or less of a schedule I or II controlled substance a level 1 drug misdemeanor, with felony charges for a fourth or later offense. S2
  • HB22-1326, signed May 25, 2022, prime sponsors Representative Alec Garnett and Senators Brittany Pettersen and John Cooke. Its title says it addresses synthetic opiates and changes the criminal penalties for them, and it also covers treatment, opiate antagonists, detection tests and a prevention campaign. S3

We read the bill pages. For HB22-1326 the saved page gave the title, sponsors and signing date but not an enacted-bill summary, so we do not say what its penalty changes were.

Who wrote it and how it reached the ballot

The Blue Book labels this measure as placed on the ballot by citizen initiative. It says the text "was drafted by the proponents of the initiative," and that the proponents "gathered the required amount of petition signatures." S1

What we do not have: the proponents' names, the filing and Title Board hearing record, earlier drafts of the text, and the signature totals. None of these is in the sources we saved, so this page does not name anyone. The arguments on each side

The Blue Book prints one argument for and one against. They are summarized here, not endorsed. S1 Our voter guide page lists who is paying for each side: Proposition 132 in the Voter Guide.

Arguments in the Blue Book
ForAgainst
Fentanyl is highly potent and deadly and warrants stronger penalties than other drugs. The measure targets traffickers with harsher sentences and disrupts distribution, including when fentanyl is mixed into other substances. Supporters say mandated prison sentences for dealers are likely to enhance public safety by keeping them off the street and give victims' families a greater sense of justice, and that greater accountability for users may deter use and help reduce overdose deaths.The measure is unlikely to reduce fentanyl use, lower crime or prevent overdose deaths. Opponents say people may be discouraged from seeking addiction treatment or calling for help in an overdose for fear of prosecution and prison, that it treats people who distribute trace amounts as major dealers, and that longer sentences are expensive and shift resources from underfunded treatment, education and other services to an overcrowded, understaffed prison system.

Both sides' claims about what the measure would do to use, crime and overdose deaths are predictions in the Blue Book's wording. We did not test them against research for this draft.

What it would cost

The Blue Book says the measure raises state revenue to the Judicial Department by $23,000 in the first year and $73,000 in the second and later years from additional criminal fines. It raises state spending by $0.9 million in the first budget year for courts and public defenders, an estimated $8.9 million in the second year as more people go to prison, and an estimated $68.2 million by year five, with prison costs continuing to rise as people serve longer terms. The state may need to build or contract for more prison space. The measure names no funding source, so the Blue Book assumes the General Fund. County-funded district attorneys would also have higher costs to prosecute longer cases. S1

What research says

We have not yet gathered research for this study. Questions it could address include whether mandatory minimum sentences deter drug distribution or use, how overdose-reporting protections ("Good Samaritan" laws) affect calls for help, how fentanyl penalties in other states have affected overdose deaths and prison populations, and what share of people charged with fentanyl distribution are large-scale traffickers versus people who shared a small amount. We will not list a finding until we have read the study and can say who ran it, what place and period it covers, and what it cannot show.

What we do not yet know

We do not know how many people each year would face the new sentences, which depends on charging and plea decisions that no saved source measures. We do not know how the measure's text would treat each situation in the Blue Book's table beyond the table itself, because we compared the Blue Book's summary only and did not read the full statutory text line by line. We have not confirmed which earlier law set the current penalties.

Open work before this could be called a reviewed study: read the full text of the measure against current statute; get the Title Board record and proponent names from the Secretary of State; find the law that set today's fentanyl tiers; gather research on all sides; and get a reviewer who knows Colorado criminal sentencing.
