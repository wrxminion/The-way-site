Proposition 134: male and female school sports teams

What Proposition 134 would require of school and college sports teams, how state law, athletic associations and the courts handle eligibility today, what the official arguments say on each side, and what is still unknown.

This page gives no recommendation for or against any measure. A sourced first draft: no reviewer has signed off, and each source says how much of it was read. Prepared October 31, 2026.

What the measure does

Proposition 134 is a citizen initiative that changes Colorado statutes. It needs a majority vote to pass. Its short title in the text is the "Protect Women and Girls Sports Act," and it would take effect January 1, 2027. S1

The text adds a new article 60 to title 25. Every interscholastic or intramural team or sport sponsored by a school or athletic association must be expressly designated as for males, for females, or coeducational (mixed). A female-designated team "shall not be open to a male student or participant." A male-designated team is not open to a female unless no female team is offered or available for that sport for her. Coeducational teams are open to everyone. Government entities, licensing or accrediting bodies and athletic associations may not "entertain a complaint, open an investigation, or take any other adverse action" against a school for keeping a separate team or sport for females. The governing body of each school must adopt a policy to carry this out. S1

What a yes or no vote means

Official wording, Blue Book
VoteWhat the Blue Book says
Yes"means that all school-based athletic teams and sports must be designated as being for biological males or biological females only, or as mixed, and requires schools and school districts to adopt policies on sex eligibility for sports."
No"means that individual schools, school districts, and athletic associations may continue to set their own policies in alignment with current state and federal law about who is eligible to participate on a team or in a sport."

Who and what it covers

The text defines "female" as a person whose biological reproductive system is organized around the production of ova, and "male" as one organized around the production of sperm. The Blue Book notes the measure imposes no specific method for determining or showing biological sex, so each school or district sets its own verification procedures. S1

It applies to teams and sports organized by schools or athletic associations, including teams that compete against other schools, in-house intramurals and school-sponsored after-school programs. "School" includes public, charter, private and religious schools from elementary through high school, and public or private colleges, universities and community colleges. It does not apply to youth sports and leagues that run separately from schools. S1

For public K-12 schools, if the Commissioner of Education decides a district has intentionally refused to comply, the commissioner must notify the district, allow a reasonable time, and then, if the district has made no good-faith attempt to comply, take appropriate remedial action. The Blue Book says the measure sets up no enforcement mechanism for private K-12 schools, colleges and universities or athletic associations. S1 The text also says that people born with a diagnosis of a disorder or difference in sex development must receive all protections and accommodations afforded under federal law. S1

State and federal law

The Blue Book says the Colorado Anti-Discrimination Act (CADA) prohibits discrimination in employment, housing and public accommodations based on sex, sexual orientation, gender identity and gender expression, and that public K-12 schools, colleges and universities may not exclude students from extracurricular activities that align with their gender identity. It says it is unclear how the measure would affect current state law. S1 The saved record includes HB21-1108, signed in 2021, whose official summary says it added "gender expression" and "gender identity" to many statutes against discrimination, including those on public accommodations and on enrollment in public and charter schools. Its prime sponsors were Representative Daneya Esgar and Senator Dominick Moreno. S2

At the federal level the Blue Book says Title IX prohibits sex discrimination in federally funded education, that courts and administrations have read "sex" in different ways, and that Executive Order 14201 of February 5, 2025, "Keeping Men Out of Women's Sports," directs agencies to read Title IX as barring biological males from teams designated for biological females, with the possibility of federal funding cuts or legal challenges for states that do not follow it. It reports that at least seven Colorado schools and districts have adopted policies restricting biological males who identify as female from female sports. S1

On June 30, 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court decided West Virginia v. B.P.J. (No. 24-43), argued together with Little v. Hecox (No. 24-38). Its syllabus says the Court held that Title IX "allows schools to provide separate women's and men's sports teams defined by biological sex" and that West Virginia and Idaho did not violate the Equal Protection Clause by keeping female sports teams for biological females. The case asked whether states and schools may do this; the syllabus does not say a state must. S3 S4

Courts and athletic associations in Colorado

The Blue Book says the Colorado High School Activities Association (CHSAA) recognizes a right for transgender athletes to take part in school activities and can review schools' eligibility decisions for transgender students. In 2025 eight schools and districts sued over CHSAA rules and CADA requirements; the Blue Book says the schools and CHSAA reached a settlement in December 2025 allowing schools to keep separate teams and facilities for biological males and females without being penalized by CHSAA, and that the part of the suit challenging CADA is still ongoing. At the college level it says NCAA rules limit competing in female sports to athletes assigned female at birth, and that the rules do not apply to intramural sports. S1

Chalkbeat Colorado reported the lawsuit's start: on May 9, 2025, District 49 near Colorado Springs sued Attorney General Phil Weiser, the Colorado Civil Rights Division and CHSAA in federal court, arguing that the state's anti-discrimination law and CHSAA's bylaws put it "in an untenable position." The day before, its school board had narrowly passed a policy barring transgender students from teams that match their gender identity. S5 On January 16, 2026, Chalkbeat reported that on January 13 a federal magistrate judge recommended dismissing the suit, finding the districts and schools lacked standing to sue on their own behalf or on behalf of their students; she did not rule on the legitimacy of the policies themselves. A district court judge would review the recommendation, and the plaintiffs' attorney said they would object. S6 We do not know what happened afterward.

Who wrote it and how it reached the ballot

The Blue Book labels this measure as placed on the ballot by citizen initiative. It says the text "was drafted by the proponents of the initiative," and that the proponents "gathered the required amount of petition signatures." S1

What we do not have: the proponents' names, the filing and Title Board hearing record, earlier drafts of the text, and the signature totals. The arguments on each side

The Blue Book prints two arguments for and two against. They are summarized here, not endorsed. S1 Our voter guide page lists who is paying for each side: Proposition 134 in the Voter Guide.

Arguments in the Blue Book
ForAgainst
Females should be able to compete against females. Supporters say allowing biological males on female teams creates an unfair advantage and can keep female athletes from wins, records, podium spots and scholarships, and that a consistent statewide standard replaces uneven local policies.The measure does not protect fairness; opponents say it turns eligibility into a "body inspection" and creates new grounds for discrimination. They say many athletes, not just transgender ones, could face uncomfortable physical inspections, that the measure sets a standard without a method so schools will make separate policies and treat athletes unevenly, and that some athletes could be made to undergo invasive or costly testing while others only need a doctor's note.
Allowing biological males on female teams jeopardizes the safety and physical and emotional boundaries of female athletes. Supporters point to differences in strength, stature, lung capacity and growth, the risk of injury, and privacy concerns in shared locker rooms.School sports should be a place where all kids build confidence and belonging. Opponents say requiring an at-risk group to play on teams that do not match their gender identity leads to harassment, shame and isolation, and that the rigid standard applies to kids of all ages and abilities, including young children who just want to play with friends.

Claims about unfair advantage, safety, testing and harassment are predictions or contested assertions in the Blue Book's wording of each side's argument. We did not test them against research for this draft.

What it would cost

The Blue Book says the measure will increase workload and legal costs by an indeterminate amount in the Colorado Department of Education if a district is intentionally out of compliance and the commissioner must act, with legal services from the Department of Law. State colleges and universities will have increased costs to adopt or update athletic policies, assumed to be paid from tuition, fees or athletic department revenue. Schools, districts and charter schools will have more workload and possibly minimal costs to update and implement policies, and more legal costs if the Department of Education takes remedial action. S1

What research says

We have not yet gathered research for this study. Questions it could address include what studies show about athletic performance differences between groups at different ages and after different medical treatments, how many transgender students take part in school sports in Colorado, how eligibility verification has worked in states with similar laws, and how exclusion from teams affects students' wellbeing. These are contested areas; we will not list a finding until we have read the study and can say who ran it, what place and period it covers, and what it cannot show.

What we do not yet know

We do not know how a court would square the measure with CADA, which the Blue Book says is unclear. We do not know how schools would verify eligibility, since the text sets no method. We do not know the outcome of the federal suit after the magistrate judge's January 2026 recommendation. The Supreme Court ruling says states may keep sports separate by biological sex; it does not decide how Colorado's own anti-discrimination law applies.

Open work before this could be called a reviewed study: read the Title Board record and proponent names from the Secretary of State; read the full Supreme Court opinion and any concurrences and dissents, not only the syllabus; follow the federal case through its latest filings; gather research on all sides; and get a reviewer who knows education law.
