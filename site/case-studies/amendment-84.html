Amendment 84: mail ballot voter identification

What Amendment 84 would add to mail ballot verification, how signature checks work now, what the official arguments say on each side, and what is still unknown.

This page gives no recommendation for or against any measure. A sourced first draft: no reviewer has signed off, and each source says how much of it was read. Prepared October 31, 2026.

What the measure does

Sourcing note: this page so far rests on the Colorado Blue Book alone. We have not yet read the Title Board record, the proponents' filings, or any outside reporting.

Amendment 84 is a citizen initiative that changes the Colorado Constitution. It needs 55 percent of the vote to pass. The text adds Section 13 to Article VII, "Voter Authentication." For any federal or statewide election, a voter must sign the self-affirmation on a returned mail ballot and also include the last four digits of a social security number, a Colorado Real ID driver's license number or a Colorado Real ID identification number. A voter may instead use the last four digits of another Colorado identification that meets the federal Real ID Act. S1

If the voter leaves the information blank or incomplete, or an election judge cannot match it to the statewide voter registration database, the county clerk must send notice of how to correct the discrepancy and "shall not count the ballot until the discrepancy is corrected." A voter can correct it by providing, in person or digitally, a copy of the identification above, a U.S. passport, a U.S. military identification card or a tribal photo identification. A voter who has the right to vote other than in person under the federal Voting Accessibility for the Elderly and Handicapped Act may use any form of identification that Colorado law accepts for in-person voting. S1

The Blue Book adds that, as with the current signature process, the ballot is not counted unless the voter confirms identity within eight days of election day. The eight-day deadline is not in the measure's text as printed in the Blue Book; we have not checked where it comes from. S1

How mail ballots are checked today

The Blue Book says every Colorado voter receives a mail ballot for federal and statewide elections, and that in 2024, 92 percent of ballots cast in Colorado were mail ballots, most returned to a drop box. Today the voter signs the return envelope and the county compares that signature with the one on file from election and driver's license records. If a discrepancy is found, two election judges of different parties review it. If both agree the signature likely does not belong to the voter, the county contacts the voter, and the ballot is not counted if the voter does not confirm identity within eight days after election day. S1

The measure does not change the identification allowed for in-person voting, which the Blue Book says is broader than the mail ballot requirement and includes documents showing name and address. S1

Who wrote it and how it reached the ballot

The Blue Book labels this measure as placed on the ballot by citizen initiative. It says the text of the measure "was drafted by the proponents of the initiative," and that the proponents "gathered the required amount of petition signatures." S1

What we do not have: the proponents' names, the filing and Title Board hearing record, earlier drafts of the text, and the signature totals. None of these is in the sources we saved, so this page does not name anyone. The arguments on each side

The Blue Book prints one argument for and one against. They are summarized here, not endorsed. S1

Arguments in the Blue Book
ForAgainst
Added verification strengthens election security while keeping voting convenient. Identification documents are more consistent and less subjective than signature checks. Most voters already have an eligible identification card or know their social security number, and a voter without one can vote in person with several identification options. Supporters say this promotes public confidence that ineligible ballots will not be counted.The measure adds an unnecessary burden without meaningfully improving security. Opponents say eligible voters' ballots could go uncounted over minor mistakes, that voters with privacy concerns or without the required identification may be discouraged from using mail ballots, and that the current signature process is effective, secure and convenient and catches the rare cases of ineligible ballots.

These are the sides' claims in the Blue Book's wording. We have not tested them against election data or research for this draft.

What it would cost

The Blue Book says the measure would increase state and local spending by up to $2.7 million in budget year 2026-27 and $1.2 million a year after that, for voter outreach, printing and election staff to verify voter information. County costs would be up to $2.5 million in 2026-27 and up to $1.2 million in later years, with about 45 percent reimbursed by the state. Counties would need more election judge hours, notices and segregated ballots for voters with discrepancies, and updated envelopes, systems and outreach. The Department of State would run an outreach campaign at $100,000 in 2026-27 and $200,000 in 2027-28, plus about $20,000 for each presidential primary, statewide primary and general election, and would reimburse counties up to $1.1 million in 2026-27 and up to $500,000 later. The Blue Book says these costs have historically been paid from fees and other department revenue, not the General Fund, and that business filing fees may need to rise to cover some or all of them. What we do not yet know

We do not know who proposed the measure or how its text changed before the vote, how many Colorado voters lack one of the listed identification numbers, or how many ballots would need correction. We do not know where the eight-day correction deadline comes from, since it is not in the printed text.
