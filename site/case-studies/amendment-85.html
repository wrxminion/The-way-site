Amendment 85: plain language ballot titles

What Amendment 85 would require of ballot titles, how titles are written under current law, what the official arguments say on each side, and what is still unknown.

This page gives no recommendation for or against any measure. A sourced first draft: no reviewer has signed off, and each source says how much of it was read. Prepared October 31, 2026.

What the measure does

Amendment 85 is a citizen initiative. It would add a new paragraph (5.6) to section 1 of article V of the Colorado Constitution. It needs 55 percent of the vote to pass. S1

The whole text is two sentences: "Ballot questions shall be in plain language at no more than an eighth-grade reading level. No statute may interfere with this provision by mandating language preceding, following, or in a ballot title of an initiative." S1

The Blue Book points out what the text leaves out. It does not define plain language and does not say who decides whether a title meets the standard. It describes an eighth-grade level as one "easily and fully understood by a majority of people," typically with short sentences, familiar words and a conversational tone. It also says the ban on required language does not reach titles of measures the legislature refers to the ballot. S1

The wording differs slightly in the three places it appears. The text says "ballot questions." The official ballot title says "all state and local ballot questions" must meet the standard. The Blue Book summary says "all ballot titles." How a court would read "ballot questions" against "ballot titles" is not settled by any source we read.

What a yes or no vote means

The official ballot title asks: "Shall there be an amendment to the Colorado Constitution concerning ballot question language, and, in connection therewith, requiring all state and local ballot questions to be written in plain language and at no more than an 8th grade reading level and prohibiting a state statute from requiring language that conflicts with these requirements in ballot questions for citizen-initiated measures?" How ballot titles are written today

A person can propose a law through the initiative process and put it on the ballot with enough voter signatures. Before signatures are collected, a state Title Board writes the ballot title. The Blue Book says the board is made up of representatives from the Attorney General's office, the Secretary of State's office and the legislature's legal office, and that state law requires titles to be brief, to help voters understand the effect of a yes or no vote, to be in plain language understandable by the widest possible audience, and to avoid legal, technical or specialized terms when possible. When the legislature refers a measure, the legislature writes the title, and state law requires bills and bill titles to be in plain, nontechnical language. S1

Some current laws require specific wording in citizen initiative titles. The Blue Book gives the example of tax measures: an initiative that raises or lowers state taxes must include specific language describing the changes and their impact on taxpayers, and one that reduces tax collections must include an estimate of possible reductions in state spending and how the lost revenue might affect the three largest areas of government spending. S1

Two recent laws are part of that picture, according to the General Assembly's bill pages:

  • HB21-1321, signed July 7, 2021. As enacted, it requires specific language at the start of a ballot title for an initiative that reduces state or local property tax revenue through a tax change, and specific language about public services for one that increases tax revenue. It also adds requirements to the Blue Book entry for some tax measures, including a table of tax filers by income group and the change in tax burden. S2
  • HB26-1320, signed by the governor May 29, 2026 according to its bill history. As enacted, it adds a requirement that the Title Board write titles in "accessible language," meaning plain language understood by the widest possible audience, and lists things the board may consider, such as avoiding technical terms and double negatives. It also changes the tax-measure rules so the required language only needs to be "substantially similar" to the statute's wording and may appear anywhere in the title. It applies to initiatives submitted for title setting on or after its effective date. S3

We read the bill pages' official summaries, which say they describe each bill "as enacted." We did not read the signed acts. The HB26-1320 page lists a signed act dated June 2, 2026 while its bill history shows the governor signing on May 29, 2026; we did not resolve the difference. We also have not checked whether any initiative title has been written under HB26-1320.

What it might change

The Blue Book's analysis points to two possible effects. First, it describes a constitutional eighth-grade standard that sits beside the current general plain-language rule; it says opponents note the measure gives no guidance on how the two differ. Second, the ban on statutes "mandating language preceding, following, or in" an initiative title could be read to reach laws like the tax-title requirements above. The Blue Book says opponents fear exactly that: laws that add language to ballot titles "could be prohibited, even if that language provides critical information for voters," and the measure "may lead to challenges of existing laws." S1

Supporters, in the Blue Book, say the same ban is a feature: it would stop a future legislature from adding laws that make initiative titles "complicated or confusing." S1 Whether the existing tax-title laws would be struck down, narrowed or left alone is not decided by any source we read, and it would fall to courts.

Who wrote it and how it reached the ballot

The Blue Book labels this measure as placed on the ballot by citizen initiative. It says the text of the measure "was drafted by the proponents of the initiative," and that the proponents "gathered the required amount of petition signatures." S1

What we do not have: the proponents' names, the filing and Title Board hearing record, earlier drafts of the text, and the signature totals. None of these is in the sources we saved, so this page does not name anyone. The arguments on each side

The Blue Book prints two arguments for and two against. They are summarized here, not endorsed. The Secretary of State lists the organizations supporting and opposing each measure. S1 Our voter guide page lists who is paying for each side: Amendment 85 in the Voter Guide.

ForAgainst

A constitutional standard would help in writing simple titles that voters may better understand, and may help them with complicated and technical information.The measure is unnecessary because state law already gives ample guidance. What it would cost

The Blue Book says the measure may minimally increase workload for the Title Board to update its standards for plain language and for what counts as an eighth-grade reading level, and to apply them when it sets titles for citizen initiatives. It gives no dollar figure. S1

What research says

We have not yet gathered research for this study. Questions it could address include how readable current Colorado ballot titles are by common reading-level formulas (which give different grades for the same text), whether plainer titles change how well voters understand what a vote does, and what happened where other states set a reading standard for ballot language. We will not list a finding until we have read the study and can say who ran it, what place and period it covers, and what it cannot show.

What we do not yet know

The text does not say which reading-level test applies, who applies it, or what happens to a title that fails it. We do not know which existing statutory title requirements a court would treat as "interfering." We have not checked how many Colorado ballot titles meet an eighth-grade level today.

Open work before this could be called a reviewed study: ask a lawyer who handles Colorado election law what the second sentence of the text would reach, including the tax-title laws; read the signed texts of HB21-1321 and HB26-1320; measure the current statewide titles with a named reading-level formula and say which; gather research on all sides; and get a reviewer.
