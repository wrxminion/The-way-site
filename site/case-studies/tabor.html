TABOR: voter control, public revenue, and Colorado's ballot choices

Understand the rules behind Colorado's recurring debates about taxes, refunds and public spending, and the evidence needed to assess their results.

The policy in plain language

Colorado voters adopted the Taxpayer's Bill of Rights, known as TABOR, in 1992. Its rules include voter approval for tax increases and a limit on how much revenue government may keep. Revenue above the applicable limit generally has to be returned unless voters allow it to be kept. Setting a tax rate and allowing the government to keep revenue are two different questions. S1

Referendum C, approved in 2005, let the state keep all the revenue it collected for fiscal years 2005-06 through 2009-10. It also set an ongoing adjusted cap that grows from fiscal year 2007-08 revenue. Revenue above that cap is still subject to refunds. The limit grows with inflation and population. These are state mechanics; local governments are treated separately. S1

Official 1992 and 2005 results, checked October 26, 2026 in the Colorado Secretary of State's Historical Elections Database: 1992 Amendment 01, 812,308 yes and 700,906 no out of 1,513,214 votes on the question (53.68 percent yes) S7. The same database lists 83,952 blank votes and 1,597,166 ballots cast; those are different denominators. 2005 Referendum C, 600,222 yes and 552,662 no out of 1,152,884 votes (52.06 percent yes) S8.

Article X, section 20 (the TABOR text): we read the current official edition, the 2026 Colorado Constitution published by the Office of Legislative Legal Services, on October 26, 2026, and keep a dated copy. Its opening rule says the preferred interpretation is to "reasonably restrain most the growth of government." A reader should open the official text for any legal detail: olls.info, 2026 edition. This page quotes only that sentence and summarizes the rest in plain words. We read these totals in the state database in a browser. The certification statements on the original 1992 and 2005 abstract-of-votes scans have not been read (see "What we do not yet know").

What does that look like in action?

Invented teaching numbers, not Colorado figures and not a calculator.

Suppose revenue subject to a limit is $110 and the limit is $100. With no voter authorization, the $10 excess is refundable. If voters instead authorize keeping revenue up to $108, then $8 can be kept and $2 is still refundable. Neither case changes any tax rate.

A government can have revenue above a legal cap and still have unmet needs. A formula "surplus" does not measure whether every service is paid for. In the other direction, an unmet need does not show that a given appropriation will fix it. Both the budget rule and the proposed use deserve a look.

Intent, implementation, and outcomes

Three lanes run side by side. Each card names its source and how far to trust it. A text version of the same information follows the lanes.

Intent

  1. 1992 Ballot question asks voters to require voter approval for certain tax increases and debt, restrict some taxes, limit the rate of growth in state and local spending, allow more initiative elections, and mail information to voters. Official ballot text S7
  2. Constitutional language favors restraining the growth of government. Whether that is desirable is a value judgment. Official 2026 text read S2

Implementation

  1. 1992 Voters adopt TABOR. Official result S7
  2. 2005 Voters approve Referendum C. The question covered five years of keeping excess revenue and ongoing retention under a defined cap afterward. Official ballot text S8
  3. FY 2005-06 to 2009-10 Referendum C retention period. Official explanation S1
  4. FY 2010-11 onward Ongoing Referendum C cap. Official explanation S1
  5. September 8, 2025 Colorado Supreme Court holds Lakewood's ordinances were new taxes imposed without required voter approval (MetroPCS v. Lakewood). Court opinion S10
  6. June 2026 HB26-1419 becomes law, tying a conditional over-refund calculation to federal tax changes and limiting how much can be offset in a single future year. Enacted summary read; check the signed act before legal use S9

Outcomes

Ordered by publication year. A study's evidence covers a different period, shown on each card.

  1. 2007 Martell and Teske: reports pressure toward earmarking, privatizing and shifting responsibilities during downturns. Peer reviewed; abstract level S12
  2. 2012 St. Clair: evidence of higher revenue and spending volatility in Colorado local-government data. Peer reviewed; abstract level S11
  3. 2015 Resnick, Brown and Godshall (Lincoln Institute): says TABOR forced fairly significant redistribution of school funding in its simulation; the paper says its findings did not get detailed staff review. Working paper; sentence read on page 33 S13
  4. 2018 Eliason and Lutz, Journal of Public Economics: no detectable effect on total taxes or spending in their comparison of Colorado with other states (evidence covers 1977 to 2012). Peer reviewed; abstract level for the final article S3
  5. 2026 Jansen and Moore (Texas A&M research center): emphasizes limits on non-exempt revenue and adaptation through Referendum C and exempt activity. Research report; peer review not established S4

What the research currently says

These studies measure different things over different periods. Taxes and spending in total, volatility, and who pays school property taxes are not the same outcome, and they should not be averaged into one verdict.

Published evidence includes both fiscal-management and volatility costs and a finding against a large drop in total government activity during the period studied. Neither settles whether direct voter control is worth it, and none alone establishes TABOR's net effect today.

Connection to the November 2026 ballot

The official 2026 Blue Book connects TABOR to four measures. All four are proposals; none has been decided. S5

  • Proposition NN: retention of revenue. The Blue Book separates about $4.6 billion of added first-year capacity from about $500 million in expected actual retention. These are estimates, not receipts.
  • Amendment 87: graduated income taxation and related revenue.
  • Proposition 136: a statutory rate ceiling.
  • Proposition 137: dedicated sporting-goods sales-tax revenue and exemption from the limit.

Two vintages of numbers: the Blue Book (September 10, 2026) gives refund estimates for budget years 2026-27 and 2027-28 and says no forecast was available beyond that. The Legislative Council's September 2026 forecast, shown below, projects the current-law baseline through 2028-29. The baseline is not a re-estimate of any measure, so its figures are not swapped into the Blue Book's measure estimates. Household refund figures anywhere in the guide are estimates.

The Blue Book also says it is uncertain how a conflict would be resolved if 87 and 136 both pass. Do not assume an outcome. These descriptions are short; use each measure page and the Blue Book for the exact wording. Numbers from the September 10 Blue Book are dated official estimates and are kept as published.

Questions to bring to any TABOR-related proposal

  • Does it change a rate, a revenue limit, an exemption, an allocation, or several of these?
  • What happens under current law if it fails?
  • Is a dollar figure a legal maximum, a forecast, an appropriation or actual spending?
  • What uses are required and what is left to lawmakers, now and later?
  • Who gets refunds or services, and how is that measured?
  • What reporting would let anyone compare what was promised with what was delivered?

What we do not yet know

This draft does not establish TABOR's net effect on economic growth, school quality, inequality, voter understanding or administrative cost. It does not blame Colorado's fiscal condition on TABOR alone: recessions, population change, federal money, other laws and local choices all matter.

Open work before this could be called a reviewed study: read the certification statements on the original 1992 and 2005 abstract-of-votes scans and check the 1992 database wording against the ballot scan (the state's file host blocks automated download, so a person has to fetch them); check the latest Legislative Council forecast and the fiscal notes for each measure; read the full published text of Eliason and Lutz (2018), St. Clair (2012) and Martell and Teske (2007), since only abstracts were read; build a consistent yearly series of covered revenue, cap and refunds; find research that disagrees; and get a public-finance or methods reviewer. No reviewer has signed off.
