WIC Nutrition Assistance (United States, 1972 to 2026)

Evidence first. Conclusions revisable.

WIC began in 1972 as a two-year pilot that gives food, nutrition advice and health referrals to low-income pregnant women, infants and young children; studies link it to better birth outcomes, but most are observational, and about 44 percent of eligible people, including more than half of eligible children, do not take part.

In brief

WIC began in 1972 as a two-year pilot to give food, nutrition advice and health referrals to low-income pregnant women, infants and children under 5. It was made permanent in 1975. Today it serves about 6.9 million people a month and costs $7.7 billion a year. Studies link it to modest gains in birth weight and to fewer preterm and low birth weight births, though almost all rest on comparisons, not trials, and critics say the savings are overstated. In 2023, USDA estimated that 56.1 percent of eligible people took part. Four in five eligible infants were covered, but only 27 percent of eligible four-year-olds and 24 percent of eligible people outside metropolitan areas were. A rebate system that pays for formula for about one family in five also left states exposed when one maker stopped production in 2022. The record is public and detailed. It shows a program that works for those it reaches and misses many who qualify.

The record

Origins and rules

  • Concern about hunger among low-income Americans grew in the 1960s. A 1969 White House Conference recommended special attention to low-income pregnant women and young children. USDA started the Commodity Supplemental Food Program in 1969. Clinic-linked food voucher programs in Atlanta (1968) and Baltimore (developed by Dr. David Paige of Johns Hopkins) were models. (USDA Economic Research Service, The WIC Program: Background, Trends, and Issues (FANRR-27); opened; the excerpt had no author or date, and its data run through fiscal 2000)
  • Public Law 92-433, signed September 26, 1972, created WIC as a two-year pilot by amending the Child Nutrition Act of 1966. Senator Hubert Humphrey sponsored it. USDA at first took little action, and a federal court ordered it to implement the program in 1973. The first site opened in Pineville, Kentucky, on January 15, 1974. By the end of 1974 WIC was in 45 states with about 88,000 participants monthly. (ERS FANRR-27; opened. We did not open the public law text.)
  • Public Law 94-105, October 7, 1975, made WIC permanent and extended it to non-breastfeeding women up to six months after birth and children up to age 5. (ERS FANRR-27; Wikipedia, WIC program; both opened)
  • Eligibility today: pregnant, breastfeeding and postpartum women, infants and children under 5 who are at nutritional risk and have household income at or below 185 percent of the poverty level, or who qualify through SNAP, Medicaid or TANF. Benefits are food packages, nutrition education, breastfeeding support and referrals. It is run by 88 state-level agencies, including tribal organizations and territories. Congress has funded it every year since the late 1990s to serve all eligible applicants who apply. (USDA ERS, WIC program page; opened)
  • Federal costs rose from $10.4 million in fiscal 1974 to almost $3.9 billion in fiscal 2000. Food was about 73 percent of the cost in fiscal 1999. ERS credits infant formula rebates that began in the late 1980s with cutting the real monthly cost of a food package from nearly $60 in the mid-1970s to $33 in 2000. (ERS FANRR-27)
  • For fiscal 2025, ERS reports federal costs of $7.7 billion, about 6.9 million participants a month, an estimated 41 percent of all U.S. infants served, and about $1.6 billion in formula rebates. (ERS WIC page; the page says figures are as of January 2026 and subject to revision)

Who participates and who does not (2023)

USDA's annual estimate for 2023: average monthly eligible population 11,829,215, participants 6,631,309, coverage 56.1 percent. This is the highest since 2016. (USDA, WIC Eligibility and Coverage Rates 2023; opened)

  • Coverage was 61.1 percent in metropolitan areas and 24.0 percent in nonmetropolitan areas. (USDA 2023)
  • The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities (CBPP), a research group that supports the program, reports earlier coverage as 54.2 percent in 2016, 51.2 percent in 2021 and 53.5 percent in 2022, notes that post-2015 figures are not directly comparable to earlier ones because USDA changed its estimation method, and says nearly half of WIC-eligible Medicaid or SNAP enrollees do not receive WIC. It says the shortfall "has no single cause," pointing to state administrative practices, local housing and transportation costs, and whether agencies refer people already enrolled elsewhere. (CBPP; opened)

Barriers

  • A 2013 qualitative study of 70 WIC-eligible families in Minnesota (Wilder Research for the state health department) found that scheduling appointments was the hardest part of staying enrolled for about a third of participants, that over four-fifths of former participants phoned reported trouble finding approved items in stores, and that many immigrant families wrongly believed employment or non-citizen status made them ineligible. Nearly half of former participants said stigma may keep neighbors away. The sample came mainly through community groups and may not represent unconnected families. (Wilder Research, 2013; opened)
  • A 2022 research brief by Stephanie Ettinger de Cuba and colleagues in the Journal of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics argues that fear tied to the public charge rule has led some immigrant families to avoid WIC. It cites a study that found a measurable drop in child enrollment after the rule was announced, and another (Venkataramani and colleagues) that found the opposite pattern for children of mothers eligible for DACA. The brief notes that WIC does not record immigration status, so data are scarce. (House Rules Committee Democrats copy of the brief; opened)

Infant formula system

  • Since 1989, WIC agencies have generally had to use single-supplier bids for formula, choosing the lowest net price after a rebate. GAO reports rebate savings of about $1.6 billion in 2023, which let states serve about one-fifth of monthly participants, and also that participants have limited choice and that reliance on one supplier leaves states exposed to supply disruptions. Over half of U.S. infant formula is bought through WIC, and as of August 2024 two manufacturers held almost all WIC contracts. GAO found that winning a contract raised the winner's price for specified products by 1.7 percent, about 30 cents per 12-ounce container of powder. (GAO-25-106503; opened)

  • A federally sponsored systematic review of WIC and health outcomes exists; the USDA page that announces it says it examines whether participation "is associated with" outcomes and whether associations differ by length of participation. The full report was not opened. (USDA FNA; opened, landing page only)
  • Two secondary summaries describe it differently (see table below). The AAFP blog says WIC likely reduces preterm birth, low birth weight and infant mortality, with lower-strength evidence for other outcomes, no association with breastfeeding rates, and insufficient evidence for childhood obesity. A summary in the Hospitalist says the authors found evidence that was "absent" in some areas and moderate or low in others, and that it is unclear how WIC achieves the results. (AAFP blog, Sept. 19, 2022; MDedge, Hospitalist; both opened)

What this case shows

  1. A program people can see and judge. WIC is funded by annual appropriation. USDA publishes yearly eligibility and coverage estimates by group and by rural or urban area, and GAO reviewed the formula system in 2025. An ordinary person can find that 56.1 percent of eligible people took part in 2023 and which groups lag. (USDA 2023; GAO-25-106503)
  2. Who was left out is measurable. About half of eligible pregnant women and children, and three of four eligible 4-year-olds, did not take part in 2023. Rural coverage was 24.0 percent against 61.1 percent in metropolitan areas. Qualitative studies point to appointment hours, store problems, language, stigma and fear of immigration consequences. Those left out are not those who were turned away by a rule alone; many were never reached. (USDA 2023; Wilder; Ettinger de Cuba et al.)
  3. The evidence is mixed in quality, not necessarily in direction. The strongest designs (county rollout, sibling comparisons) tend to find modest gains in birth weight, especially for the most disadvantaged, but no study we opened is a randomized trial of the whole program. Voters were asked to rely on associations. (Hoynes et al.; Bitler and Currie; Kowaleski-Jones and Duncan)
  4. Costs and trade-offs are in the record. Formula rebates of about $1.6 billion fund benefits for roughly one-fifth of monthly participants, and the single-supplier rule that produces them also limited choice and left states exposed during the 2022 shortage. The public paid in the form of reduced choice for participants and, GAO says, possibly higher retail prices for non-WIC buyers. (GAO-25-106503)
  5. A program born from a court order and clinic experience. A federal court ordered USDA to implement the 1972 law in 1973, after USDA took little action. The origin shows the pattern in this project's question: a law passed in public, but the public needed a lawsuit to see it carried out. (ERS FANRR-27)

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: WIC saves money, and the evidence is strong. Defenders say the program is among the most studied, with over 200 published studies (Leighton Ku, as summarized by the University of Maryland Welfare Reform Academy), and cite savings estimates of $1.77 to $3.13 per dollar in Medicaid costs (Wikipedia). Mitigation: We give both sides. Besharov and Germanis call savings claims "simply untrue" because the research covers pregnant women and suffers from selection bias, and say impacts for pregnant women range from "perhaps zero to substantial." We report designs and limits for each study and do not state a net savings figure.
  2. Objection: Observational studies cannot separate WIC's effect from mothers' motivation. Mitigation: Correct, and several authors say so (Kowaleski-Jones and Duncan; Bitler and Currie). The county rollout study and sibling comparisons reduce but do not remove the problem. The title of this case and the summary use "link" rather than "cause" for this reason.
  3. Objection: Low take-up shows people do not want it, or eligibility is too broad. Besharov and Germanis also argue the "nutritional risk" definition is too broad and that income testing has become less thorough as funding rose; a USDA study found 5.7 percent of participants had incomes too high to qualify (reported by Wikipedia). Mitigation: We report both. Coverage is highest for infants (82.3 percent) and falls with age, which fits a view that families judge the value of the benefit against the effort of recertifying. Our sources show barriers but no survey ranks reasons for non-participation, so we make no claim about cause.
  4. Objection: The immigrant chilling effect is advocacy. The source brief is hosted on a legislative committee's page, and its authors argue a point. Mitigation: We label it as an argument by researchers. The brief itself cites one study with the opposite finding for DACA-eligible families, and notes it is not a national sample. We could not open the underlying studies.
  5. Objection: The 2022 shortage was a manufacturer's failure, not a WIC failure. Mitigation: GAO attributes the shortage to a production halt after a recall; it also says reliance on one supplier leaves states vulnerable. We state both.
  6. Objection: Two of our sources about the federal review disagree. Mitigation: We put both in the table. We did not open the review report.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • We did not open the full federal systematic review report. Our two summaries disagree on the study count (82 or 20) and framing. Unverified until the report is read.
  • The 1985 randomized study (Metcoff and colleagues) cited by Leighton Ku as finding a 91 gram birth weight gain was not opened.
  • Savings per dollar ($1.77 to $3.13) come from Wikipedia's secondary list; the underlying studies were not opened. Unverified.
  • The text of Public Law 92-433 and P.L. 94-105 was not opened; dates come from ERS and Wikipedia.
  • The 1973 federal court case that ordered implementation is named nowhere in what we read. Case name unverified.
  • The 5.7 percent over-income figure and the Wikipedia participation and cost series were not checked against USDA.
  • The Hoynes, Page and Stevens article was read through an NBER digest. The digest gave a percent figure for the participant effect that we did not reproduce because we could not confirm it.
  • Rossin-Slater's clinic-access study (Journal of Public Economics, 2013) could not be opened (a bot-protection page blocked the Columbia copy). It is not used.
  • We did not find a current, independent estimate of the cost of the barriers (administrative cost of recertification) or why take-up differs across states.
  • Pages were read through a summarizing fetch tool. Quoted phrases and table figures should be rechecked against the original files before print.
  • What would change our minds: a randomized or well-identified national evaluation showing no gain for infants, or a study showing take-up barriers falling after remote or online enrollment.
