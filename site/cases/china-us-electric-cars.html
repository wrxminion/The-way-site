Chinese makers sold about 60% of the world's electric cars in 2025 and China makes most battery cells. The United States keeps Chinese cars out with a 100% tariff and a connected-car security rule, and ended its EV tax credit in 2025, after which U.S. electric car sales fell. Both sides hold leverage through tariffs and battery materials.

In brief

China now leads the world in electric cars. Chinese makers sold about 60% of the world's electric vehicles in 2025, and China makes more than 80% of battery cells, according to the International Energy Agency. The United States has taken a different path. It keeps Chinese cars out with a 100% tariff and a rule banning Chinese connected-car software from model year 2027, and Congress ended the $7,500 federal EV tax credit on September 30, 2025. U.S. electric car sales then fell from a peak of 11.4% of new cars to about 5 to 6%, while hybrids grew. In October 2026 the Air Force barred BYD cars from a U.S. base in Japan as a security risk. Supporters of these limits point to jobs, subsidies and spying risk. Others point to higher prices for American buyers, and President Trump has said Chinese makers could build plants in the U.S. if they hire Americans. A one-year pause in China's battery export controls ends November 10, 2026.

Prices, tax credits and tariffs

  • United States: the new and used EV credits ended on Sept. 30, 2025 and the home charger credit on June 30, 2026. Chinese EVs face a 100% Section 301 tariff. Chinese EV batteries face 25% since 2024, other Chinese lithium-ion batteries 25% since Jan. 1, 2026, and a further 12.5% Section 301 tariff since July 24, 2026. Imported vehicles in general face the Section 232 tariff of 25%, lower for some allies under trade deals. We could not reach the U.S. Trade Representative's own pages, so these rates come from two trade guides. (CargoTrans; The Charge Port)
  • In late September 2026 the administration also withdrew California's emissions waiver and scaled back fuel economy standards. (NPR via KUNC)
  • China: since Jan. 1, 2026 the purchase-tax break is halved, and since Sept. 1, 2026 a 2% consumption tax applies to lithium-ion batteries, rising to 4% in 2027. (electrive)
  • Subsidies: U.S. automakers call China's industry heavily subsidized. We did not find a primary source that measures China's local subsidies and trade-in programs, so we give no figure. (Carscoops)

Connected cars and security

  • The Commerce Department rule bans importing or selling passenger vehicles with connected-car software tied to China or other named foreign adversaries from model year 2027, and such hardware from model year 2030. Its stated reason is "undue and unacceptable risk to U.S. national security," because access could expose driver data and allow remote control. Trucks and buses were left for a later rule. (Commerce BIS)
  • The main U.S. automaker trade group asked Congress for a "permanent ban on the sale, import and manufacture of Chinese connected vehicles, hardware and software," and a Senate committee advanced a tougher bill in July 2026. (Carscoops)
  • Kadena Air Base: the 18th Wing called BYD vehicles "an unacceptable and unmitigable risk to our personnel and our mission." The ban covers private cars, taxis, delivery and contractor vehicles at Kadena only. Japanese reporting says the concern is that car cameras and sensors could record aircraft, radar and operations. No public evidence was cited that any BYD car sent data from the base. This is a base-access order, not a law. (SOFX; Jiji via Nippon.com)

Batteries and minerals

  • China makes more than 80% of the world's battery cells. (IEA via Energy Digital)
  • China has used export controls on battery materials: graphite in 2023 and a wider set of battery cells and materials announced in October 2025, later paused for one year. Whether China lets the pause end on Nov. 10, 2026 is open. (Automotive World; search results)
  • We did not find a primary figure for U.S. battery factory capacity.

The arguments

For keeping Chinese cars and parts out

  • Subsidized Chinese cars could undercut U.S. makers and jobs. (U.S. automakers, via Carscoops)
  • Connected cars carry cameras, sensors and data links that raise spying and sabotage risk. (Commerce BIS; 18th Wing)
  • Depending on China for batteries and minerals is a weak point, as its export controls show. (Automotive World)

For more openness, or other concerns

  • President Trump: "If China wanted to come in and open a plant to build their cars here, I'd be okay with that," as long as they hire Americans; he does not want them building in Mexico and shipping north. (Carscoops)
  • Without Chinese competition, American buyers pay more; the average EV costs over $54,000. (Cox Automotive via NPR)
  • Ford asked the administration in February 2026 to allow some Chinese EV technology in the U.S. (headline only, article not read).
  • The Kadena ban can be read as a narrow security step taken without public proof of a leak. (SOFX)

What this case shows

  1. Two countries made opposite bets. China backed electric cars with tax breaks and now dominates sales and batteries; the U.S. ended its credits and walled off Chinese cars. (IEA; NPR)
  2. Policy moved the market fast. U.S. EV share roughly halved after the credit ended, while hybrids grew. (Edmunds and Cox via NPR)
  3. Security and trade now overlap. The connected-car rule and the Kadena ban are framed as security, but they also shape who can sell cars. (Commerce BIS; SOFX)
  4. Both sides hold leverage. The U.S. uses tariffs; China uses control of battery materials. A deadline on that leverage falls on Nov. 10, 2026.

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: This page leans on trade guides for tariff rates. Agreed. The U.S. Trade Representative's pages were not reachable, so we name the two guides and mark the rates as secondary.
  2. Objection: China's subsidies are the whole story. Our answer: They are a central claim of U.S. automakers, and we report it, but we found no primary figure to measure them.
  3. Objection: The Kadena ban proves Chinese cars spy. Our answer: The Air Force called it a risk; no public evidence of data being sent was cited. We report both.
  4. Objection: Lower U.S. EV sales are a good thing, not a failure. Our answer: The page takes no side on that. It reports the numbers and the policy changes that came before them.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • The tariff rates confirmed in the Federal Register or on the U.S. Trade Representative's site.
  • Whether China extends or ends its battery export pause after Nov. 10, 2026.
  • A primary estimate of Chinese government support for its automakers.
  • U.S. battery factory capacity, and China's share of lithium and graphite processing, from the IEA's full report.
  • Whether other U.S. bases follow the Kadena ban.
  • The outcome of BYD's challenge to the Pentagon listing.
