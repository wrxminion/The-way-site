Title IX and girls' school athletics (United States, 1972 to today)

Evidence first. Conclusions revisable.

Descend into the record ↓
Sources checked 2026-10-19 · Preliminary research, not a final verdict
Report for revisionDownload research packet

Members only. Sign in to send a correction.

A short federal sex-discrimination law, passed in 1972 without the word "athletics," was turned into athletics rules by agency regulation and court cases, and girls' sports grew from 294,015 high school participants to about 3.2 million, while enforcement was slow, men's team cuts remain disputed, and gaps by race and school poverty persist.

In brief

In 1972 Congress passed Title IX, a short law against sex discrimination in federally funded education. It did not mention sports. Agency rules in 1975 and a policy interpretation in 1979 turned it into rules for teams, and students used the courts to hold schools to them. In 1971 to 1972, 294,015 girls played high school sports. In 2021 to 2022, 3,241,472 did, and 4,376,582 boys did. The law was not enforced by cutting off money: a 1999 federal audit found the Department of Education had never pulled funds from a college. The fight over men's teams is real. Wrestling lost 171 college teams from 1981 to 1999, and schools gave reasons of interest, equity and budget. Yet men's teams overall rose slightly, and a 2023 study found cut money went mostly to football and basketball. Girls at majority Black and Hispanic high schools, one advocacy report says, still get fewer chances than girls at mostly white schools. The record shows what a rule can do and who had to push for it.

The record

The statute and who wrote it

  • Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972 (Pub. L. 92-318) was signed by President Richard Nixon on June 23, 1972. Senator Birch Bayh (D-IN) introduced it and led the Senate debate. Representative Edith Green chaired the House education subcommittee and wrote an early draft. Representative Patsy Mink led House efforts to protect it; Congress renamed it for her in 2002. (Wikipedia, Title IX; opened)
  • The core text begins: "No person in the United States shall, on the basis of sex, be excluded from participation in" any federally funded education program or activity. It then lists exceptions: admissions at some schools, religious schools whose tenets conflict, military schools, fraternities and sororities, youth groups, father-son and mother-daughter activities, and some pageant scholarships. (20 U.S.C. 1681, Cornell Legal Information Institute; opened)
  • Subsection (b) says no school must give "preferential or disparate treatment" because of an imbalance in the number or percentage of one sex in a program. It adds that statistical evidence of imbalance may still be considered in proceedings. The word "proportion" does not appear in the statute. (20 U.S.C. 1681, opened)
  • The statute does not mention athletics. The Congressional Research Service says so directly. (CRS, Title IX Athletics legal history, In Focus IF12325, updated March 27, 2025; opened)

How athletics got in

  • In 1974 the Senate rejected an amendment by Senator John Tower that would have exempted revenue-producing sports. It adopted Senator Jacob Javits's amendment, which told the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare (HEW) to write rules that consider "the nature of particular sports." Final HEW regulations were published in June 1975 and are codified at 34 CFR Part 106. (Wikipedia, Title IX; opened. The Javits wording is summarized there, not quoted from the Congressional Record.)
  • 34 CFR 106.41 is the athletics rule. It bars sex-based exclusion from interscholastic, intercollegiate, club or intramural athletics. It lets schools keep separate teams by sex when selection is based on competitive skill or the sport is a contact sport. It requires "equal athletic opportunity for members of both sexes" and lists ten factors, including equipment, scheduling, travel, coaching, facilities and publicity. It says unequal total spending does not by itself prove a violation. (eCFR, 34 CFR 106.41; opened)
  • The 1979 Policy Interpretation (HEW) set three areas: scholarships in proportion, equivalent benefits, and effective accommodation of interests and abilities. It was written mainly for colleges, but its principles are often applied to high schools. The accommodation part is the "three-part test." A school passes by meeting any one prong: (1) participation roughly proportionate to enrollment by sex, (2) a history and continuing practice of expanding opportunities for the underrepresented sex, or (3) full and effective accommodation of that sex's interests and abilities. (CRS IF12325; opened)

  • GAO said there was broad agreement that Title IX increased women's participation, but it "could not isolate" the law's effect from other factors. (GAO/HEHS-00-24; opened, summarized)
  • Spending in 1997 to 1998 at NCAA schools: about $6,500 per female athlete and $8,200 per male athlete. In Division I-A, about $18,000 versus $29,000. Men held most athletic director jobs. (GAO/HEHS-00-24)
  • The high school gap in 2021 to 2022 is about 1.1 million (4,376,582 minus 3,241,472). Girls' total is still below the 3.67 million boys had in 1971 to 1972 (NFHS numbers).

Court cases: Cohen v. Brown

  • In 1991 Brown University moved women's gymnastics and volleyball, and men's water polo and golf, from university-funded varsity to donor-funded club status. Women students sued in 1992. (Wikipedia, Cohen v. Brown University; opened). CRS describes the same events as Brown "cut" two women's and two men's teams, with athletes about 63% male while the student body was about 52% male. (CRS RL31709, 2005; opened)
  • The district court (Judge Raymond Pettine, 879 F. Supp. 185, D.R.I. 1995) found a violation. The First Circuit upheld the findings on November 21, 1996 (101 F.3d 155), approved the three-part test, and said Title IX does not mandate quotas. The Supreme Court denied review on April 21, 1997 (No. 96-1321). (Wikipedia, Cohen; CRS RL31709; Leagle, 520 U.S. 1186; all opened)

Enforcement

  • By the Department of Education's own history in GAO's 1999 report, it "has neither suspended nor terminated" any college's federal funds under Title IX, and OCR had never referred a college Title IX case to the Justice Department. (GAO/HEHS-00-24; opened)
  • OCR postsecondary compliance reviews averaged about 39 a year (fiscal 1982 to 1989), about 10 a year (1990 to 1995), and zero (1996 to 1998). OCR blamed resource limits and other priorities. Title IX complaints at colleges, fiscal 1982 to 1998: 5,227. (GAO/HEHS-00-24)
  • Of 74 athletics cases (1994 to 1998) where the compliance path was identified, schools used full accommodation of interests in 49, proportionality in 21 and program expansion in 4. (GAO/HEHS-00-24)
  • Enforcement in practice runs through private lawsuits and complaints. Individuals can sue (since 1979) and seek damages (since 1992). (CRS RL31709)

What this case shows

  1. A short rule, written by people most citizens never met, changed daily life. The statute is silent on sports. The athletics rule came from HEW's 1975 regulation after a congressional fight over the Javits and Tower amendments, and the working test came from a 1979 agency interpretation. Ordinary families could read a statute that never said "sports" and still find their daughters on teams a decade later. (Wikipedia; CRS IF12325)
  2. Participation grew sharply, with a legal channel for ordinary people to push. High school girls went from 294,015 to more than 3.2 million. The Franklin and Cohen cases let students sue. Cohen was brought by Brown athletes and decided in federal court. (NFHS; Wikipedia, Cohen; CRS)
  3. The benefits may go beyond sport. Betsey Stevenson used differences between states in boys' pre-1972 participation to estimate effects. She found a 10-point rise in girls' state sports participation led to about a 1-point rise in female college attendance and a 1 to 2 point rise in female labor force participation. (Stevenson, Review of Economics and Statistics, 2010, via Penn repository; abstract opened)
  4. Enforcement ran mostly through complaints and lawsuits, not federal sanctions. GAO found no fund cutoffs and no Justice Department referrals for colleges, and OCR reviews fell to zero in 1996 to 1998. Who got compliance depended on who complained and who could afford a lawyer. (GAO/HEHS-00-24)
  5. Who was included is still uneven. The Women's Sports Foundation, citing a 2015 National Women's Law Center report, says girls at majority Black or Hispanic high schools have 67% of the opportunities their male peers have, compared with 82% at heavily white schools. It also says only 14% of female NCAA athletes are people of color. (WSF; advocacy source, reports not opened)

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: Title IX forced schools to cut men's teams, wrestling above all. Critics (such as the American Sports Council, as summarized by Wikipedia) say the proportionality prong works as a quota. Data point: GAO found men's wrestling teams fell from 428 to 257 and wrestlers from 9,214 to 6,566 between 1981 to 1982 and 1998 to 1999. Of 272 schools that dropped men's teams in 1992 to 1993 through 1999 to 2000, 33% cited insufficient student interest, 31% cited gender equity needs, and 30% cited budget reallocation. In Division I-A, 54% cited gender equity. Mitigation: GAO also found men's teams rose by 36 overall (9,113 to 9,149) and men's athletes rose 5%, while women's teams rose 66%. 963 schools added teams and 307 dropped at least one. The Department's 1996 text says "nothing in the three-part test requires an institution to eliminate participation opportunities for men," and its 2003 clarification called cutting teams disfavored. We report both sides and do not give a net verdict. (GAO-01-297; CRS RL31709)
  2. Objection: Cuts were a budget choice, not a legal requirement. A 2023 study of 85 Division I institutions (2007 to 2014) by Anne Marx, Joel Cormier and Roman Ciapalo found that money from eliminated men's sports went mainly to football and men's basketball. They wrote that administrators' Title IX explanations were "not supported by the data." (Journal of Intercollegiate Sport, 2023; abstract opened). Mitigation: this is one study, limited to Division I and those years, and it does not say whether schools felt pressure from the proportionality test. GAO's survey shows 31% of schools did cite gender equity. The two findings can both be true.
  3. Objection: Proportionality ignores differences in interest. The third prong, and the 2005 web-survey clarification, were meant to let schools measure interest. Critics said surveys that count non-response as no interest were flawed (Wikipedia notes the NCAA and others objected), and OCR dropped the 2005 clarification in 2010. The 2003 Commission's majority recommendations included interest surveys, and two members dissented on parts. (CRS RL31709; Wikipedia) Mitigation: we show the dispute and the rollback; the full surveys and commission minority views were not read.
  4. Objection: The law is not enforced, so the story of success is overstated. GAO's finding is that no funds were ever pulled from a college. Women's Sports Foundation says 86% of NCAA institutions gave male athletes higher rates of opportunity than their enrollment would justify and about 60,000 opportunities were missing in 2019 to 2020 (advocacy source). The 2025 federal reorganization reportedly halved OCR staff (CRS). Mitigation: participation grew anyway, and the legal route through lawsuits (Franklin, Cohen) is real. We do not call either a success or a failure.
  5. Objection: Participation gains may reflect culture, not law. GAO said it could not isolate the law's effect from other factors. Mitigation: Stevenson's state-level design is built to separate the two, but it covers high school in the 1970s only.
  6. Objection: Current disputes over eligibility rules for girls' and women's teams are not covered. True. A later executive order and withdrawn proposed rules are mentioned by CRS only. We do not take a side and did not research that dispute here.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • The 1975 regulation's effective date and the school compliance deadlines were not verified. The eCFR page lists no compliance date. Unverified.
  • The 1979 Policy Interpretation text (44 Federal Register 71413) and the 1996 clarification letter were not opened; we rely on CRS summaries.
  • The Department of Education OCR athletics page failed to load; the 2010 rescission date comes from Wikipedia only. Unverified against OCR.
  • Wikipedia's figures for men's teams lost 1981 to 1999 (171 wrestling; 84 tennis; 56 gymnastics; 27 track; 25 swimming) were not checked beyond the wrestling number, which GAO confirms (428 to 257).
  • The Women's Sports Foundation figures (67% versus 82%; 60,000 missing opportunities; 14% of female NCAA athletes of color) come from an advocacy page. We did not open the underlying National Women's Law Center report or NCAA data. Unverified.
  • Whether the 2023 Marx study's finding holds for Division II and III, or for high schools, is unknown.
  • Spending data after 1998, and the Equity in Athletics Disclosure Act records by sport, were not read.
  • The 2025 OCR staff cut and any change in complaint handling since then rests on one CRS sentence ("reportedly").
  • The full Commission on Opportunity in Athletics report (2003) and its minority statement were not read.
  • Current eligibility disputes over girls' teams were not researched.
  • Evidence that would change our reading: a funds-termination record after 1999; a study showing men's cuts were mainly driven by the proportionality test across all divisions; or new participation data by race and school income from the Civil Rights Data Collection.
