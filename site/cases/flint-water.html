Flint water crisis (Flint, Michigan, 2014 to 2023)

Evidence first. Conclusions revisable.

Descend into the record ↓
Sources checked 2026-10-19 · Preliminary research, not a final verdict
Report for revisionDownload research packet

Members only. Sign in to send a correction.

In April 2014 a state-appointed emergency manager's administration switched Flint's water to the Flint River without corrosion control, residents and outside scientists exposed the lead problem over state denials, official reviews placed primary responsibility on state agencies, and criminal prosecutions ended in 2023 without convictions of the senior officials charged while a $626 million civil settlement was approved in 2021.

In brief

In April 2014 Flint switched its drinking water from Detroit's system to the Flint River. The decision was made under a state-appointed emergency manager, not by the elected city government, and the water was not treated to prevent lead from leaching from old pipes. In 2015 a resident, an EPA staffer, a Virginia Tech team and a Flint pediatrician showed that lead levels were high, while a state agency spokesman said residents could relax. A task force the governor appointed wrote in 2016 that the state environmental agency bore primary responsibility and had worked to discredit those who raised concerns. The federal EPA's inspector general found the agency had enough information to act by June 2015 and did not. A Legionnaires' outbreak with 12 official deaths was announced about 18 months after the first diagnoses. A $626 million settlement was approved in 2021. Criminal charges against officials, including a former governor, ended in 2022 and 2023 after the state Supreme Court held that a one-person grand jury could not issue indictments. What ordinary people could do was gather evidence themselves and push. What they could not do was vote on the decision, because the law that moved it away from them was one they had rejected at the ballot box two years earlier.

The record

This case is about who decided, who was told, and whether people could check or undo the decision. Flint residents are the public here. Numbers come from official reports or from named secondary sources, and the source is given each time.

What we could and could not open. Primary sources we opened: the Flint Water Advisory Task Force final report (March 21, 2016, michigan.gov); the EPA Office of Inspector General report 17-P-0004 (Oct. 20, 2016); the Michigan Supreme Court opinion in People v Peeler, People v Baird and People v Lyon (June 28, 2022); and the Michigan Department of Civil Rights study guide to the Civil Rights Commission report (Feb. 2017). We could NOT open the Civil Rights Commission's full report (fetch error), the House Oversight hearing transcript of March 17, 2016 (NPR page blocked by robots rules), or the text of Public Act 436 of 2012 beyond one section. Everything else is secondary. Where counts differ across sources we say so.

What the official reviews found

Flint Water Advisory Task Force (March 2016). The Task Force was appointed by the governor. Its executive summary (opened) says MDEQ "failed in its fundamental responsibility to effectively enforce drinking water regulations" and that MDEQ and the health department (MDHHS) repeatedly worked to discredit people raising concerns (p. 1). Finding F-1: MDEQ "bears primary responsibility for the water contamination in Flint" (p. 6). F-3: MDEQ "misinterpreted the LCR [Lead and Copper Rule] and misapplied its requirements" (p. 6); the report says MDEQ wrongly let Flint run two six-month monitoring rounds before deciding on corrosion control (p. 17). F-7: MDHHS "bears ultimate responsibility" for coordinating childhood lead follow-up (p. 7). F-12: ultimate accountability for executive branch decisions rests with the Governor (p. 7). F-14: the Governor's office relied on incorrect information from MDEQ and MDHHS until October 2015. F-18: "Emergency managers, not locally elected officials, made the decision to switch to the Flint River" (p. 7). F-36 calls the crisis "a clear case of environmental injustice" (p. 9). The summary also says state-appointed emergency managers "replaced local representative decision-making in Flint," removing checks and public accountability (p. 1), and credits residents, independent experts and the press for uncovering the crisis (p. 1). The cover letter says the causes lie "primarily at the feet of the state."

EPA Office of Inspector General (Oct. 20, 2016). The OIG found EPA Region 5 did not issue an SDWA Section 1431 emergency order in 2015 although it had authority and enough information "as early as June 2015." The Region concluded the state's ongoing activities were a "jurisdictional bar." The Region briefed EPA's enforcement office (OECA) only in September 2015, and OECA recommended action that Region 5 declined. The OIG recommended updating the 1991 guidance and training staff.

Health and disputed numbers

  • Lead. The Hanna-Attisha numbers above come from Wikipedia and were not checked against her published study.
  • Legionnaires' disease. Counts differ by source. Snyder's January 2016 announcement was 87 cases, 12 deaths (Wikipedia). PBS Frontline (2019) says the state reported 90 cases and 12 deaths, counting only deaths in hospital or within 30 days of discharge, and that its own analysis found at least 20 deaths among 78 patients who survived the initial diagnosis, weeks to years later. Becker's (2017) says 92 confirmed cases and 12 deaths. Which count to use depends on the definition; we use the official state count of 12 deaths and note the dispute.
  • Cause of the outbreak. A 23-member scientific team's PNAS paper attributed 80 percent of cases to the switch (Frontline). The state called the findings "inaccurate" and "incomplete" because samples and tests were missing, and now attributes the outbreak to a single source, McLaren Flint hospital, which denies it (Frontline). Marc Edwards' unpublished lab study released to CNN said the river switch without corrosion control let Legionella grow; MDHHS said no water samples from the city system were genetically linked to cases (Becker's). We did not open the PNAS paper.
  • What the state knew and when. County and state health officials knew of a rise in cases in summer 2014; in February 2015 clinical guidance went to about 15 staff at three hospitals and a clinic; Snyder announced the outbreak in January 2016, about 18 months after the first diagnosis, saying he had just learned of it. The state said it lacked enough information to warn the public earlier and had no legal duty to issue a public notice (Frontline).

Who knew what: the governor's account

Snyder told House Oversight on March 17, 2016 that MDEQ repeatedly said the water was safe and that he did not learn of the contamination until October 1, 2015 (SEJ page; NBC). NBC reports that he later said he was briefed on Sept. 28, 2015 and that a staff briefing had told him there was no real problem. He said he was misled for over a year and that it was a failure of government at all levels. At the hearing the Oversight Committee's ranking member Elijah Cummings (D-MD) is reported by Roll Call to have told Snyder "you lied" (headline only; not opened). The Task Force found the Governor's office relied on wrong information from the agencies "until October 2015" (F-14) but also placed ultimate accountability for executive decisions on the Governor (F-12).

Courts and criminal cases

  • Counts. Wikipedia says 15 criminal cases were filed and only one minor conviction resulted, a probation sentence for former health official Corinne Miller. Frontline (April 2019) says seven defendants had taken plea deals and six cases were pending. These two statements are not easy to reconcile from what we read; the plea outcomes (misdemeanors and later vacatur) were not verified.
  • Nessel's 2021 charges. Seven former officials (Nick Lyon, Nancy Peeler, Eden Wells, Jarrod Agen, Richard Baird, Gerald Ambrose, Darnell Earley) were charged via a one-person grand jury (Bridge Michigan). Snyder was charged with misdemeanors (Bridge; Wikipedia counts the charges at 41 across nine defendants).
  • June 28, 2022: People v Peeler, Baird and Lyon. The Michigan Supreme Court's unanimous opinion (Chief Justice Bridget McCormack; Justice Elizabeth Clement did not participate because she had been Snyder's chief legal counsel; Justice Richard Bernstein concurred separately) held that "if a criminal process begins with a one-man grand jury, the accused is entitled to a preliminary examination before being brought to trial" and that the statutes authorize a judge to investigate and issue warrants "But they do not authorize the judge to issue indictments." Lyon's lawyer John Bursch compared the tactics to the "star chamber" (Michigan Public). Snyder's team said the Attorney General's office "egregiously mishandled these cases."
  • Sept. 20, 2023. The Court declined to revisit dismissals. The prosecution team, co-led by Wayne County Prosecutor Kym Worthy and Solicitor General Fadwa Hammoud, said the ruling was "not based on the facts of this case" and defended the one-person grand jury. Baird's lawyer Randall Levine said his client was "elated." Flint Mayor Sheldon Neeley said the decision eroded public trust in government checks and balances. Bridge Michigan said Snyder's misdemeanor case and the appeal over Howard Croft remained active at the time of that article. The final status of those two matters is unverified.
  • Civil. $626 million approved Nov. 10, 2021 (OPB/AP). Attorney fees of up to $200 million were sought and deferred. Per-person allocation not verified. Wikipedia says an earlier $600 million figure grew to $641 million by November 2020; OPB gives $626 million total at approval; we use $626 million.

Pipe replacement

Wikipedia reports that 27,133 lines had been excavated and 10,059 lead pipes replaced by July 16, 2021, and that the last lead pipe was replaced on July 1, 2025. We did not verify these figures against the city or state.

What this case shows

  1. The decision was made by people residents did not elect, after residents had voted against the law that allowed it. The Task Force says emergency managers, not elected officials, chose the river and that emergency management "removed checks, balances, and public accountability." Michigan voters had rejected Public Act 4 in November 2012 by 52.67 to 47.33 percent. A replacement law followed within about a month (AlterNet, advocacy; the Task Force only mentions PA 436). Whether PA 436 was a lawful and different response or a work-around is contested (see Section 3), but the central question is plain: when a city loses its ability to vote on core decisions, what is the fix?
  2. Residents and outside scientists, not agencies, exposed the problem. The Task Force credits "residents, independent experts, and the press." The chain runs from Walters (Feb. 2015) to Del Toral to Edwards (Sept. 8, 2015) to Hanna-Attisha (Sept. 24, 2015). The state agency spokesman said residents "can relax" on July 13, 2015, per Wikipedia. Public data collected by non-experts changed official action.
  3. The Task Force says the agencies tried to discredit people raising concerns. That is the sharpest accountability finding for a project about disclosure. It was written by a body the governor appointed, so it cannot be dismissed as hostile.
  4. Federal oversight was also late. The EPA OIG found Region 5 had authority and enough information by June 2015 and did not act until January 2016. Failure was not only local or state. A voter looking for who to blame or who to ask to fix it faced overlapping agencies each pointing to another.
  5. The health record itself was withheld or incomplete. The Legionnaires' outbreak was announced about 18 months after the first diagnoses and the public notice came from the governor in January 2016. The state said it had no legal duty to give public notice. A disclosure rule on outbreak notification is a concrete reform voters could ask for.
  6. Accountability came through civil settlement more than criminal law. The $626 million settlement was approved in 2021; the criminal cases ended without convictions of the senior officials, after the Supreme Court found a procedural defect. Whatever one thinks of the procedure, the public record of who was responsible is mostly in the Task Force and EPA OIG reports and in the settlement, not in a trial. A trial record that never happened is a disclosure gap.
  7. The Civil Rights Commission raises a question about equal treatment. It found that "decades of systemic racism" shaped city policy and played "a crucial role," while finding no intentional outcome. The Task Force calls it "a clear case of environmental injustice." This tie to our question is about whether all residents had an equal voice in the decision, not about intent of any individual.

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: The state agencies made honest mistakes, not cover-ups. Support: Snyder said he was misled; the Task Force F-14 says the Governor's office relied on wrong information. MDEQ's rule interpretation (two six-month monitoring rounds) is described by the Task Force as a misreading (F-3), which implies error rather than intent. Mitigation: The Task Force also found agencies "repeatedly worked to discredit" those who raised concerns (p. 1), which goes beyond error. Both are recorded. Whether any individual acted knowingly is exactly what the criminal cases would have tested, and they did not reach trial on the merits.
  2. Objection: The prosecutions were flawed, and dismissal protects the rule of law. Support: the unanimous Michigan Supreme Court opinion (McCormack); Bursch's "star chamber" comparison; Levine's "antiquated process." Mitigation: Agreed that the Court's holding is about process. The prosecution team (Worthy, Hammoud) argued the ruling was not based on the facts, and Mayor Neeley said it eroded public trust. We present both and do not suggest guilt or innocence. A dismissal is not a finding of innocence or of guilt.
  3. Objection: The Task Force is state-appointed, so its blame of the state is more credible, not less; but the state's own agencies disagree with parts of it. Support: MDHHS disputes the link between the water and Legionnaires' cases and attributes the outbreak to McLaren Flint hospital, which denies it. Mitigation: We state the dispute, and say the state counts 12 deaths and others count more.
  4. Objection: The decision to leave Detroit's system followed local and cost pressures, not secret motives. Support: the Task Force describes the 2013 KWA agreement and the interim river plan. Wikipedia, the PBS and other pieces mention cost savings. The date of the council vote and who bore responsibility were disputed publicly in 2016: a UPI headline reports a former mayor calling the blame on the city council "blatantly false." Mitigation: We did not open that article, and the Task Force report did not give the vote count we were looking for. We attribute responsibility to emergency managers because the Task Force does (F-18) and flag the council role as unverified.
  5. Objection: The Civil Rights Commission's finding is about systems, not intent. Support: Great Lakes Now reports the Commission said decision-makers were not racists. Residents also criticized the report as underwhelming (Claire McClinton, Flint resident). Mitigation: Agreed. We quote the Commission as saying systemic failures "played a crucial role" and that outcomes were not found to be intentional, and we do not say anyone acted with racial intent.
  6. Objection: EPA also failed, so the focus on the state is selective. Mitigation: Agreed, and we include it. The Task Force says the state bears primary accountability, the OIG says EPA Region 5 could have acted by June 2015; both are in the record.
  7. Objection: The emergency manager law exists to rescue insolvent cities. Support: Snyder maintains that EM laws are needed for financially insolvent municipalities (AlterNet, paraphrasing his position). Mitigation: Both positions stand. The Task Force recommends reviewing PA 436 (R-20, p. 12) but does not say the law should be repealed.
  8. Objection: Numbers conflict, so the record is unreliable. Mitigation: We give counts by named source (12 deaths official; 20 or more per Frontline's own analysis; 87, 90 or 92 cases depending on source).

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • Civil Rights Commission report. We did not open the full report. The study guide says "crucial role"; we have not confirmed the full report's wording. Open the report on michigan.gov.
  • Public Act 436 and the 2012 referendum. The 52.67 to 47.33 result is from Wikipedia. The link between the referendum, PA 436 and the emergency manager's authority in Flint is described in an advocacy source and in the Task Force report only by a recommendation. Whether PA 436 included a provision shielding it from referendum is unverified (we checked one section only). Open the statute and the Legislature's analysis.
  • Flint's emergency management dates and which EM held office when. The Task Force names Kurtz (2013) and Earley (2014); other emergency managers and the dates they served are unverified.
  • The 2013 city council vote. Not found in the Task Force report; a 2016 dispute with a former mayor was seen only as a headline.
  • Snyder's March 2016 testimony. We did not open a transcript. The quotes come from NBC and SEJ. The Cummings "you lied" quotation is from a headline only.
  • Criminal outcomes. The count of pleas, convictions and dismissals differs between Wikipedia and Frontline. Snyder's misdemeanor case and the Croft appeal were active as of the Bridge article. Their final status is unverified. Also unverified: Wikipedia's 41 counts and the date of the Nessel charges beyond Jan. 13, 2021.
  • Legionnaires' cause and counts. The PNAS paper, the state's McLaren finding and Edwards's study were not opened.
  • Lead data. Hanna-Attisha's numbers and Edwards' 40 percent figure come from Wikipedia. Open the studies.
  • Pipe replacement progress. Not verified. The city's figures should be checked.
  • Settlement distribution. How the $626 million was divided, and the fee award, are unverified.
  • EPA's Del Toral memo. The OIG text we read did not mention it by name; Wikipedia dates a June 24, 2015 memo. Check.
  • Other responsible parties. Private engineering firms and a later settlement with them were not examined.
  • If the full Civil Rights Commission report showed it found intent, argument 7 would change. If a court record showed a decision-maker knew of contamination and concealed it, argument 3 would strengthen; the dismissals mean that record was not produced at trial.
