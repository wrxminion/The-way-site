Rural Free Delivery mail service (United States, 1892 to 1930)

Evidence first. Conclusions revisable.

After a small trial in West Virginia in 1896, Congress made home mail delivery to farms permanent in 1902, which cut thousands of small post offices, pushed road improvement and opened rural homes to catalogs and newspapers, while route placement followed politics, cost, road quality and merchant opposition.

In brief

In the 1890s, most farm families had to travel to a post office for their mail. Congress gave $10,000 in 1893 for a trial, and carriers began riding routes in West Virginia on October 1, 1896. After a county-wide test in Carroll County, Maryland, where 63 small post offices closed, Congress made the service permanent on July 1, 1902. To get a route, farmers petitioned their congressman, and the route needed passable roads and at least 100 households. Researchers have found that routes went disproportionately to districts where the party in power needed votes. Catalogs, newspapers and later parcel post reached farm homes, local merchants and express companies lost business, and the number of small post offices fell after peaking at 76,945 in 1901. The service also pushed communities to improve roads. People could petition and vote for rural delivery, but who got a route depended on roads, politics and paperwork.

The record

Before the service

  • Rural families had to collect mail from distant post offices or pay private express companies. The National Grange lobbied for more than 20 years for free rural delivery, and local shopkeepers worried about mail-order competition and tried to delay it. (Britannica, Rural Free Delivery; opened)
  • In 1892 the House Committee on Post Office and Post Roads rejected a bill priced at $6 million and also rejected a $100,000 amendment. Postmaster General John Wanamaker had asked for $6 million in 1893 and received $10,000. (USPS Historian, Rural Free Delivery, Aug. 2013; Richmond Fed, Econ Focus, 2025; both opened)
  • Representative Tom Watson of Georgia sponsored the 1893 appropriation. Postmaster General Wilson S. Bissell declined to run the experiment, citing a $20 million cost estimate that USPS later calls a guess. His successor William L. Wilson, from West Virginia, agreed once funds were provided. (USPS history, web version and PDF)

The trial and the law

  • Service began October 1, 1896, in Charles Town, Halltown and Uvilla, West Virginia. (USPS; Wikipedia, Rural Free Delivery)
  • In December 1899 Carroll County, Maryland, became a county-wide test: 63 small post offices closed and 35 star route and messenger routes ended. The Richmond Fed reports postal revenue rose 23% and net cost was $236, under superintendent August Machen. (USPS; Richmond Fed)
  • A petition process was set up in 1898. Any group of farmers could petition their congressman, with a description of the community and its roads. A route needed at least 100 households, 20 to 25 miles, and year-round passable roads (Britannica; Richmond Fed). From 1902 customers had to use mailboxes approved by the Postmaster General. (USPS)
  • Rural free delivery became permanent on July 1, 1902. The word "free" was dropped from the name in 1906. (USPS)
  • Parcel post began in 1913. Before that, rural carriers could carry packages of up to four pounds, and heavier packages went through private express companies. (USPS; Wikipedia)
  • The Rural Post Roads Act of 1916 authorized federal funds for rural post roads. (Wikipedia)

Who got routes, and why

  • Samuel Kernell and Michael McDonald found that Republicans who unseated Democratic incumbents in 1898 received 11 times as many new routes as Democrats who unseated Republican incumbents, and that the Post Office also weighed district literacy rates. (Richmond Fed summary of Kernell and McDonald, 1999; the original article was not opened.)
  • Kernell argued Congress, not postal reformers, drove expansion. Daniel Carpenter argued reform minded postal officials drove it. (Student notes on Kernell, 2001; unreliable, original not opened.)
  • Elisabeth Perlman and Steven Sprick Schuster found more routes in a county were linked to higher minor party vote shares outside the South, and to representatives voting closer to constituents' views where newspapers circulated. James Feigenbaum and Martin Rotemberg argued RFD lowered advertising costs; their example is Vick's VapoRub after a route reached Selma, North Carolina, in 1903. (Richmond Fed summary; original papers not opened.)

Costs and system effects

  • Mail-order catalogs reached farm homes and daily newspapers brought national news. Rural delivery also spurred the good roads movement because passable roads were a service requirement. (Encyclopedia.com; USPS)
  • Carpenter (2000) argued the Post Office's return to profit around 1911 came from urban efficiency, because rural delivery cost more to run than it earned (Richmond Fed summary).
  • In 2012 about 41 million homes and businesses got rural carrier service (USPS). In 2022, 63% of rural post offices failed to cover their costs (Richmond Fed).

What this case shows

  1. Ordinary people petitioned and Congress voted. Customers sent more than 10,000 petitions during the six year experiment and the service was then made permanent by Congress. People with a petition and a congressman could shape the map. (USPS; Britannica)
  2. Who was reached depended on politics and paperwork. Routes needed roads, 100 households and a petition, and the Kernell and McDonald finding says electoral gain shaped which districts got routes first. Inclusion was not equal. (Richmond Fed)
  3. Others paid or lost. About 1,000 post offices fewer existed by 1902, 63 of 94 offices closed in Carroll County, express companies and town merchants lost business, and carriers supplied their own horses and wagons. The benefit to farm families came with a cost to small town institutions and workers. (USPS; Wikipedia)
  4. Information access changed what voters knew. Daily papers, catalogs and market prices arrived at the door. Perlman and Sprick Schuster link this to voting behavior, though the result is a working finding we did not read in full. (Richmond Fed)
  5. Public choice on roads. Because roads were required for service, a mail rule became a road rule. Local people had to improve roads to qualify. (USPS)

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: It was too costly. Critics said cost was prohibitive and farm journals questioned it. Costs rose from $1.75 million in 1901 to $36.9 million in 1910 (one source). Carpenter argues rural delivery was not what made the department profitable. Mitigation: we show cost figures and note they are unverified; supporters point to the Carroll County net cost of $236 and higher revenues in the 1892 trial offices, which are single source claims.
  2. Objection: It was vote buying. Contemporary critics said so, and the Kernell and McDonald finding supports partisan targeting of routes. Mitigation: we state the finding with its source and also note the Grange and farmers' groups sought the service for years. Both can be true.
  3. Objection: Business and information benefits may reflect other changes. Railroads, higher incomes, telephones and roads moved at the same time. Mitigation: we call the effects associated, not caused. The Feigenbaum and Rotemberg and Perlman and Sprick Schuster papers use county variation, and we did not open them.
  4. Objection: Small town merchants and postmasters were harmed. Yes. Merchants feared catalogs and postmasters lost offices. Mitigation: we include them as costs and as opponents who delayed service by about three years (Encyclopedia.com).
  5. Objection: Wikipedia and web summaries are weak sources. Mitigation: we use USPS, Britannica and a Federal Reserve Bank publication for key claims, and mark Wikipedia only claims in the tables.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • The 1893 appropriation and the 1902 permanence were not checked against statute text or the Postmaster General's reports. The year of the first $10,000 differs by source (1891 or 1893).
  • Counts of routes, carriers and cost by year come from Wikipedia, Encyclopedia.com and a Federal Reserve publication, and are not reconciled with postal reports.
  • The original studies by Kernell and McDonald (1999), Carpenter, Perlman and Sprick Schuster (2016), and Feigenbaum and Rotemberg were known only through the Richmond Fed summary. Their exact methods are unverified.
  • Whether race or region limited access (Southern roads, literacy testing of districts) was not found in any opened source. The Kernell and McDonald literacy factor needs the original article.
  • The Carroll County result (revenue up 23%, net cost $236) is from one source.
  • The 2022 rural post office cost figure (63%) comes from one source and may follow a different accounting than 1900s costs.
  • The "once a day, six days a week" and tri-weekly facts show some customers got less service; how many in 1902 is unknown.
  • A study showing route placement did not follow politics, or that business effects vanish once railroads are controlled, would change our framing.
  • A WebFetch limit was hit during research, so the Wikipedia page was read once and other primary documents (HathiTrust annual reports) were not opened.
