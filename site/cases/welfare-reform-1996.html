The 1996 law ended the federal cash-aid entitlement and replaced it with a fixed state block grant, a 60-month limit and work rules; caseloads fell by more than half within four years, and researchers still disagree about what that meant for poor children.

In brief

In August 1996 President Clinton signed a law that replaced the federal cash aid program for poor families with state block grants, a 60-month limit on federally funded aid and work requirements. The House passed the final bill 328 to 101 and the Senate 78 to 21. The statute says it does not entitle any family to assistance. Average monthly caseloads fell from 4,543,397 families in fiscal 1996 to 2,264,806 in 2000. The block grant has stayed at $16.5 billion a year, and in 2020 states used about 22% of TANF funds for basic cash assistance. Researchers still disagree about what happened to poor children. One Brookings scholar argues that work supports cut single-mother poverty from 48 to 24 percent by 2013, while two Stanford-based researchers report that deep poverty rose. The record shows a law that voters could see being passed, and a set of later effects that depended on state choices few residents followed.

The record

What the law did

  • The Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996 (Public Law 104-193) replaced Aid to Families with Dependent Children (AFDC) with Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF). (Public Law 104-193, govinfo; opened, all but a small tail of the text)
  • Section 401(b): "This part shall not be interpreted to entitle any individual or family to assistance." Section 401(a) lists purposes that include job preparation, work, and marriage, reducing out-of-wedlock pregnancies, and encouraging two-parent families. Section 101(1): "Marriage is the foundation of a successful society."
  • Section 403(a)(1): each eligible state gets a "State family assistance grant," set as the greatest of three amounts based on earlier federal payments (fiscal years 1992 to 1994, fiscal year 1994, or the first three quarters of fiscal year 1995). The dollar total per year was not read from the statute; see the table below for the figure we opened.
  • Section 408(a)(7)(A): a state "shall not use any part of the grant to provide assistance to a family that includes an adult" who has received federally funded assistance "for 60 months (whether or not consecutive)." Section 408(a)(7)(C): a state may exempt families for hardship or abuse, but exemptions may not exceed 20 percent of the average monthly caseload.
  • Section 402(a)(1)(A)(ii): the state plan must require a parent or caretaker to work once the state deems them ready, or after 24 months of assistance, whichever is earlier.
  • Section 407(a): required participation rates for all families rise from 25% (1997) to 50% (2002 and after); for two-parent families the rate is 75% in 1997 and 1998 and 90% from 1999. Section 407(b)(3)(A) lets the Secretary reduce the required rate for caseload declines not required by federal law. The statute's effect: states that cut caseloads could meet the rate with fewer people working.

  • From 1996 to 2000 the average monthly family count fell from 4,543,397 to 2,264,806, about 50%. That is our arithmetic from the table, not a figure the source states.

Money and reach

  • The federal block grant has been fixed at $16.5 billion a year since 1996, and the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities says its real value fell about 40 percent (no date given for that figure). States also must spend about $15 billion a year of their own money (about $15 billion in 2020, maintenance of effort). (CBPP, Policy Basics: An Introduction to TANF; opened. CBPP is an advocacy-leaning think tank; its numbers are secondary.)
  • CBPP: about 68 of every 100 poor families received TANF in 1996, about 21 of every 100 in 2020, and in 2020 fourteen states reached 10 or fewer per 100. (same page)
  • State use of funds, fiscal year 2020, total $31.5 billion (CBPP): basic assistance $7.1 billion (22%), child care $5.2 billion (17%), other $4.1 billion (13%), refundable tax credits $2.8 billion (9%), pre-K and Head Start $2.7 billion (9%), work, education and training $3.0 billion (10%), program management $3.2 billion (10%), child welfare $2.6 billion (8%), work supports $0.8 billion (2%). Fifteen states spent 10% or less of TANF funds on basic assistance. (same page)

Poverty data

  • Census, 2023: the official poverty rate was 11.1% (36.8 million people), and the Supplemental Poverty Measure child rate was 13.7%, up 1.3 points from 2022. We did not open a table giving the official child poverty rate for 1996 or 2023, so we make no before and after child poverty comparison here. (Census P60-283; opened in part)

What studies say: two readings

Reading Claim Source
Optimistic The "$2 a day" estimate "is based on survey data that omits much of TANF income"; income-based poverty is almost 50 percent higher than consumption-based poverty (citing Meyer and Sullivan); work support benefits cut the poverty rate for single mothers and children from 48 to 24 percent in 2013; "It makes little sense to focus only on cash." Ron Haskins, Brookings, Sept. 14, 2015
Critical Experiments from as early as 2000 found welfare-reform-style programs reduced overall poverty but increased deep poverty; "$2-a-day poverty" rose after reform, likely tripling or quadrupling for children in single-mother families rather than doubling; correcting survey underreporting made the decline "more severe"; SNAP administrative data support it. H. Luke Shaefer and Kathryn Edin, Stanford Center on Poverty and Inequality, Jan. 2018

(Brookings, Haskins; Stanford CPI, Shaefer and Edin; both opened.) Haskins also wrote that "Calling attention to the plight of disconnected mothers is an appropriate and important task for researchers." Neither side's underlying datasets or the original Meyer and Sullivan papers were opened.

What this case shows

  1. The vote was recorded and public. Both chambers passed the conference report by wide margins (328 to 101; 78 to 21) and the President's reservations were published the day he signed. This is our question in plain form: people could see who voted how. The party breakdown shows the House Democrats split 98 to 98; we draw no conclusion about motive. (House Clerk; Senate roll call; signing statement)
  2. The rules changed who is entitled to what. Section 401(b) removed individual entitlement. After that, a state could decide what to fund, and families could not sue for aid as a right. Ordinary people could vote on the federal law once; day-to-day decisions moved to state plans. (P.L. 104-193)
  3. The fixed grant shifted risk to states and families. The grant stayed at $16.5 billion and lost about 40 percent in real value (CBPP). States use funds for many things beyond cash: in 2020 only 22% went to basic assistance. Whether that is good or bad is a judgment; the record shows where the money went. (CBPP)
  4. Caseload fell by about half while poverty findings split. Caseload fell from 4.54 million families to 2.26 million from 1996 to 2000. Haskins says work supports cut single-mother poverty from 48 to 24 percent by 2013; Shaefer and Edin say deep poverty rose. Both claims are in print and neither is settled in the sources we opened. (ACF table; Brookings; Stanford)
  5. Some groups had a narrower voice. The signing statement names legal immigrants and childless adults on food stamps as groups the President objected to excluding. They were affected by the same bill but not its main subject. (signing statement)

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: Work requirements raised employment and ended dependency. Mitigation: We cite Haskins' reading in full. We did not open employment-rate series, so we state no employment figures. The statute's participation-rate credit for caseload decline means falling caseloads cannot alone prove more people worked.
  2. Objection: Caseload decline reflects the strong late-1990s economy, not the law. Mitigation: We did not open studies separating the two causes, so we state both are possible and leave it unresolved. See Section 5.
  3. Objection: The $2-a-day finding is a survey artifact. Mitigation: Haskins argues exactly this. Shaefer and Edin respond that corrections for underreporting make the finding stronger. We show both and do not pick.
  4. Objection: Fixed block grants gave states flexibility to innovate. Mitigation: Section 401(a) gives flexibility as a purpose. CBPP shows most states spend a smaller share on cash than before. Whether spending on child care, tax credits and pre-K helped poor families more than cash would is not answered by the sources we opened.
  5. Objection: Clinton signed the law and both parties voted for it, so criticism is partisan. Mitigation: The vote record shows both parties on both sides. We do not score any party or official, and we report that the President himself named parts of the bill he wanted changed.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • Employment of single mothers before and after 1996, from a primary series (BLS or Census). Not opened.
  • Child poverty rates for 1996 to 2023 from a Census table. Not opened.
  • How much of the caseload decline came from the economy versus the law. No study separating the two was opened.
  • The text of section 403's dollar total ($16.4 or $16.5 billion) and the year-by-year grant. Only CBPP's $16.5 billion was opened.
  • Sanction rates and how many families hit the 60-month limit. Not opened.
  • Current TANF caseload; the ACF 2022 caseload page held no totals when opened.
  • The Meyer and Sullivan consumption poverty papers and the original Edin and Shaefer book. Not opened.
  • Whether the 20 percent hardship exemption was ever fully used by states. Not opened.
