Love Canal (Niagara Falls, New York, 1942 to 2004)

Evidence first. Conclusions revisable.

A chemical company buried about 22,000 tons of waste in a canal, sold the land to a school board for $1, and families later lived and sent children to school on it; after residents organized in 1978, two federal emergencies and the Superfund law followed.

In brief

From 1942 to 1952, Hooker Chemical buried about 22,000 tons of waste in an abandoned canal in Niagara Falls, New York. In 1953 it sold the site to the school board for $1 and included a clause releasing the company from future claims. A school opened in 1955, and hundreds of homes were built nearby. In the 1970s, chemicals seeped into basements, and in August 1978 residents formed the Love Canal Homeowners Association. The state declared an emergency on August 2, and President Carter declared a federal emergency five days later. Congress held 13 hearings with 106 witnesses, and a second emergency in 1980 led to the relocation of hundreds more families. Congress passed the Superfund law that December. In 1995, Hooker's successor agreed to pay $129 million, and EPA removed the site from its priority list in 2004. The health effects remain debated. What the record shows is that families learned what lay beneath their homes largely because neighbors organized.

The record

The dumping and the sale

  • Hooker Chemical dumped waste in the old canal from 1942 to 1952. Quantity is reported three ways: about 21,800 short tons (Wikipedia; Levin Center), about 22,000 tons (EPA, Federal Register 2004), and about 25,000 tons from 1941 to 1954 (the court's finding in 1994). (Wikipedia, Love Canal; Levin Center, Portrait in Oversight: Love Canal; EPA, Federal Register, March 17, 2004; United States v. Hooker Chemicals & Plastics Corp., 850 F. Supp. 993 (W.D.N.Y. 1994); all opened)
  • On May 7, 1953, Hooker sold the site to the Niagara Falls Board of Education for $1, with a clause releasing the company from future liability. (University at Buffalo Love Canal collection, timeline; opened)
  • Hooker's position, as summarized by the Levin Center: the deed referred to the buried chemicals, the clay cover was legally sufficient at the time, and both sides agreed to the release. A city lawyer, Ralph Boniello, warned the board of "risk and possible liability" in accepting Hooker's terms. (Levin Center; University at Buffalo glossary; opened)
  • The 99th Street School opened in 1955; during construction in 1954 contractors found chemical seepage and the site was shifted. The board kept land for a school and sold the rest, and more than 800 homes were built near the canal. (Buffalo glossary; Levin Center)
  • The 1994 court found Hooker "definitely recognized that these materials were toxic" (quoting an expert) and knew by 1944 of DDM residue seeping to the surface, and that fencing was not installed in the southern and central sections. (U.S. v. Hooker, 1994, excerpt)

Residents, relocation, and the fight for it

  • The 1978 federal emergency funded relocation of 239 families in the two rings closest to the landfill (Levin Center) or 236 (UB timeline). The state bought more than 200 homes for almost $7 million (Levin Center). At least 700 families did not qualify, though tests found toxic substances in their homes. After the 1980 emergency, EPA says about 950 families in total were evacuated from a 10-block area. (EPA news release, Sept. 30, 2004; opened)
  • The path to the second emergency ran through public pressure: the December 1978 arrests, the 1980 detention of EPA officials, and congressional hearings. (UB timeline)
  • Lois Gibbs told Congress that drums had popped up "right on the baseball diamond." Elliot Lynch, a former chemist at the Niagara County Water District, testified he saw Hooker trucks dumping near the county water plant. (Levin Center)
  • Gibbs later founded the Citizens Clearing House for Hazardous Waste (1981), now the Center for Health, Environment and Justice. (Buffalo glossary)

Cleanup, court and delisting

  • EPA's 2004 notice: the Emergency Declaration Area was about 350 acres and the landfill cap about 40 acres. The remedy included a 1984 liner cap, leachate treatment, creek sediment work, and off-site disposal of stored waste bags (completed August 1999). EPA and the state said "all appropriate response actions under CERCLA have been implemented." Hazardous substances remain above unrestricted-use levels, so five-year reviews continue and deed restrictions apply to vacant parcels. (FR 2004; opened)
  • 1994: Judge John Curtin found Hooker liable under CERCLA and public nuisance earlier, documented "many specific instances of defendant's negligence," but denied the state's punitive damages claim because the state "failed to prove" reckless disregard. (U.S. v. Hooker, 1994, excerpt; the transfer analysis was cut off in the text we read)
  • 1995: Occidental Chemical, Hooker's successor, agreed to pay $129 million ($102 million to the Superfund, $27 million to the U.S. for FEMA), covering about $101 million in cleanup costs and $28 million in interest. The U.S. agreed to cover an additional $8 million to resolve Occidental's claim that federal agencies shared responsibility. (Justice Department release, Dec. 21, 1995; opened)
  • 2004: delisting; EPA's regional administrator Jane M. Kenny: "Decades later, Love Canal has become a symbol of our success under Superfund." (EPA release)

Health findings: contested

  • 1980 EPA chromosome study: 11 of 36 residents showed damage (UB timeline). Dante Picciano of Biogenics Corporation did the work and said he found clear evidence of damage. A Science review called it "close to zero scientific significance," and an EPA review panel disputed it (Christian Science Monitor column by Robert Cowen, June 25, 1980). (CSM; opened. The column is an opinion piece.)
  • 1983 CDC report (MMWR, May 27, 1983): 46 residents and 44 controls tested in 1981 to 1982 showed no significant differences in chromosome changes. The report lists limits: time since 1978 and no individual exposure measurements. (CDC MMWR; opened)
  • 2008 New York State Department of Health follow-up (6,026 of 6,181 residents traced): overall death and cancer totals were within expected ranges; congenital malformations were about twice the expected rate and were associated with childhood exposure; the authors caution about many comparisons, low power and exposure measured by proxy. (NYSDOH, Love Canal Follow-up Health Study, Oct. 2008; opened)
  • A 2010 Reason column by Ronald Bailey argues earlier claims of widespread illness were overstated, citing the state follow-up and doubts about Beverly Paigen's 1979 survey. (Reason; opened. A libertarian magazine, an opinion source.)

What this case shows

  1. The key fact was in a deed, not in public view. A liability release traveled with the land from a company to a school board. The 1953 buyers were a public body; the later families were not. The record shows a city lawyer warned of "risk and possible liability" yet the sale went ahead. (Buffalo glossary)
  2. Residents had to build their own evidence and their own voice. The Love Canal Homeowners Association formed in August 1978, and relocation expanded only after hearings, arrests and a detention of EPA officials. About 700 families first outside the line later got the second emergency. Public pressure, not a routine process, moved the boundary. (UB timeline; Levin Center)
  3. Open hearings produced a law. A House subcommittee held 13 hearings with 106 witnesses from October 1978 to June 1979, and Congress passed Superfund in December 1980. This is our question answered well: public record, then public rule. (Levin Center)
  4. Even the cleanup record is not simple. EPA says all response actions are complete, but waste remains above unrestricted-use levels, and deed restrictions bind later owners. People can read the controls; they still need to know to look. (FR 2004)
  5. Science and courts did not settle the harm quickly. The first chromosome study was criticized and a 1983 CDC study found no difference; a 2008 state study found a malformation signal and urged caution. Residents acted on a fear that years of data only partly confirmed. (CSM; CDC; NYSDOH)

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: Hooker disclosed the waste and sold the land in good faith. The deed mentioned the chemicals, and the board insisted on the purchase, with a lawyer's warning on record. Mitigation: We report Hooker's position. The 1994 court found negligence but not reckless disregard, and we do not call the company's conduct criminal. The court also found Hooker knew its wastes were toxic and knew of seepage by 1944.
  2. Objection: The school board and local government bore responsibility too. Mitigation: Yes. The board bought the site, built a school and sold the rest; local agencies approved development. The Occidental settlement also gave the U.S. an $8 million credit for the claim that federal agencies shared blame. We make no claim about who was most at fault.
  3. Objection: The health harm was overstated. Mitigation: The evidence is mixed and we show it: a criticized 1980 study, a null 1983 CDC study, and a 2008 state study that found total cancer within the expected range and malformations about twice the expected rate. Our question does not depend on proven harm; it depends on residents and officials acting without clear data, and on who could see the data.
  4. Objection: The system worked; it is a Superfund success story. Mitigation: EPA calls it a "symbol of our success." We include that. It also took two emergencies, a lawsuit begun in 1979 and settled in 1995, and citizen action.
  5. Objection: Wikipedia and local timelines are weak sources. Mitigation: We use Wikipedia for background and checked its claims against EPA, DOJ and the court. Where it conflicted (NPL date, $98 million figure), we followed the primary source or dropped the claim.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • The 1953 deed text and the board's meeting minutes were not opened. The exact wording of the liability clause is taken from secondary summaries. Unverified.
  • The second half of the 1994 court opinion (transfer analysis and conclusions) was not readable; the finding on what Hooker told the board is unverified.
  • Wikipedia's "$25 million to New York State, 1994" and "$98 million" figures were not verified; we omitted them.
  • The 239 versus 236 family count is unresolved.
  • The date and number of the 1984 class settlement ($20 million for 1,336 versus a medical fund for 1,328) were not reconciled.
  • The NPL listing year (1981 proposed, 1983 added per EPA FR) should be checked against the Federal Register notice of listing.
  • Governor's Panel report (Oct. 1980) and the Janerich 1981 cancer study were not read; the panel PDF was too large for our fetch tool.
  • We did not open the congressional hearing volumes (Eckhardt subcommittee, 1978 to 1980); quotes about them come from the Levin Center summary.
  • Whether the 1958 health department observation of chemical burns is documented in an original record: unverified.
