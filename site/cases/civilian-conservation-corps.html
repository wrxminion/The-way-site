Civilian Conservation Corps (United States, 1933 to 1942)

Evidence first. Conclusions revisable.

Descend into the record ↓
Sources checked 2026-10-19 · Preliminary research, not a final verdict

A New Deal program put about 3 million unmarried young men to work on forests, parks and erosion control for $30 a month, with $25 sent home, but its camps were mostly segregated by race, Black enrollment was held to about 10 percent or less, and women were left to a smaller, short-lived program.

In brief

In March 1933, Congress created the Civilian Conservation Corps. Over nine years more than 3 million unmarried young men joined, lived in Army-run camps, and earned $30 a month, $25 of which went home. They planted billions of trees, built trails and parks, and fought fires, and national park visits rose from 3.5 million in 1933 to 21 million in 1941. The law said no one could be discriminated against because of race. In practice, Black enrollment was about 10 percent or less, local agents in the South turned many Black applicants away, and in July 1935 the program directed complete segregation. Director Robert Fechner replied to the NAACP that "segregation is not discrimination." Women were not enrolled and had a smaller program of their own. The public could see the parks and the 82 percent approval in 1936 shows people liked what they saw. They could not easily see who was kept out.

The record

Creation and rules

  • Congress passed the Emergency Conservation Work Act on March 31, 1933 (Public No. 5, 73rd Congress). It authorized the President to put unemployed citizens to work on reforestation, fire and flood prevention, erosion control, and trail and road work in national parks and forests, and provided housing, subsistence, medical care and cash allowances. Section 1 says "no discrimination shall be made on account of race, color, or creed." The authority ran two years and was later extended. (Paige, The CCC and the National Park Service, Appendices, reproducing the Act; opened, through a summarizing fetch)
  • By an order dated April 5, 1933, President Franklin Roosevelt named Robert Fechner Director of Emergency Conservation Work and created an advisory council of representatives of the War, Agriculture, Interior and Labor departments. The first enrollee was inducted on April 7, 1933. The Army ran the camps. (Paige appendices; National Archives, Atlanta, finding aid; both opened)
  • Enrollees were unmarried, unemployed U.S. citizen men, aged 18 to 25 at the start (later 17 to 28, then 17 to 23). They received $30 a month, and $25 of that was a required allotment to a dependent. They also received housing, food, clothing and medical care. (Wikipedia, Civilian Conservation Corps; Living New Deal; both opened)
  • Britannica adds that unions first resisted the low wages, fearing employers would cut pay, and that most states deducted the $25 sent to families from relief benefits. (Britannica; opened; we did not check the state deduction claim against a state record)

Scale and cost

  • More than 3 million men took part over nine years (NARA; NPS; Britannica). One thesis puts enrollment at over 2.5 million (see table).
  • Congress appropriated more than $3 billion. About $663 million was sent to enrollees' dependents and over $200 million was returned to the Treasury. (Britannica)
  • Work credited to the CCC: roughly 3 billion trees planted (Wikipedia; Living New Deal, citing the National Park Service) or more than 2.3 billion (Britannica). About 6.5 million person-days fighting forest fires, about 800 state parks established, and large road, trail, bridge and check dam works. (Britannica; Living New Deal)
  • National park visits rose from 3.5 million in 1933 to 16 million in 1938 and 21 million in 1941, according to a review of Neil Maher's book. (High Country News, 2008 review by Jon Christensen; opened)

Race: what the record shows

  • The Act forbade discrimination, offered by Representative Oscar DePriest of Illinois. Segregation was nonetheless widely practiced. (Britannica; Cascade PBS)
  • Fechner allocated about 10 percent of placements to Black men, matching their share of the national population. Cascade PBS notes this ignored that Black unemployment was nearly double the national figure. The National Park Service says the aim was enrollment in proportion to each state's 1930 census share, with a 10 percent cap. (Cascade PBS; NPS, Company 818 and Segregation in the CCC; both opened)
  • Local selection agents, especially in the South, often excluded Black applicants. In Georgia, where Black residents were 36 percent of the population, no Black applicant was accepted in the first months. Florida initially enrolled no non-White applicants. Federal threats to withhold funds eventually led to some enrollment, far below population share. (Cascade PBS; Hoak thesis, which reports that Georgia agents enrolled no Black applicants for almost a year)
  • Camps were not segregated at first. After white community protests and Army attitudes, a July 1935 policy stated: "Complete segregation of white and colored enrollees is directed." Mixed companies were allowed only where Black numbers were too small to form a company. (Olen Cole Jr., African American Youth in the CCC of California, 1933 to 1942, California State Parks; opened)
  • Fechner defended the policy in reply to the NAACP: "segregation is not discrimination." (NPS; Cascade PBS)
  • In 1935 Fechner followed an Army suggestion and put Black enrollment on a "replacement only" basis, which capped new Black camps. (Hoak thesis; opened; the second half of the thesis was not read)
  • Black men were generally barred from supervisory posts; the Army, which ran the camps, followed its own segregation practices. Fechner accepted white-only supervision in Black camps. (Hoak thesis) Wikipedia says he refused to appoint Black adults to supervisory roles except as education directors in all-Black camps.
  • Segregated camps were often moved to remote sites. In Virginia's Yorktown, local white leaders accepted Black camps only on condition that segregation and white skilled positions continued, and wrote to Senator Harry Byrd and Governor George Peery when rumors suggested Black foremen. (Hoak thesis; NPS)
  • In California, Black residents were 1.4 percent of the population in 1930. After an order in summer 1935, integrated Black enrollees were transferred to five all-Black companies. (Cole)
  • At the Grand Canyon, Company 818 was an exception: Black and White enrollees did the same trail work at Phantom Ranch. Most other Black enrollees there were assigned domestic and cooking work. (NPS)

Native Americans, Hispanic enrollees and women

  • A separate Indian division operated from April 1933 (NARA). Wikipedia says about 14,000 to 15,000 Native Americans took part. We found this figure only in that secondary source.
  • Wikipedia also reports that Chicano enrollees faced segregation in sleeping, work and social spaces, and that some camps punished Spanish speakers. We did not find a second source for this.
  • The CCC enrolled only men. A separate, smaller program for women, nicknamed the "She-She-She camps," ran from 1934 to 1937, with about 28 camps in 26 states at its peak and about 8,500 women over its life. Eleanor Roosevelt pushed for it and Hilda Smith designed it around education and training rather than paid work. Federal leaders gave it little money and some critics argued the money should go to men. (History.com; opened)

What this case shows

  1. What ordinary people could know and vote on. The program began in an open statute with a stated nondiscrimination clause. Contemporary polling put approval at 82 percent in April 1936. The public could see the camps and, in many places, use the parks. The rules about who got a place were not in the statute: local selection agents, Army camp commanders and one director's orders decided them. (Paige appendices; Wikipedia)
  2. Who was included and who was left out. The law said no discrimination, yet the policy of July 1935 directed complete segregation, and Black enrollment was capped at about 10 percent and later put on a replacement-only basis. Women had a separate program with little money, and Black women appear only in a caption in the History.com article. A reader of the statute would not have learned this. (Paige appendices; Cole; Hoak; History.com)
  3. Who paid. Enrollees kept $5 of $30 a month. Families received $25, which Britannica says most states then deducted from relief. Federal appropriations exceeded $3 billion. Local white communities objected to Black camps and the camps were moved to remote areas. (Britannica; NPS)
  4. The work is visible and lasting. Parks, trails, fire towers and stand improvement exist now, and park visits rose sharply. The record supports a claim of real public goods that ordinary people use. (HCN review; Living New Deal)
  5. Counting output is not the same as net benefit. The Maher review reports that Aldo Leopold and conservation biologists criticized CCC crews for poor ecological understanding and that Maher calls some actions "biological blunders." The number of trees planted does not say whether the right trees were planted in the right places. (HCN review; secondary summary of Maher, whose book we did not read)
  6. Public correction was possible but slow. Black newspapers, the NAACP and some officials protested, and by 1941 Black men were again encouraged to enroll. The change came a year before the program ended. (NPS; Cascade PBS)

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: Segregation was the norm in the 1930s, and the CCC gave jobs to about 250,000 Black men who would otherwise have had none. Mitigation: We say this. Hoak notes Black enrollees stayed longer on average and that Virginia's Black enrollment was above the 10 percent Fechner required, though below the state's Black population share of 26.8 percent. We do not judge individuals; we report the policy and its effect on access. The statute itself said no discrimination, so the norm of the time is context, not an exemption.
  2. Objection: The Army, not the CCC, imposed segregation. Mitigation: The sources differ in emphasis. Cole attributes segregation to local opposition, Army attitudes and racial fears. Hoak says Fechner accepted the Army's approach because the Army was vital to the program. NPS and Cascade PBS attribute the July 1935 order and the "segregation is not discrimination" reply to Fechner. We name Fechner as the director who issued or defended it and the Army as the operator, and do not assign shares.
  3. Objection: The 10 percent cap matched the Black share of the U.S. population. Mitigation: Cascade PBS notes that Black unemployment was nearly double the national figure, and the NPS article says the cap ignored how hard the Depression hit Black communities. We give both.
  4. Objection: Counting work output proves nothing about benefit, so criticism of the CCC as wasteful is as unproven as praise. Mitigation: Agreed. We do not claim net benefit in either direction. We report Maher's reported finding of some ecological mistakes alongside lasting parks and fire work. We did not find an independent cost-benefit study.
  5. Objection: A program that served 3 million men cannot be summed up by its race policy. Mitigation: We report scale, wages, work and public approval first, and the exclusion record as part of the same record.
  6. Objection: Sources like Wikipedia and advocacy sites are weak. Mitigation: Where a number appears only on Wikipedia, we flag it as unverified (Native American count, Hispanic enrollee treatment). Statute text and NARA are primary; Salmond's standard history is cited by others but not opened.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • We did not open John Salmond's standard history (1967), which is cited widely but whose online chapters redirected to a landing page on the NPS site. All claims about Fechner's orders come from NPS and Cascade PBS articles, the Hoak thesis and the Cole article, not from an original order. Unverified.
  • The text of the July 1935 segregation directive is quoted by Cole; we did not open the order. The NPS article quotes slightly different wording ("complete segregation of colored and White enrollees"). We use Cole's quotation.
  • No primary tally of Black enrollees was opened. The figures of 250,000 and "more than 200,000" are from secondary sources. Unverified.
  • Whether the program as a whole kept a record of enrollment by race by year: not found.
  • Native American and Hispanic enrollee numbers and treatment rely on Wikipedia and one NARA mention. Unverified beyond that.
  • The state deduction of the $25 from relief (Britannica) was not checked against any state record.
  • Total cost: $3 billion is cited by Britannica and Living New Deal; we did not open the Director's final report.
  • The ecological critique (Leopold, "biological blunders") was read only through a book review. Maher's book was not opened.
  • Second half of the Hoak thesis (final 22,000 characters) was not read.
  • The Paige appendix text was read through a summarizing fetch; page numbers and exact words should be checked in the original before print.
  • What would change our minds: an original enrollment table by race, or a CCC director's file showing a different reason for the 1935 policy.
