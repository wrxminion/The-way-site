Jeffrey Epstein got a secret federal non-prosecution deal and a state guilty plea in 2008, was charged with sex trafficking in 2019 and died in jail before trial; Ghislaine Maxwell was convicted in 2021. Millions of pages are now public. What courts and official reports found is narrow, and most of the rest is allegation, shown here with who made it and the replies.

In brief

Palm Beach police began investigating Jeffrey Epstein in 2005 after a complaint involving a 14-year-old girl. In 2007 federal prosecutors in Florida signed a secret non-prosecution agreement with him, and in 2008 he pleaded guilty to two state charges. The Justice Department's own ethics office later found "poor judgment" but no professional misconduct by the U.S. Attorney, and found that victims were not told about the plea hearing. Federal prosecutors in New York charged Epstein with sex trafficking in July 2019; he died in jail a month later, and the medical examiner ruled it suicide. His associate Ghislaine Maxwell was convicted in 2021 and is serving 20 years. In 2025 Congress passed a law ordering the files released, and the Justice Department has published nearly 3.5 million pages, warning that some of them contain false or unverified submissions. What a court has found is narrow. Most of what is in the files and in the news is allegation, and this page names who made each one and what the accused person said in reply.

What we checked, and how we wrote this

  • Court records, government reports and official filings come first. News is used to find them or to report replies.
  • Every accusation against a named person is written as an allegation unless a court found it. Each named person's denial or reply is given where we found one, or we say we found none.
  • No victim names, addresses or contact details appear here, even where the files are public. "Jane" is the pseudonym used at Maxwell's trial.
  • We read the Justice Department's ethics office report (2020, executive summary and overview), the Inspector General's report on the jail (2023, executive summary and key passages, not all 128 pages), the Justice Department's January 30, 2026 release statement, the transcripts of Maxwell's July 2025 interview, and the House Judiciary Democrats' October 26, 2026 press release.
  • We did not open the 2007 agreement itself (we rely on the ethics office's description), the Maxwell indictment, verdict form or appeals opinion (dates come from news and reference sources), or the full 50-page House Democrats' report.

What is alleged (not found by a court), and the replies

Read each item as "X says Y," not "Y happened."

The House Judiciary Democrats' staff report, October 26, 2026.

  • What it is: a report by the committee's minority-party staff, not a finding of the full committee, a court or any grand jury. The minority cannot issue subpoenas. (NBC News)
  • What it says: it draws on staff interviews, FBI records and released files, and gives accounts from 11 women. Five say President Trump raped or sexually assaulted them in or near Epstein's company in the 1990s; six describe apparent attempts by Epstein or Maxwell to traffic them to him. It concludes the President and another named man "may have been co-conspirators" in Epstein's trafficking in the 1990s, and says the Justice Department withheld evidence. Ranking member Jamie Raskin said "an independent special counsel must investigate Epstein's co-conspirators and DOJ's cover-up." (press release; Spectrum News)
  • The replies: White House spokeswoman Lauren Bis said, "None of these allegations are true," and called the report "nothing more than a desperate attempt by crazed Democrats to distract Americans." A Justice Department spokesperson called it "nothing more than a last-ditch effort by House Judiciary Democrats to mislead the public," and pointed to the files it has made public. The President has denied knowing about Epstein's crimes. (Spectrum News; NBC News; press release)
  • Earlier, in its January 30, 2026 statement, the Justice Department said some files contain claims against the President "submitted to the FBI right before the 2020 election," and that "the claims are unfounded and false." That is also a statement by a party, and we have not seen the evidence behind it. (DOJ)
  • What no court has done: no court has found any of these allegations, and we found no charge against the President based on them.

The Maxwell interview, July 2025: the steering claim, checked against the transcripts.

  • The claim: the Democrats' press release says Blanche "prevented Maxwell from discussing" a trial witness called "Jane," who per the release told the FBI that Epstein introduced her to the President at Mar-a-Lago when she was 14. It says he did this "at least three times," and that it "potentially constituted witness tampering and obstruction." These are the Democrats' claims.
  • What the transcript shows (day 2, July 25, page numbers from the transcript; our quotes come from machine-read text, so wording may differ slightly): on page 293 Blanche asks whether Maxwell recalls "any obviously under 18 woman" coming to give Epstein a massage. Maxwell says, "I believe you're talking about Jane... I actually don't think that the testimony is correct." Blanche says, "Yeah, yeah, I don't want to get into ... he said, she said." Maxwell keeps talking about Jane, saying Epstein did not meet her until she was 16. On page 300 Blanche says, "Let's not talk about individuals," and Maxwell again goes on about Jane. We found two such moments, not three. Nothing on pages 290 to 301 mentions the President, and the transcript does not show whether Blanche knew of the claim the release attributes to Jane. Whether these moments were steering or keeping an interview on topic is a judgment we do not make. (DOJ transcripts)
  • Elsewhere in the interview, Blanche asked whether she ever heard that the President "had done anything inappropriate with masseuses or with anybody in your world"; Maxwell answered, "Absolutely never." She said there is no client list ("No, there's no list," page 330 of day 2, a passage we did not re-check against the PDF). Maxwell is a convicted offender serving 20 years, so her statements are those of an interested party. (DOJ transcripts)
  • Reply: we found none from Blanche or the Justice Department on the steering claim specifically. Blanche is now Attorney General. (NBC News; Spectrum News)

Other claims in circulation.

  • Allegations against other named people appear in the files and in civil suits. We do not repeat them here because we have not read those filings and the named people's replies.
  • The "client list" claim: Maxwell told Blanche there is no list. We did not check any other official statement on it. Unverified either way.
  • Claims that Epstein was murdered: the Medical Examiner and the Inspector General found suicide, alongside serious jail failures. Some commentators dispute this. The autopsy report is not public in New York. Homicide claims are unproven. (CBC; Inspector General report)

What this case shows

  1. The Justice Department's own reviewers found failures under both parties. The ethics office faulted the 2007 Florida deal, made under President George W. Bush. The Inspector General faulted the 2019 jail under President Trump. Both reports were written by the department itself. (OPR; Inspector General)
  2. Victims were left out. The 2007 agreement was signed without telling or consulting them, and they were not told about the 2008 plea hearing. (OPR)
  3. A guilty plea, a death before trial and one conviction are what courts have settled. Everything else, including much of what is shared online, is allegation. (OPR; court dismissal; Maxwell verdict)
  4. Releasing files is not the same as proving what is in them. Congress voted 427 to 1 to release the files, and the department itself warns they include false submissions. Readers have to ask who made each claim and what backs it. (DOJ)
  5. Each side's statement is a claim. The Democrats' report alleges a cover-up; the Justice Department and the White House call the allegations false. Neither is a court finding. (Press release; NBC News; Spectrum News)

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: Repeating unproven allegations against a sitting President is unfair to him. Our answer: We report the allegations only as claims by a named congressional staff report, next to the White House and Justice Department denials, and we say plainly that no court has found them. The report is an official congressional document about a matter of public concern.
  2. Objection: Leaving the allegations out would be a cover-up of its own. Our answer: We include them, attributed, because Congress and the press are debating them. We do not add any we could not trace to a document.
  3. Objection: The minority report is partisan. Our answer: Agreed that it is a minority staff report, and we say so. It is also the only document making these specific claims that we could read, and it was written by members of Congress.
  4. Objection: The Justice Department is led by an official the report accuses, so its denial is not neutral. Our answer: We note that Blanche conducted the Maxwell interview and is now Attorney General, and we compared the claim about him with the transcript ourselves.
  5. Objection: The medical examiner and the FBI could be wrong about the death. Our answer: Possibly, but no official body has found otherwise, and we label the homicide claims as unproven.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • The exact text of the 2007 agreement and how courts later ruled on the victims' rights case under the Crime Victims' Rights Act. Not read.
  • The exact date and wording of the Supreme Court's denial in Maxwell's case (sources give Oct. 2 and Oct. 6, 2025).
  • What the Democrats' full report cites, page by page, and whether its FBI records are in the public release.
  • Any reply from Blanche on the steering claim, and the third interjection the release counts.
  • A person's check of our transcript quotes against the PDF images.
  • Reports that a judge sealed filings with unredacted victim names. Not checked.
  • What laws changed after this case, federal and state.
