Until 2018 a minor could legally marry in every state. By November 2026, 17 states and D.C. ban marriage under 18 with no exceptions and California has signed an 18th, while other states still allow it with a parent's or a judge's approval. An advocacy group counts about 314,000 minors married from 2000 to 2021.

In brief

Until 2018, a person under 18 could legally marry in every state. Since then, states have started closing that door. By November 2026, 17 states and Washington, D.C. ban all marriage under 18 with no exceptions, from Delaware and New Jersey in 2018 to Oklahoma in 2026, and California's ban, signed on September 30, 2026, will make 18. Republican and Democratic lawmakers sponsored these bans. In the remaining states, a parent or a judge can still say yes, and Mississippi and New Mexico set no minimum age at all. One advocacy group's count of state records finds about 314,000 minors married from 2000 to 2021, mostly 16 and 17 year old girls, with yearly numbers falling from about 32,700 to about 1,700. Supporters of bans say a married minor often cannot divorce, sign a contract or enter a shelter. Opponents say the choice belongs to parents and courts, and some argue a ban could mean more abortions or births outside marriage. No federal agency counts these marriages.

The record

This page is about who can say yes to a marriage involving a person under 18, and who can check or undo it. It names no private person as having done anything wrong. State statutes were not opened for this version, so every state's rule below comes from a secondary source and is marked that way.

What the other states do

  • No minimum age at all: Mississippi and New Mexico, and California until its new law takes effect. (Stateline; CHILD USA)
  • Most other states allow marriage at 16 or 17 with a parent's consent, a judge's approval, or both. Some partial reforms: Texas (2017) set 18 but lets emancipated 16 and 17 year olds marry; Arizona (2018) allows 16 with court approval and a three-year age-gap limit; Florida (2018) allows 17 with a parent and a judge and a two-year gap limit; Kentucky (2018) allows 17 with a judge; Ohio (2019) allows 17 with juvenile court approval; West Virginia (2023) allows 16 with a parent. A full 50-state table from the statutes was not built. (Wikipedia)
  • Ohio: SB 341, which would set 18 with no exceptions, passed a Senate committee unanimously in June 2026 but had no floor vote before the summer break. We found no later vote. If it does not pass by the end of the year, it must be reintroduced. (Stateline; news reports seen in search results)
  • California also passed a companion law letting people facing forced marriage seek court protection, according to news reports we did not open.

How many minors have married

The only national count we found comes from Unchained At Last, an advocacy group that requested marriage records from each state. It is not an official count. There is no federal requirement to collect marriage data and no central database. (Unchained At Last)

  • Total from 2000 to 2021: 314,154 minors legally married (each minor counted once, so a marriage between two minors counts as two). About 86% were girls and about 96% were 16 or 17. The average age gap with the spouse was about four years. The youngest reported age was 10.
  • By year: 32,652 in 2000; 12,829 in 2010; 1,655 in 2020; 1,717 in 2021. The study does not separate how much of the drop came from new laws and how much from fewer marriages overall.
  • Rates per 1,000 minors aged 15 to 17, by the state that issued the license: national average 1.13. Highest: Nevada 6.15, Idaho 3.56, Utah 3.23.
  • Marriages that would have been a sex crime outside of marriage: at least 66,415, by the group's count.
  • Gaps the group itself reports: 33 states gave full data, six gave none and were estimated, and some states gave partial data. It calls its total "almost certainly" an undercount.

Federal law and the 2026 bill

  • Marriage age is set by the states. There is no federal minimum marriage age. (CHILD USA)
  • In 2022, Congress removed marriage as a defense to federal statutory rape charges, which cover sex with a person 12 to 15 on federal land. (CRS R47570; 18 U.S.C. 2243)
  • Federal law defines forced marriage as "a marriage to which 1 or both parties do not or cannot consent" and in which "1 or more elements of force, fraud, or coercion is present." (CRS R47570)
  • The Child Marriage Prevention Act of 2026 (H.R. 9758, introduced July 16 by Rep. Gwen Moore, D-Wis., and S. 5060, introduced July 21 by Sens. Dick Durbin, Brian Schatz and Kirsten Gillibrand, all Democrats) would not set a national marriage age. It would create a national study commission, order GAO reports, give grants for state task forces, give extra Justice Department grant money to states that ban marriage under 18, bar marriages under 18 on federal land, have the Justice Department write a model state law, and require spouse and fiance visa petitioners and beneficiaries to be 18, with a narrow humanitarian exception. At our last check it had been introduced and sent to committee, with no further action found. (H.R. 9758 text; S. 5060 text; Rep. Moore)
  • The bill's findings section repeats the sponsors' claims, such as that "70 to 80 percent" of marriages involving someone under 18 end in divorce. Those are the sponsors' statements; we did not check the studies behind them.

The case for ending exceptions, in supporters' words

  1. Coercion the law cannot see. Oklahoma Sen. Warren Hamilton (R), who sponsored the ban: "Oklahoma children must be able to grow up free from coercion and exploitation." Rep. Nicole Miller (R): "children are simply not equipped to shoulder that kind of commitment." (Oklahoma Senate)
  2. A married minor often cannot get out. Missouri Rep. Renee Reuter (R): "Women who get married before 18 don't have the option to get a divorce." CHILD USA notes minors generally cannot sign binding contracts, which makes hiring a lawyer hard, and many shelters cannot take an unaccompanied minor. (St. Louis Public Radio; CHILD USA)
  3. Marriage can erase a crime. CHILD USA recommends ending exemptions "that allow a marriage to erase sex crimes." Sen. Durbin: "Child marriage overwhelmingly affects underage girls who are married to adult men." (CHILD USA; Rep. Moore)
  4. Survivors. Courtney Stodden, married at 16, backed California's ban and said, "I will keep fighting until children are protected from marriage in all 50 states." Gov. Gavin Newsom called the law "a long overdue measure to protect young Californians and minors." (Governor of California)
  5. Both parties. The Oklahoma and Missouri bans were sponsored by Republicans; California's by a Democrat. The Missouri Senate vote was 32 to 1, and Oklahoma's Senate vote was unanimous. (KGOU; St. Louis Public Radio)

The case against, and other concerns, in opponents' words

  1. Parents' rights. Some Oklahoma Republicans called the ban government overreach that takes away parents' right to consent; KGOU reported the House passed it "by one vote." Reform supporters answer that a parent can be the one forcing the marriage. (Stateline; KGOU)
  2. Pregnancy and abortion. Missouri Rep. Hardy Billington (R) argued that barring pregnant teens from marrying would increase abortions: "We all claim to be pro-life here in the body." Some Oklahoma Republicans said a ban would increase births outside marriage. We found no study on either side of this claim. (St. Louis Public Radio; Stateline)
  3. The right to marry. The ACLU and Planned Parenthood opposed a 2017 California bill to ban marriage before 18, arguing it intruded on a fundamental right; that bill was narrowed. We did not read their statements. (Stateline)
  4. Let a judge decide. Many states keep 16 or 17 with court approval and age-gap limits. Supporters of that model say a judge can catch coercion; reform groups say it is not enough. We found no data on how often judges refuse. (Wikipedia)
  5. The numbers are an advocacy estimate. The 314,154 total comes from one group's records requests, with six states estimated. It is the best count we found, not an official one.

What this case shows

  1. States changed this quickly and from both parties. From zero states in 2017 to 18 signed by 2026, with Republican sponsors in Oklahoma and Missouri and a Democratic sponsor in California. (Table above)
  2. Nobody official is counting. No federal agency collects marriage records, so the only national count comes from an advocacy group. (Unchained At Last)
  3. The real disagreement is about who decides. Bans take the choice from parents and judges; opponents want it left with them. Both sides say they are protecting the minor.
  4. Washington's role is limited. Marriage age is a state matter, so the federal bill uses money, study and visa rules, not a national ban. (H.R. 9758; CHILD USA)

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • A verified 50-state table built from each state's statute, with signing and effective dates. Not done for this version.
  • California's effective date in the law's own text, and the companion forced-marriage law.
  • Whether Ohio's SB 341 passes before the end of 2026.
  • Any committee action on the federal bill.
  • An official count of marriages under 18, and how much of the drop since 2000 came from new laws.
  • Any study of teen births or abortions after a ban, which would test the pregnancy argument either way.
  • How often judges deny requests from 16 and 17 year olds in states that allow it.
