Congress authorized a 41,000 mile highway network in 1956, paid for 90 percent from a new trust fund fed by fuel taxes; it built a system of 46,876 miles that moved people and goods, and it also cut through cities, displaced a number of people that sources do not agree on, and drew local revolts that changed the rules.

In brief

In 1956 Congress authorized a 41,000 mile Interstate network, set the federal share at 90 percent, and created a Highway Trust Fund fed by fuel and tire taxes. FHWA now counts 46,876 miles, and a 1991 estimate put the cost of the main construction program at $128.9 billion. Economists find real gains: Fernald calls the Interstate a one-time productivity boost, and Baum-Snow estimates that each highway through a central city cut its population by about 18 percent. Cities paid in other ways. Mohl reports about 37,000 urban housing units demolished a year in the 1960s, and sources disagree on the total number displaced, with figures of 475,000 households and over 1 million people that we could not trace to a primary count. The 1956 act asked states to certify that hearings were held or offered, but relocation payments were added only in 1968 and applied to all states from July 1970. Residents in San Francisco, New Orleans, Baltimore and Philadelphia organized, and some routes were stopped. The record shows how a national decision was made; it shows less about whether those on the path of each route were able to shape it.

The record

The law and the money

  • The Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956 (70 Stat. 374) raised the authorized Interstate length from 40,000 to 41,000 miles (Sec. 108(l)). It set the federal share at "90 per centum of the total cost" (Sec. 108(e)), with a ceiling of 95 percent for any single project in states with large untaxed public lands. It created the Highway Trust Fund in the Treasury (Sec. 209(a)) and sent fuel and tire-related taxes received after June 30, 1956 into it (Sec. 209(c)). (70 Stat. 374, GovInfo; opened. The text we read was partly truncated, so later sections were not checked.)
  • Section 116(c) required a state that planned a project passing through or bypassing a city to certify that it held public hearings, "or gave the opportunity" for them, and considered economic effects. The statute says nothing in the sections we read about relocating displaced households. (70 Stat. 374; opened, partial)
  • The act was signed June 29, 1956. It authorized $25 billion for Interstate construction for fiscal years 1957 through 1969. The earlier Clay Committee had put the cost of the 40,000 mile system at $27 billion and proposed bond financing, which Congress did not adopt. The Trust Fund was modeled on pay-as-you-go financing, and FHWA says it was patterned on the Social Security trust fund. (FHWA, The Greatest Decade, Part 1; opened)

What it cost and what was built

  • FHWA reports the system is now 46,876 miles. A 1991 estimate put the cost of the 42,795 miles built under the Interstate Construction Program at $128.9 billion, of which $114.3 billion was federal. FHWA says the program did not simply end; a remaining I-95 link north of Philadelphia was still expected. (FHWA, Interstate FAQ; opened)
  • Cost estimates rose early. The 1958 estimate covered 38,548 miles at $37.6 billion; the June 1959 update was $41 billion. By 1961, urban miles averaged $3.658 million per mile against $639,000 for rural miles, and Congress faced a gap of $11.6 billion to finish on schedule. The 1958 act added $800 million and the 1959 act raised the gas tax one cent. (FHWA, Greatest Decade Part 1; opened)
  • The 1968 Federal-Aid Highway Act (82 Stat. 815) authorized $18.225 billion more for fiscal years 1970 through 1974 ($4 billion for each of four years and $2.225 billion for the last) and moved the completion deadline from June 30, 1972 to June 30, 1974. (82 Stat. 815, GovInfo; opened)

Relocation rules came later

  • The 1956 act (sections read) had no relocation payment. The 1968 act added a new chapter 5 of title 23. A state could not get approval for a project that displaced people unless it gave assurances of fair relocation payments and services (Sec. 502). An owner-occupant of a one to three family home could get up to $5,000 to close the gap to a comparable dwelling (Sec. 506(a)), and a renter or other displaced person up to $1,500 (Sec. 506(b)). Moving allowances were up to $200 plus a $100 dislocation allowance for dwellings (Sec. 505(b)). A business could take a fixed payment of the lesser of its average net earnings or $5,000 (Sec. 505(c)). Sections 502 and 505 to 508 applied to all states from July 1, 1970. Section 24 amended Sec. 128(a) so location decisions consider social and environmental effects and consistency with local planning. (82 Stat. 815; opened)
  • This means 12 years passed between the 1956 act and statutory relocation payments for highway displacement. We make no claim about why. (Inference from the two statutes only.)

We found no single official count of people displaced by the Interstate program. The 475,000 household figure and the 1 million person figure cannot be traced to a DOT document from what we opened, so we label them Disputed rather than established. Mohl's figures for single cities and the Altshuler forecast are different kinds of numbers and should not be added. Urban renewal displacement is a separate program and is covered in Case 58; we do not repeat it here.

Freeway revolts

  • San Francisco's supervisors opposed freeways in 1959. In New Orleans, preservationists fought the Riverfront Expressway, and Volpe ended the Vieux Carre route in 1969. In Baltimore, the Movement Against Destruction, an interracial coalition, is credited by Mohl with preventing demolition of 28,000 housing units. Philadelphia's crosstown plan ended in 1970. (FHWA Part 1; Mohl, Stop the Road; opened. Mohl is a scholarly review hosted by an advocacy group.)
  • Mohl's account of Boston says expressways dislocated downtown businesses and residential areas, including Chinatown and part of the North End. The text we read did not describe a Boston revolt.

What this case shows

  1. The funding rule decided where the money went before the routes were drawn. A 90 percent federal share and a dedicated fuel-tax trust fund gave states a strong reason to build Interstate miles rather than other projects. The 1956 statute is open to read. What ordinary voters could not easily see was how a route would cross their own block.
  2. Hearings existed, but not a right to a veto or to compensation. The 1956 act asked for certification that hearings were held "or" offered. Relocation payments did not arrive in statute until 1968 and applied to all states from July 1970.
  3. Costs were known to rise and were visible to Congress. The 1958, 1959 and 1961 estimates show urban miles costing about five times rural miles, and the 1968 act extended the schedule. The public record shows the cost shift; it does not show the public choosing it.
  4. People with the least power took the largest local hits in the places documented. Mohl reports 85 percent minority share of displaced families in Camden. This is a documented pattern in listed cities, not a finding for every project.
  5. When residents organized, routes changed. San Francisco, New Orleans, Baltimore and Philadelphia show that organized objection could stop segments, and the 1968 act's relocation and social-effects clauses followed that period.

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: The Interstate was a national success. Fernald finds a large productivity gain in the 1950s and 1960s. Mitigation: We include it. Fernald also finds the gain was one-time. Thesis point is who was consulted and who bore costs, not whether the system was worth building.
  2. Objection: The 1956 act did include hearings. Mitigation: True, Sec. 116(c). It required certification that hearings were held or offered. We do not claim hearings were never held.
  3. Objection: Displacement counts are inflated. Mitigation: We show that no official count was found, and we label the 475,000 and 1 million figures Disputed. Readers should treat city-level figures as city-level.
  4. Objection: Suburbanization had many causes, not highways alone. Mitigation: Baum-Snow's estimate is one study. We do not attribute all central city decline to the Interstate, and the 18 percent figure applies per highway through a central city.
  5. Objection: Relocation help came as soon as Congress understood the problem. Mitigation: The record shows the statutory payments in 1968. We do not know when Congress first understood the scale and do not infer intent.
  6. Objection: Urban renewal, not highways, caused most clearance. Mitigation: Mohl says highways and urban renewal each demolished about 37,000 units a year in the 1960s. Programs overlapped; see Case 58.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • The DOT or FHWA source for "475,000 households" and "1 million people" was not found. A primary count would resolve the Disputed claim.
  • The rest of the 1956 act (after the truncation) was not read; a relocation provision could exist in an unread section.
  • We did not open the hearing transcripts or any state certification, so we do not know how many hearings were real opportunities to object.
  • Chandra and Thompson (2000) findings were not read.
  • Mohl's Baltimore 28,000 unit figure and Camden HUD task force figure come from a secondary review; the 1968 HUD report was not opened.
  • We did not verify the Overtown figures against Rothstein or a Miami record.
  • Who financed the Trust Fund by income group (fuel tax incidence) was not studied here.
  • The 1991 cost estimate is old; a current inflation-adjusted total was not located.
