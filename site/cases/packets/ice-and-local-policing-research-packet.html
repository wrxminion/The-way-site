Since 1996 the law has let willing local officers do immigration work, and since 2008 jail fingerprints have gone to ICE. Courts say localities cannot be forced to help. In 2025 and 2026 partnerships, detention and funding grew far past earlier levels, alongside deaths in custody, shootings by federal agents and new surveillance tools.

In brief

Immigration enforcement is a federal job, but most arrests happen where local police and jails already are. Since 1996 the law has let willing local officers be trained and deputized for immigration work under section 287(g). Since 2008 fingerprints taken at local jails have gone to ICE. Courts have said the federal government cannot force local officers to help, and several have said a jail cannot hold someone on an ICE request alone. In 2025 a new executive order, a new detention law and about $75 billion in new funding pushed local partnerships far past earlier levels: ICE says it has signed 2,618 agreements. The record now also includes rising deaths in ICE custody, shootings by federal agents in Minneapolis, a fight over plate-reader surveillance and privacy, and a debate over agents near polling places. Supporters say partnership puts limited federal officers where criminals already are. Critics say it moves civil-rights and money risk onto local departments and erodes trust. Both readings are shown here.

What each side says, in its own words

Supporters of closer local partnership

  • DHS spokesperson Tricia McLaughlin called the partnerships "critical resources" and called racial-profiling claims "disgusting and categorically FALSE." (NPR/GBH)
  • Simon Hankinson of the Heritage Foundation said civil-rights worries are "driven by ideology" and violations are "pretty rare." (NPR/GBH)
  • Sen. Catherine Cortez Masto, a Democrat who voted for the Laken Riley Act: "Anyone who commits a crime should be held accountable." (OPB)
  • The argument: ICE is small next to the number of people booked into local jails. A jail is the safest place to take custody. Congress wrote 287(g) and made it voluntary.

Critics

  • Annie Lai of UC Irvine: "The potential for civil rights violations is acute." Doris Meissner, a former INS commissioner: "putting 287(g) agreements on steroids." (NPR/GBH)
  • Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez said the Laken Riley Act lets people merely accused of a crime be detained without a day in court. (OPB)
  • The argument: policing depends on immigrants being willing to call police and act as witnesses. Local departments carry the legal and money risk of acts done under federal direction. Courts have said locals cannot be conscripted.

Open on the evidence: the studies we found showed no clear effect of Secure Communities on crime, and DHS says it does not track what share of removals come from 287(g). (Wikipedia overview; NPR/GBH)

The Minneapolis shootings

Renee Good, January 7, 2026. Good was shot by an ICE officer. DHS called it "domestic terrorism"; Minneapolis Mayor Jacob Frey and Gov. Tim Walz disputed that after seeing video. The Hennepin County Medical Examiner ruled the death a homicide, which describes a death at another person's hands, not a finding of crime. The administration's position is that the officer acted in self-defense. On January 19, Todd Blanche, then Deputy Attorney General, said, "we are not investigating. And if there comes a time when we need to, we will, but it's not now." No charge has been reported. (Al Jazeera; The Marshall Project)

Alex Pretti, January 24, 2026.

  • Pretti, 37, a U.S. citizen and ICU nurse, was shot by a Border Patrol agent and a CBP officer. The federal notification to Congress says an agent shouted "He's got a gun!" and about five seconds later two agents fired; an agent had already taken a 9 mm pistol from his waistband. CNN's description of video: he had been pepper-sprayed and was on his knees when "Gun!" was shouted, a gun was removed, and then at least 10 shots were heard. (Fox News; CNN via KRDO)
  • The Medical Examiner ruled the death a homicide (Feb. 2). (FOX 5)
  • The federal side: then-DHS Secretary Kristi Noem called him a "terrorist" who "attacked" officers, and Stephen Miller called him an "assassin," as reported by CNN. The family side: his parents say he was not holding a gun and called the official account's "lies" "sickening and reprehensible." NBC reported that videos it obtained appear to contradict parts of DHS's version. (CNN via KRDO; NBC News)
  • On January 30, Blanche said the Justice Department's Civil Rights Division was investigating, separately from DHS's own review. He gave no timetable and did not commit to releasing body camera footage. (NPR via NHPR)
  • State access: federal authorities kept Minnesota's Bureau of Criminal Apprehension from the scene; a court order later opened access, and Minnesota sued on March 24. An evidence-sharing deal followed in June or July (sources differ). Court records filed October 13 say prosecutors cannot reach the weapons used or Pretti's gun. (MPR News; CNN via KRDO)
  • Nine months on, no charges have been reported. The family says the Justice Department has given no substantive update, and U.S. Attorney Dan Rosen said he "can't tell you for all kinds of reasons." (CNN via KRDO)
  • Left out: the agents' names, which one outlet reported but DHS has not confirmed.

Deaths in custody and shootings: what the data shows

  • ICE's own death-reporting page lists each death by fiscal year from FY2018. Our count of its entries: FY2018 6, FY2019 8, FY2020 21, FY2021 5, FY2022 3, FY2023 4, FY2024 12, FY2025 24, and FY2026 36 so far (latest listed Aug. 23, 2026). A news count, citing ICE, gave 25 for FY2025, so our hand count may be off by one. The list does not give causes of death. (ICE death reporting page, archived Oct. 25, 2026; WMC Action News 5)
  • ICE says detainee health care is "one of the agency's highest priorities," that national standards require medical, dental and mental health care from arrival, and that every death is reviewed and reported to Congress within 90 days. Critics tie the rise to detention growth and crowding; the sources we read show the pattern but do not prove the cause. (ICE page; NBC News)
  • Shootings by federal immigration agents: no official count exists in anything we read. The Marshall Project counted at least three people killed by federal officers in the five months before Minneapolis, from news reports. DHS says shootings follow threats to officers. (The Marshall Project)

Surveillance, privacy and traffic stops

The same data networks that feed immigration enforcement, such as license plate readers and shared lookup systems, also feed ordinary traffic stops. That makes this a privacy question for every driver, not only for immigrants. The legal question is when tracking public movements becomes a "search" that needs a warrant under the Fourth Amendment.

  • AP reported in November 2025 on a Border Patrol program that reads plates, builds "patterns of life," flags routes it finds unusual, and tips local police, who then make stops on minor traffic violations. CBP said it uses readers to identify threats and disrupt criminal networks and that "for national security reasons, we do not detail the specific operational applications." (AP via ABC7)
  • Bexar County, Texas: on September 28, 2026 a federal jury found the county liable for a 76-minute stop of driver Alek Schott after a plate-surveillance tip, and awarded $76. The deputy cited lane drift; Schott's dashcam showed none, and a search found nothing. The county said at trial that it did nothing wrong; the sheriff and the district attorney declined comment, citing a possible appeal. (KSAT; Police1)
  • Norfolk, Virginia: on January 27, 2026 a federal judge ruled that the city's Flock cameras are not a Fourth Amendment search "today," because the snapshots are miles and 40 to 50 minutes apart, but warned that more cameras could change that. Police say the cameras help solve cases. The plaintiffs plan to appeal. (WHRO)
  • Flock says customers own their data. It admitted a pilot that gave DHS direct access after earlier denials, then paused federal pilots and added blocks on searches tied to civil immigration enforcement. Oakland police called it "one of the most effective crime-fighting technologies" they have; several cities ended or paused contracts. (The Independent via Yahoo, one roundup)
  • The law: Carpenter v. United States (2018) held that historical phone-location records need a warrant, but called itself narrow and did not reach ordinary cameras. Plate-reader cases are being decided one at a time. (Carpenter (Cornell LII))
  • An October 2026 ACLU article argues these systems should be stopped. The ACLU is an advocacy group and its article quotes no police or vendor, so we used it only as a lead and checked its main claims elsewhere. (ACLU)

Federal agents and elections

  • The law: 18 U.S.C. 592 bars officials from bringing or keeping "troops or armed men" at a place where an election is held, unless needed to repel armed enemies of the United States. Whether ICE officers serving a warrant would count has not been decided by a court. (18 U.S.C. 592)
  • DHS positions: a DHS official told state officials in February, "There will be no ICE presence at polling locations." On September 1, DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin said agents could be there "if there is a threat to that polling place or we're serving a warrant," and DHS said it was not planning operations at polls. FBI Director Kash Patel did not rule out agents at polls. (NPR; Votebeat)
  • Nine groups sued in September to keep armed agents away from polls; the case was at an early stage. Democrats organized at least 10,000 volunteers to watch for intimidation, a partisan effort. (Votebeat; NPR via TPR)
  • What happened on November 3: Governing reported ICE "didn't show." In Arizona, a volunteer group said no agents were reported at polling sites, but it reported ICE arrests in a parking lot near one Mesa polling site; no official confirmed that account. (Governing; KJZZ)

What this case shows

  1. Local cooperation is a choice, and the courts have kept it one. Congress made 287(g) voluntary, and the Supreme Court has said the federal government cannot conscript local officers. Places that join and places that decline are both following the law. (8 U.S.C. 1357; Printz; CRS)
  2. The program grew fast, and the counts are hard to read. ICE's own page gives one total and three model counts that do not add up to it. "Signed" and "active" measure different things. Residents can ask their sheriff which kind of agreement their county has. (ICE; NPR/GBH)
  3. Oversight has lagged since the start. GAO found missing controls in 2009, and there is still no official count of shootings by immigration agents. (GAO testimony; The Marshall Project)
  4. When federal agents use force, local and state officials can be shut out. In the Pretti case, Minnesota had to go to court for access, and prosecutors say they still cannot reach the weapons. (MPR News; CNN via KRDO)
  5. The same data tools reach everyone, not only immigrants. A Texas jury found a county liable for a stop built on plate-reader data, while a Virginia judge found a camera network lawful for now. (KSAT; WHRO)

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: The page leans on advocacy groups. Our answer: The ACLU and the Institute for Justice are labeled as parties, each of their claims is checked against independent reporting, and the county, CBP and vendor responses sit next to them.
  2. Objection: Naming two people shot by agents makes the program look worse than the numbers. Our answer: The two cases are the most documented uses of force in this record. We give the federal account, the family's account and what the video shows, and we say no charge or finding exists.
  3. Objection: Rising custody deaths reflect a larger detained population, not worse care. Our answer: That is possible. We give the counts and say plainly that the sources do not prove the cause.
  4. Objection: The polling-place fears were overblown. Our answer: The Election Day reports we found support that; we report them, and we also report the official statements that left the door open.
  5. Objection: This is fresh news and could change. Our answer: Agreed. The Minneapolis entries carry a checked date and will be updated when an investigation reports.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • Why ICE's 287(g) total (2,618) is 100 less than its model counts add to.
  • Deaths in custody before FY2018, and a person's check of our FY2025 count (24 versus 25).
  • An official or academic count of shootings by immigration agents.
  • The outcome of the Justice Department's civil rights investigation of the Pretti shooting, and any review of the Good shooting.
  • The primary texts we did not open: the 2010 DHS Inspector General report, the 2014 Johnson memo, the 2021 GAO follow-up, Executive Order 14287 and the 2025 budget law.
  • The ruling in the September 2026 lawsuit over armed agents at polls.
  • How Colorado counties and sheriffs handle detainers and 287(g) under state law today.
