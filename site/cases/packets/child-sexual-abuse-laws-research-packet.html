Most child sexual abuse cases fall under state law, where the age of consent runs from 16 to 18. Federal law covers crossing borders, federal land, abuse images and trafficking, with long minimum sentences. Time limits have been lifted step by step, and state high courts are split on reopening old civil claims.

In brief

Most child sexual abuse cases are handled under state law. Each state sets its own age of consent, from 16 to 18, with exceptions for partners close in age and higher ages when the adult is a teacher, coach or guardian. Federal law covers what crosses state lines or happens on federal land, online abuse images and trafficking, and it carries heavy minimum sentences: 10 years to life for trafficking a 14 to 17 year old, 15 years when force is used or the child is under 14. Federal charges for child sexual abuse can be brought at any time during the child's life, or for ten years after the crime, whichever is longer. Since 2022, a person abused as a child can sue under federal law with no time limit. By one advocacy group's count, 44 states have lifted the criminal time limit for at least some of these crimes. The sharpest fight now is over laws that reopen old civil claims: state supreme courts have split, five to five over six years, on whether that is fair to the people and institutions being sued.

The record

This page explains the laws. It is not about any one person, and it names no victim. Related cases: Child marriage laws and The Epstein record.

Federal law

Federal law reaches conduct on federal land and in federal facilities, across state or national borders, online, and in trafficking. Everyday statutory rape cases are state cases.

State law: age of consent

  • Each state sets its own age of consent. A 2018 count put about 29 states at 16, seven at 17 (Colorado, Illinois, Louisiana, Missouri, New York, Texas and Wyoming) and 14 at 18. That source contradicts itself for a few states, so we do not publish a state-by-state list until the statutes are read. (Wikipedia, secondary)
  • Many states have close-in-age ("Romeo and Juliet") rules, so two teenagers near the same age are not charged the same way as an adult with a teen.
  • Many states raise the age when the older person holds a position of trust, such as a teacher, coach or guardian. For example, Alabama bars school employees from sexual contact with students under 19 and says consent is no defense; Connecticut raises the age to 18 for guardians, coaches and some professionals. (Wikipedia, secondary)
  • Sexual images of anyone under 18 are a federal crime regardless of the state's age of consent. (18 U.S.C. 2251)

Time limits and reopening old claims

  • Criminal: CHILD USA, an advocacy research group, counts 44 states, D.C. and the federal government as having removed the criminal time limit for at least some child sex abuse crimes. What "some" covers differs a lot by state and by the victim's age. (CHILD USA)
  • Civil: by the same count, 19 states and federal law have no time limit for new civil claims: Alaska, Arizona, Arkansas, California, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, Florida, Illinois, Louisiana, Maine, Maryland, Minnesota, Nebraska, Nevada, New Hampshire, Utah, Vermont and Washington. (CHILD USA)
  • Reopening old claims ("revival windows"): many states have passed laws that let survivors sue over abuse whose time limit had already run out. State high courts are split on whether that is allowed. State Court Report counts "a two-to-two split this year (and five-to-five split in the last six years)." Upheld: North Carolina (2025), Maryland (2025, 4 to 3), Vermont and, after rehearing, Louisiana. Struck down or barred: Utah (2020), Kentucky, Colorado (2023), Maine (2025, 5 to 2) and New Hampshire (2025). (State Court Report, two articles)

The two sides of the revival fight, in their own words

  • For reopening: survivors often take years to tell anyone, out of "fear of retaliation or ostracization," and these laws "promote the public good by exposing hidden predators." Advocate Kathryn Robb: "When do institutions pay attention? When they get sued." The Maryland high court held that an expired time limit "does not provide a potential defendant with a vested right in remaining free from liability." (State Court Report)
  • Against reopening: defense and tort-reform groups say these laws unfairly impose new duties on past conduct. The American Tort Reform Association argued they "reopen the courthouse doors to stale claims in which records and witnesses are no longer available." The Maine high court held that "once a statute of limitations has expired for a claim, a right to be free of that claim has vested, and the claim cannot be revived." School districts and churches face large liability under these laws. (State Court Report)

Where this meets marriage law

  • Marriage can change whether sex with a minor is a crime. Federal law removed its marriage defense in 2022. The sponsors of a 2026 federal bill say "the majority of States allow marriage to be used as a defense to statutory rape laws"; we have not checked that against state codes. (CRS R47570; H.R. 9758)
  • Some states changed both at once: Missouri's 2025 law that ended child marriage also barred nondisclosure agreements in child sexual abuse cases. (St. Louis Public Radio)
  • See Child marriage laws for the full record.

What this case shows

  1. Two systems cover the same crime. Where the abuse happened, and whether it crossed a border or a screen, decides whether state or federal law applies, and the penalties differ widely.
  2. Time limits have moved one way. Federal law went from age 25, to the child's whole life, to no limit at all for civil suits. Most states have lifted criminal limits for at least some offenses. (18 U.S.C. 3283; 18 U.S.C. 2255; CHILD USA)
  3. Reopening old cases is where courts push back. Legislatures have passed revival laws; state supreme courts are evenly split on whether they are fair to defendants. (State Court Report)
  4. The age line depends on the state and the relationship. The same act can be legal in one state and a crime in the next, or legal with a peer and a crime with a coach. (Wikipedia; state examples above)

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: Mandatory minimums take judgment away from judges. Our answer: That is a real debate in sentencing policy. This page reports what Congress set; we did not open sources arguing either side of the minimums for these crimes.
  2. Objection: Prosecutors should have to prove the defendant knew the child's age. Our answer: Federal law narrows that requirement in two places and allows a reasonable-belief defense in another. We state the rule and note that we did not find a source setting out both sides.
  3. Objection: Counting "44 states" overstates how much has changed. Agreed in part. The count includes states that lifted limits only for some crimes or some ages, and it comes from an advocacy group. We say so.
  4. Objection: Revival windows are unfair to institutions that changed long ago. Our answer: Half the state high courts that ruled agree. We give both sides' words and the rulings on each side.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • A verified age-of-consent table for all 50 states and D.C., from the statutes.
  • Which offenses and victim ages are behind each state in the "44 states" count.
  • The text of the Colorado, Maine and Maryland rulings, which we read only through summaries.
  • Which states still let marriage affect a statutory rape charge.
  • A verified list of position-of-trust laws.
