2. The article

In brief

In 1921 a white mob in Tulsa, helped by deputized citizens and some officials, burned 35 blocks of the Black Greenwood district, destroyed about 1,256 homes, and killed somewhere between several dozen and 300 people. The survivors then tried every legal path to recovery. Insurers denied their claims under riot clauses, and the city rejected more than $4 million in claims. An all-white grand jury blamed Black residents, and no white person went to prison. The survivors were also living in a state that had changed its constitution in 1910, and passed a registration law in 1916, to keep Black citizens from voting. The Supreme Court struck down both measures, in 1915 and in 1939. Decades later, a federal appeals court agreed that the victims had been "powerless" and that access to the courts "was denied," but ruled in 2004 that the deadline to sue had passed. In 2024, Oklahoma's highest court called the survivors' grievance "legitimate" and said the remedy belonged to policymakers.

The record

  • 1907 to 1910: Segregation and disfranchisement in Oklahoma law. The U.S. Department of Justice's 2025 review says Oklahoma "contained explicit 'Jim Crow' provisions, mandating segregation" from statehood in 1907, and that Tulsa "was also a deeply segregated city," where housing ordinances kept Black residents out of white neighborhoods unless they worked there as domestics (DOJ report). In 1910 Oklahoma amended its constitution to add a literacy test. A "grandfather clause" exempted anyone entitled to vote on January 1, 1866, and their lineal descendants (Guinn v. United States, Justia).
  • June 21, 1915: Guinn v. United States. The U.S. Supreme Court held that the grandfather clause violated the Fifteenth Amendment. Because the clause could not be separated from the literacy test, the whole 1910 amendment fell (Justia).
  • 1916: The replacement registration law. Oklahoma's new statute made anyone who had voted in 1914 a permanent voter. Every other qualified voter had to register between April 30 and May 11, 1916, about 12 days, or lose the vote for good. A later window through June 30, 1916 was open only to people who had been absent or kept away by sickness or "unavoidable misfortune." This law stayed in force until 1939 (Lane v. Wilson, Justia). The state's 2001 Commission report describes "the systematic disfranchisement of the black electorate through constitutional amendment in 1910, reaffirmed through state statute in 1916," and says "The point was to keep a race, as a race, away from the polls" (2001 Commission Final Report, OHS PDF).
  • May 30, 1921: The trigger. In the Drexel Building, Dick Rowland, a Black shoe shiner, had an encounter with Sarah Page, a white elevator operator. The most common explanation is that he stepped on her foot. The Tulsa Tribune reported that Rowland had "attempted to rape Page." It also ran an editorial, now lost, reportedly titled "To Lynch Negro Tonight" (Ellsworth, OHS Encyclopedia).
  • May 31 to June 1, 1921: The massacre. The violence lasted about eighteen hours (OHS Encyclopedia). DOJ found that "as many as 10,000 white Tulsans" took part and called it "a coordinated, military-style attack on Greenwood." Police "deputized as many as 500 white men in less than 30 minutes." DOJ also found that Tulsa police and the National Guard "disarmed Black residents" and that "some participated in murder, arson and looting" (DOJ press release; DOJ report). The 2001 Commission found that public officials deputized "many men, all of them white" and that "Public officials provided firearms and ammunition to individuals, again all of them white" (Commission report).
  • Destruction. Thirty-five city blocks were destroyed (DOJ; Tulsa Historical Society). The Commission counted homes "credibly estimated to have numbered 1,256," burned along with almost every other structure in Greenwood (Commission report). The OHS Encyclopedia says "more than one thousand homes and businesses were destroyed," and that "the vast majority of Tulsa's African American population had been made homeless" (OHS Encyclopedia).
  • Deaths (disputed; see Section 4). The Tulsa Historical Society says contemporary reports of deaths "began at 36" (THS). The OHS Encyclopedia gives credible estimates of "fifty to three hundred" (OHS). The Commission's estimate is "between one and three hundred." Its forensic expert, Dr. Clyde Snow, could identify 38 victims and said "as many as 150 might have to be added" for one reason and "18 more for another" (Commission report). Tulsa City-County Library gives "150 to 300" (TCCL). DOJ says "hundreds" and gives no single number (DOJ).
  • Internment. The National Guard and police took Black residents into custody. DOJ says the Guard "did not confine any white resident to the camps" (DOJ report). According to THS, "Over 6,000 people were held at the Convention Hall and the Fairgrounds," some of them for up to eight days, and "more than 800 people were treated for injuries" (THS). Thousands of people spent the winter of 1921 to 1922 in tents (OHS).
  • June 1921 onward: The grand jury. According to the OHS Encyclopedia, "an all-white grand jury blamed Black Tulsans for the lawlessness." Rowland was exonerated, and "no whites were ever sent to prison for the murders and arson" (OHS). DOJ notes that the grand jury charged Police Chief Gustafson with failing to stop armed men "of both white and colored race," which it reads as a failure-to-protect theory (DOJ report).
  • 1921: Insurance and city claims denied. DOJ says "insurance companies denied Black residents of Greenwood compensation due to the 'riot clause' in their policies" (DOJ report). In the Commission report's prologue, State Rep. Don Ross writes: "While the official damage was estimated at $1.5 million, the black community filed more than $4 million in claims. All were denied." The city did approve two claims "for guns and ammunition taken" (Commission report). TCCL gives "an estimated $1.5 to 1.8 million in claims were made against the City of Tulsa" and says "Most were disallowed" (TCCL). The Tenth Circuit later noted that victims filed "over one hundred lawsuits" and that one reached the Oklahoma Supreme Court in 1923 (Alexander, 10th Cir.).
  • Summer 1921: The fire ordinance. The city passed a fire ordinance that barred rebuilding "frame structures" in the burned area. Landowners sued, arguing it was "a direct attempt to take property without due process of law" (Tulsa Tribune, September 1, 1921, held by the National Archives; DocsTeach/NARA). A court struck the ordinance down ("Cannot Enforce Fire Ordinance," Tulsa Daily World, September 2, 1921; DocsTeach/NARA). DOJ: the fire codes "priced residents out of the area, although a court later enjoined those provisions" (DOJ report). The Commission found that city authorities "acted initially to impede rebuilding," and that city and county governments paid much of the Red Cross relief cost but "neither contributed substantially to Greenwood's rebuilding" (Commission report).
  • May 22, 1939: Lane v. Wilson. The Supreme Court struck down the 1916 registration scheme as a substitute for the grandfather clause. It wrote that the Fifteenth Amendment "nullifies sophisticated as well as simple-minded modes of discrimination" (Justia). The 1916 law had governed Black voter registration in Oklahoma throughout 1921 and the years after.
  • 1997 to February 21, 2001: The state commission. The Legislature created the Tulsa Race Riot Commission in 1997 (HB 2468). Its final report was submitted February 21, 2001 (OHS learning page). It recommended "direct payments to riot survivors and descendants," a scholarship fund, an economic development zone and a memorial (Commission report). Its summary of government's role: "In some government participated in the deed. In some government performed the deed. In none did government prevent the deed. In none did government punish the deed." The state approved measures for "a memorial, scholarships and tax incentives" but no direct payments. In April 2002 a private group, Tulsa Metropolitan Ministry, distributed "nearly $28,000" to survivors (TCCL).
  • 2003 to 2005: Alexander v. Oklahoma. Survivors and descendants sued on February 24, 2003. The Tenth Circuit affirmed dismissal on September 8, 2004, under Oklahoma's two-year statute of limitations. It agreed that "extraordinary circumstances" justified tolling for decades after 1921: "at the time of the Riot the victims were powerless against the white majority," and "Meaningful access to the courts was denied, as was any ability to obtain damages for property losses." But it held those circumstances ended once 1960s civil rights legislation opened the courts (10th Cir. opinion). Rehearing en banc was denied 9 to 4 (SCOTUSblog; 391 F.3d 1155, vLex). The Supreme Court denied certiorari on May 16, 2005 (544 U.S. 1044).
  • May 19, 2021: Survivors testify to Congress. Viola Fletcher, 107, told a House Judiciary subcommittee: "I am 107 years old and have never seen justice," and "Open the courtroom doors to us" (House testimony PDF).
  • 2020 to June 12, 2024: Randle v. City of Tulsa. Survivors Lessie Benningfield Randle and Viola Fletcher, joined by the estate of Hughes Van Ellis Sr., brought public nuisance and unjust enrichment claims. The Oklahoma Supreme Court affirmed dismissal (2024 OK 40, 556 P.3d 612; eight justices concurring, one concurring in part and dissenting in part). It called the plaintiffs' grievance "legitimate" but said the harms "implicate[] generational-societal inequities that can only be resolved by policymakers--not the courts" (Randle opinion text, Syfert). (OSCN, the court's own site, blocked automated access, so a mirror was used.)
  • January 10, 2025: DOJ review. The Civil Rights Division released its review under the Emmett Till Unsolved Civil Rights Crimes Act. No prosecution is possible: the statutes of limitations have run, no living perpetrators could be found, and the Confrontation Clause is "an insurmountable barrier." AAG Kristen Clarke called it "a civil rights crime unique in its magnitude, barbarity, racist hostility" (DOJ press release; report).
  • June 2025: Local and federal proposals. Mayor Monroe Nichols proposed a private trust of about $100 million (asset goal $105 million) for scholarships, housing help and north Tulsa revitalization. It "would not give direct cash payments" (Fox 29/AP). H.R. 4228, introduced June 27, 2025, would pay each living survivor federal damages. It was referred to the Judiciary Committee (govinfo). This page does not confirm what happened to either proposal after that.
  • November 24, 2025. Viola Fletcher died at 111. KJRH reported that Lessie Benningfield Randle was the last living survivor (KJRH).

What this case shows

  1. Black Tulsans sought redress in a state that had deliberately limited their votes. The 1910 grandfather clause and its 1916 replacement were designed to exclude Black voters, as Oklahoma's own Commission acknowledged. The Supreme Court struck down both, in 1915 and 1939 (Guinn; Lane; Commission). The officials who denied claims, passed the fire ordinance and sat on the all-white grand jury answered to an electorate that had been shaped to exclude many Black citizens.
  2. A federal appeals court found that legal redress was effectively closed for decades. The Tenth Circuit, while ruling against the plaintiffs, wrote that the victims were "powerless against the white majority" and that "Meaningful access to the courts was denied." It accepted tolling into the civil rights era (Alexander). This is a court's own finding that formal legal rights did not work in practice for Black Tulsans.
  3. Every channel for compensation failed, and the state's own records show it. Insurers invoked riot clauses. More than $4 million in claims were filed against the city and denied, apart from guns and ammunition claims (DOJ; Commission). Government "neither contributed substantially to Greenwood's rebuilding," and the 2001 recommendation for direct payments was not adopted.
  4. When courts finally heard the cases, time had become the barrier. In 2004 the plaintiffs were told they had waited too long. In 2024 they were told the problem was for "policymakers--not the courts" (Randle). Survivors who could not get a fair hearing in 1921 were pointed to the political process, which for decades had been the arena where Black Oklahomans were weakest.
  5. The wealth loss lasted. Economic research (an NBER working paper, not yet confirmed as peer-reviewed here) finds the massacre "led to declines in Black home ownership, occupational status, and child education," and that the homeownership gap "actually widened in the second half of the 20th century" (Albright et al., NBER w28985). Property is a base for economic and civic power, and that base was destroyed and not restored.

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. The numbers are uncertain. Death estimates run from 36 to about 300, and property-loss figures differ ($1.5 million official, $1.5 to 1.8 million in claims per TCCL, more than $4 million per Rep. Ross). Mitigation: Give the range and say who says what. The argument does not depend on the high figures. Even the low counts, together with the undisputed 35 blocks and roughly 1,256 homes, show massive loss that was never compensated.
  2. The direct cause was mob violence, not voting law. Disfranchisement did not start the massacre, and no source here shows that a fully enfranchised Black electorate would have prevented it or won compensation. Mitigation: Agreed. The link to voting is indirect and contextual. The strong claim is narrower: the political and legal system Black Tulsans would have needed for redress had been built partly to exclude them, and both the state Commission and the Tenth Circuit say so in their own words.
  3. Some remedies did work. The landowners' lawsuit got the fire ordinance struck down, and Greenwood was partly rebuilt by its own residents (NARA/DocsTeach). Mitigation: True, and it belongs in the essay. It shows Black Tulsans used the courts with some success. But stopping one obstacle is different from getting compensation, and the rebuilding was "left to the victims."
  4. The later court rulings rest on neutral doctrine. Statutes of limitations and the limits of nuisance law apply to everyone, and both courts expressed sympathy. Mitigation: Fair. The 2004 and 2024 rulings are not evidence of present-day bias. The point is that delay caused by earlier exclusion was later counted against the victims. The Tenth Circuit itself conceded the early decades were extraordinary.
  5. Things changed. The state created a Commission, funded scholarships and a memorial, DOJ produced an official account in 2025, and Tulsa proposed a trust. Mitigation: Real progress in acknowledgment. Still, no government ever paid direct compensation to survivors, and by late 2025 only one known survivor remained.
  6. The sources differ on the riot clause and insurance. The insurance denials are well attested, but this page did not verify a specific appellate ruling on riot exclusions. Mitigation: Cite DOJ's statement and avoid naming a specific insurance case until it is verified.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • Black voting in Tulsa in 1921. How many Black Tulsans were actually registered and voting? Precinct or county registration data would show whether disfranchisement there was near-total or partial, and so how strong the political-power link is.
  • The insurance litigation. Which insurance suits reached appellate courts, and on what reasoning? This page did not verify the case often cited as Redfearn v. American Central Insurance Co.
  • Gustafson's trial. The outcome (reportedly a conviction for neglect of duty and removal from office) was not verified from a primary source here.
  • Remains. The City of Tulsa's ongoing mass-graves investigation (Oaklawn Cemetery, from 2020) could narrow the death-toll range. This page did not review its findings.
  • Later losses. How much of Greenwood's later decline came from urban renewal and highway construction from the 1960s on, as opposed to 1921? Untangling the two would sharpen the wealth claim.
  • Status of the 2025 proposals. What happened to the Nichols trust (funding target June 1, 2026) and H.R. 4228, and is Lessie Benningfield Randle still living as of October 2026?
  • What would weaken the voting link. Evidence that Black Tulsans held real electoral influence in 1921, for example decisive votes in city elections, would weaken the voting connection while leaving the redress record intact.
