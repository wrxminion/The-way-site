U.S. polio vaccination campaigns (1952 to 2022)

Evidence first. Conclusions revisable.

Descend into the record ↓
Sources checked 2026-10-19 · Preliminary research, not a final verdict
Report for revisionDownload research packet

Members only. Sign in to send a correction.

A charity-funded field trial led to a licensed polio vaccine in April 1955, a defective-batch disaster weeks later, a rushed federal funding fight, and a fall from over 21,000 paralytic cases in 1952 to none acquired in the U.S. after 1979, with a small known vaccine risk that ended only when oral vaccine was dropped.

In brief

In 1952, polio paralyzed more than 21,000 Americans. A charity, the March of Dimes, funded the research and the 1954 field trial involving about 1.8 million children. The vaccine was announced on April 12, 1955, but the government had no distribution plan, and a fight over who would pay for poor children's shots followed. Within weeks, vaccine from Cutter Laboratories caused polio in children and their contacts. Federal surveillance counted 204 cases and 11 deaths by October. Vaccination was paused and resumed May 13 under stricter lot testing. By 1961 reported cases had fallen to 161. Oral vaccine, adopted in the 1960s, carried a measured risk of about eight vaccine-linked paralysis cases a year until the U.S. moved to an all-injected schedule in 2000. The record shows a program that people funded, doubted and fixed in public view.

The record

Before the vaccine

  • Polio peaked in the U.S. in 1952: more than 21,000 paralytic cases (CDC Pink Book). Another source gives 57,628 reported cases, 3,145 deaths and 21,269 people left with mild to disabling paralysis for 1952. The two figures are consistent if one counts paralytic cases and the other all reported cases. (CDC Pink Book, polio chapter; Wikipedia, History of polio; both opened)
  • The National Foundation for Infantile Paralysis (the March of Dimes) raised money in small donations from millions of people. By 1955 it had put $25.5 million into research, including the Salk and Sabin work, and it paid for care of more than 80% of the nation's polio patients (Wikipedia, History of polio; the page does not cite a primary source for these figures).
  • Care was segregated. Warm Springs, Georgia, did not accept Black patients. The Foundation funded the Tuskegee Infantile Paralysis Center, one of few facilities treating Black children. (Scientific American, "Hidden Black Scientists Proved the Polio Vaccine Worked"; opened)

The field trial and licensing

  • The 1954 Francis field trial began April 26, 1954 and reached about 1.8 million children in 44 states: about 440,000 vaccinated, about 210,000 given a placebo, and 1.2 million observed. Scientific American gives "nearly 420,000" inoculated, "about 200,000" placebo and 1.2 million observed. The effort mobilized about 20,000 doctors, 40,000 nurses and 200,000 volunteers (Scientific American). (Wikipedia, Polio vaccine; Scientific American; both opened)
  • Results were announced April 12, 1955. Wikipedia reports the Salk vaccine as 60 to 70% effective against type 1 and over 90% against types 2 and 3, and 94% against bulbar polio. Tuskegee Institute's Carver Research Foundation shipped about 400,000 tubes of HeLa cells for the trial (Scientific American).
  • Federal planning was thin. The government had known for about two years a vaccine was coming but had no distribution plan. Secretary of Health, Education and Welfare Oveta Culp Hobby opposed federal management or compulsion. After pressure from Congress and President Eisenhower, she created grants to states to vaccinate poor children. Senator Barry Goldwater warned a federal program would be "socialized medicine by the back door." About four million children had been vaccinated by July 1955, and Hobby resigned that month. (Government Executive, "History Does Repeat Itself," 2020; opened. A journalistic account; it does not name the statute.)

The Cutter incident (April 1955)

  • In April 1955 the Surgeon General began receiving reports of paralysis about a week after children received Cutter Laboratories vaccine, starting in the injected limb. The vaccine was pulled and a technical committee was created in May to review lots (Wikipedia, Polio vaccine).
  • The failure was in inactivation: some lots from several manufacturers were not properly inactivated. A 1955 surveillance report names five manufacturers on its tables (Cutter, Lilly, Parke-Davis, Pitman-Moore, Wyeth), though the scan is hard to read. (Poliomyelitis Surveillance Report No. 46, Oct. 14, 1955, CDC Stacks; opened, OCR partly garbled)
  • The surveillance unit (Langmuir, Nathanson, Hall) reported as of Oct. 28, 1955: 204 cases linked to Cutter vaccine, including 11 deaths; 79 vaccinated people, 105 family contacts and 20 community contacts; 158 paralytic. Cases clustered in California and Idaho. Lots 6039 and 6058 had a rate of 152.3 cases per 100,000 inoculations, versus 31.7 for other school-clinic lots. Vaccination was suspended May 7, 1955 after more than four million inoculations, and all lots were released May 13 under revised safety standards. (Langmuir, Nathanson and Hall, American Journal of Public Health, 1956 (CDC Stacks copy); opened. Data described as preliminary.)
  • Wikipedia's Cutter Laboratories page reports that the NIH Laboratory of Biologics Control certified the vaccine, that staff member Bernice Eddy reported paralyzed monkeys in 1954 and her report was rejected by NIH director William Sebrell, and that a June 1955 congressional hearing blamed mainly that laboratory's lack of scrutiny and its excessive trust in National Foundation reports. Officials including Hobby and Sebrell lost their posts. (Wikipedia, Cutter Laboratories; opened. We did not verify this against the hearing record.)
  • In Gottsdanker v. Cutter Laboratories (Cal. App., 1960), per Wikipedia, the jury found Cutter not negligent but liable for breach of implied warranty. We did not open the opinion.

The 1955 to 1957 decline and the effect data

  • A 1955 Connecticut comparison of children 5 to 9: 106,120 vaccinated with 37 cases (34.9 per 100,000), 5 paralytic (4.7); 89,500 unvaccinated with 75 cases (83.8), 18 paralytic (20.1). (Report No. 46)
  • In New York State (ages 6 to 10) the paralytic attack rate was 4.0 per 100,000 among vaccinated and 20.9 among unvaccinated. (Langmuir et al. 1956)
  • Annual cases fell from nearly 58,000 to 5,600 by 1957 and 161 by 1961 (Wikipedia, History of polio; Polio vaccine). The Pink Book gives paralytic cases of 2,525 in 1960 and 61 in 1965. The CDC surveillance manual reports under 100 cases in 1965 and under 10 in 1973.

Sabin's oral vaccine and its known risk

  • Oral poliovirus vaccine (OPV) was introduced in 1961 (monovalent, 1961 and 1962; trivalent 1963). It largely replaced the injected vaccine (Wikipedia, Polio vaccine; CDC Pink Book).
  • OPV can cause vaccine-associated paralytic poliomyelitis (VAPP). From 1980 to 1999, 162 confirmed paralytic cases were reported, 154 (95%) VAPP, 6 imported wild and 2 indeterminate, about 8 a year. By 1973, VAPP cases outnumbered paralytic cases caused by wild poliovirus. The risk was about 1 case per 2.4 million OPV doses distributed. (Pink Book; CDC Surveillance Manual, chapter 12; both opened)
  • ACIP recommended two IPV then two OPV doses in 1997 and an all-IPV schedule in 2000 (surveillance manual). The Pink Book gives both 1999 and 2000; we use the manual's 2000 and flag the discrepancy. The last U.S.-acquired VAPP case was reported in 1999 (Pink Book). Enhanced-potency IPV was licensed in November 1987 (Wikipedia).
  • The last indigenous wild poliovirus cases were reported in 1979 in Amish communities; the last imported wild case was in 1993 (surveillance manual; Pink Book).
  • In July 2022 a vaccine-derived type 2 poliovirus case was identified in an unvaccinated young adult in Rockland County, New York, the second finding of community transmission since 1979. Wastewater detections continued into February 2023 (surveillance manual).

What this case shows

  1. A public problem was solved by a private charity first. The March of Dimes funded research and most patient care; ordinary donors, mostly in small amounts, were the funders. They paid, but they did not vote on trial design, safety checks or distribution. (Wikipedia, History of polio)
  2. The state was not ready when the answer arrived. The government had no distribution plan for two years, and the debate in 1955 over who pays for poor children's shots was held in cabinet and Congress, not at the door of the clinic. Parents saw shortages and waiting lists, and grants to states arrived only under pressure. (Government Executive)
  3. A regulator trusted the maker's records, and the harm was found by surveillance, not by oversight. The Laboratory of Biologics Control certified the vaccine; the Cutter cases were found by a newly created Surveillance Program (April 28, 1955). The public record that followed, with lots named and rates published, is an example of repairing trust by publishing numbers. (Langmuir et al. 1956; Wikipedia, Cutter)
  4. Costs and harms were counted, and a known risk was kept for decades. About 8 VAPP cases a year in 1980 to 1999 were weighed against the benefit of oral vaccine until the all-IPV schedule in 2000. Families of those paralyzed bore a risk the public could read about only if they looked. (Pink Book; surveillance manual)
  5. Who was included. Black scientists at Tuskegee helped produce the HeLa cell supply for the trial, while Black children had restricted access to polio care. We did not find uptake figures by race for the 1950s and do not claim a gap. (Scientific American)

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: The Cutter incident proves vaccine campaigns are dangerous. Mitigation: We disclose it with counts from each source. The 1956 surveillance report found 204 cases and 11 deaths linked to Cutter, concentrated in two lots, and found paralytic rates two to more than five times higher in unvaccinated children in 11 states and New York City. The regulator then added safety standards and lots were released again on May 13, 1955. Both facts stand together.
  2. Objection: Polio was already declining. Mitigation: Annual reports varied widely. The Connecticut and New York comparisons are within the same years and place vaccinated against unvaccinated children, and the drop in U.S. cases from 1957 to 1961 followed mass immunization. We did not test a counterfactual and the 1955 data are described as preliminary and not a controlled evaluation. (Report No. 46)
  3. Objection: The government should not have been involved at all (Hobby, Goldwater). Mitigation: We report their view as it was stated. Hobby opposed compulsion and federal control; the grant program was a compromise. The cost to taxpayers and the dollar amounts of the Polio Vaccination Assistance Act were not verified, so we make no cost claim.
  4. Objection: OPV's paralysis risk shows regulators accepted harm. Mitigation: The risk was measured and published. About 8 VAPP cases a year over 1980 to 1999 against near-zero wild cases. The switch to all-IPV in 2000 ended new U.S.-acquired VAPP, with three imported or immunocompromised cases since. (Surveillance manual)
  5. Objection: Secondary sources here are weak (Wikipedia, journalism). Mitigation: We opened two primary documents (1955 surveillance report; 1956 American Journal of Public Health article) and CDC manuals. Wikipedia material is flagged where it stands alone.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • The Polio Vaccination Assistance Act (1955) and the Vaccination Assistance Act (1962) were searched but not opened. Amounts, dates and public law numbers are unverified. The Government Executive account does not name a statute.
  • The 1960 Gottsdanker opinion and the June 1955 congressional hearing record were not opened. Claims about both come from Wikipedia.
  • Bernice Eddy's 1954 warning to NIH is from Wikipedia only.
  • The 1963 Nathanson and Langmuir paper was not read directly. Case counts (204, "over 250," 260) remain unreconciled.
  • Vaccine uptake by year, region and race in the 1950s and 1960s was not found. The 1955 Connecticut data are one state.
  • The Wikipedia figure of 1.8 million, 440,000 and 210,000 differs slightly from Scientific American's 420,000 and 200,000. The 1955 Francis report itself was not opened.
  • Cost to taxpayers and to families for vaccine in 1955 and 1962 was not found.
  • The Pink Book gives 1999 and 2000 for the all-IPV recommendation. An MMWR citation was not opened.
  • The Pink Book (August 2021) states recommendations may be out of date.
  • Would change our minds: a primary distribution plan or Act text showing federal funding was larger or smaller than described.
