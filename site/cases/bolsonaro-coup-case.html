After losing Brazil's October 2022 runoff by 1.8 points, Jair Bolsonaro never formally conceded. The electoral court rejected his coalition's challenge as unproven, crowds stormed the seat of government on January 8, 2023, and in September 2025 four of five Supreme Federal Tribunal justices convicted him of leading a plot to stay in power.

In brief

On October 30, 2022, President Jair Bolsonaro lost Brazil's runoff to Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva by 1.8 points and never formally conceded. His own Defense Ministry reported no evidence of fraud, and the electoral court rejected his coalition's challenge and fined it about $4.3 million for bad-faith litigation. On January 8, 2023, crowds stormed Congress, the presidential palace and the Supreme Federal Tribunal (STF). In September 2025, four of five justices on an STF panel convicted Bolsonaro and seven others of a plot to stay in power and sentenced him to 27 years and 3 months; the fifth justice dissented. He is now under house arrest on health grounds. The U.S. government called the trial a "witch hunt" and answered with sanctions and tariffs. Critics say the trial was political persecution; the judges say the evidence shows an armed plot. In October 2026 his son Flávio lost the next runoff to Lula and left Brazil for Miami while under a court order to surrender his passports. This is the first case in this collection from outside the United States. It asks what happens when a losing side contests a count, and how a court answers.

The record

This case is about how an election result is protected after the vote. It is not a judgment on Brazilian politics, on any party, or on whether Bolsonaro's government was good or bad. We take no side on his guilt beyond what the Court found, and we record the dissent and the objections. Brazilian court names are explained in plain words where they first appear.

Why a Brazil case matters to Americans. Brazil is the other very large democracy in the Americas. Like the United States, it had a sitting president lose a close race and then question the machines that counted the votes. A crowd entered the seat of government two years after January 6, 2021 in Washington, and Brazil's courts tried the people accused of planning it from the top. The U.S. government also took a side, with tariffs and sanctions over the trial. Readers can see what Brazil's courts wrote, compare it with what U.S. institutions did, and judge for themselves. We do not claim the two cases are the same.

What we could and could not open. We opened the STF's own news pages on the verdict, the sentences and the rejected appeals, and the electoral court's pages on the Defense Ministry report and the fine. We could not open the STF's full written judgment (published October 22, 2025), the Federal Police and prosecutor indictments, or the Defense Ministry report itself. CNN, France 24 and Courthouse News blocked automated reading. For those we rely on the court summaries and the news reports we did open. Items we saw only as search-result headlines are marked that way.

What is and is not confirmed about Flávio Bolsonaro (as of Nov. 5, 2026)

  • Confirmed in reports we opened: he lost the runoff and did not concede; his party said it acknowledged Lula's win; he was ordered to surrender his passports and not to leave Brazil; he then left and landed in Miami with his family. (RTE; AP via CBS)
  • Reported by unnamed officials only: a senior State Department official told CBS News the family "would receive U.S. visas." President Trump told reporters, "It would be OK with me." We found no published U.S. government document on his entry or visa.
  • Not confirmed: whether he handed in his passports before leaving (AP said it "remained unclear"), whether they were cancelled (one anonymous police source via AFP), and whether any arrest warrant has been issued for him. We found no report of a warrant for Flávio as of this date.
  • Early reports were wrong or changed. On October 27, Argentina's government said it had no record of his entry; AP later reported, citing Argentine officials and flight data, that the family passed through Argentina. A widely shared article that week said he "reportedly flees" and named a private jet from a single report; its own text said those details needed independent confirmation. (ABC Australia; colombiaone.com)
  • Different explanations of the order: Exame ties it to the betting-platform inquiry; Flávio says it rested on his brother Eduardo's public remark that he should leave if he lost. We did not see the order. Flávio denies wrongdoing.

What the courts found, in their own words

The STF summary of the First Panel's judgment says Justice Cármen Lúcia found that a criminal organization led by Bolsonaro carried out a plan to attack state institutions, especially the courts, and used a "digital militia" to spread attacks on the electoral system and the voting machines. It reports her saying the events of January 8, 2023 were the culmination of efforts to stir up the population. Justice Zanin found an armed criminal organization with a hierarchy and a division of tasks, aimed at keeping Bolsonaro in power through illegal acts. Moraes, proposing the sentences, said they should deter attempts to obstruct "democratic normality." (STF)

NPR reported that evidence shown in court included a plan code-named "Green and Yellow Dagger," part of which, to poison Lula and shoot Moraes, was drafted inside the presidential palace, and that Moraes said it "wasn't drafted in secret by terrorists." We did not read the underlying document. (NPR)

In the 2022 electoral case, Moraes, then president of the electoral court, found that the coalition's petition contained false claims (for example, that machines could not be identified) and offered no evidence for an extraordinary check of the machines. That ruling came before he became the rapporteur in the criminal case. (TSE)

Bolsonaro's lawyers deny that there was a plot and say the evidence did not link him to one. Justice Fux dissented. (NPR; Al Jazeera)

Why it matters here: U.S. connections

This section records documented links between Brazil's story and U.S. politics. It does not say the two countries' events are the same, and it does not say any American caused Brazil's events. Each item says who said it.

1964: the U.S. and Brazil's last coup. Brazil's military overthrew President João Goulart on April 1, 1964, starting 21 years of military rule. Declassified U.S. records collected by the National Security Archive show the Kennedy administration moved from pressuring Goulart toward preparing for his removal. Ambassador Lincoln Gordon's March 29, 1964 memo called for a "contingency commitment to overt military intervention." "Operation Brother Sam," approved under President Johnson, was a plan to send arms, ammunition and fuel, and possibly forces, if the coup met strong resistance. On April 1, Gordon reported the coup was "95% over," and the supplies were not needed. The Archive says parts of the record, including CIA covert operations from 1961 to 1964, remain classified. The documented fact is U.S. planning and readiness to support a coup; the record we opened does not show U.S. forces taking part. (National Security Archive)

2019: a close alliance. President Trump hosted Bolsonaro at the White House on March 19, 2019, said he had done "a very outstanding job," backed Brazil's bid to join the OECD, and said he was "very strongly" considering support for Brazil as a "major non-NATO ally." Bolsonaro's son Eduardo has met Trump and Steve Bannon repeatedly since 2019, per NBC News. (Defense News; NBC News)

2022: fraud claims on both sides of the border. NBC News reports that Bannon said before the 2022 vote that Bolsonaro would lose only if "the machines" stole it, and afterward posted "Lula stole the election." Former U.S. diplomat Scott Hamilton told NBC the U.S. example of election denial was a template Bolsonaro likely would not have devised alone. That is one observer's view; we found no document showing coordination. (NBC News)

January 2023: the January 6 comparison, as others drew it. Graham Brookie of the Atlantic Council cited "undeniable similarities," including imagery and online calls to action. Both movements rested on claims that voting machines were rigged. Observers also named differences: Trump was still president on January 6 and urged supporters to march to the Capitol, while Bolsonaro had already left office and was in Florida; the Brasília attack came on a Sunday, when few officials were in the buildings. Christopher Garman of Eurasia Group called it "a little bit of a different animal." (NBC News)

Florida, 2022 to 2023. Bolsonaro flew to Florida in late December 2022 and skipped the January 1 inauguration. Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez said "The U.S. must cease granting refuge to Bolsonaro in Florida." National Security Adviser Jake Sullivan said any request from Brazil would be evaluated under legal precedent. Bannon called the Brasília protesters "freedom fighters." President Biden condemned the attack. (AP via Fox 35; NBC News)

2025 to 2026: U.S. government actions on the case.

  • July 2025: the U.S. sanctioned Justice Moraes under the Global Magnitsky Act, citing his handling of the trial and his orders against online accounts, and added a 40% tariff on Brazilian goods, for 50% in total. After the verdict, Trump called the trial a "witch hunt" and Secretary of State Rubio said the U.S. would "respond accordingly." Brazil's Foreign Ministry said it would not be intimidated. (Bloomberg Law via CBS; NPR; Al Jazeera)
  • November 2025: the White House said it would remove the 40% tariff on some imports, including beef and coffee. (Bloomberg Law via CBS)
  • December 12, 2025: the Treasury removed Moraes, his wife and her institute from the sanctions list. An unnamed senior official said "lawfare conditions in Brazil are improving." (Bloomberg Law via CBS)
  • June 2026: Eduardo Bolsonaro, a U.S. green card holder living in Texas, was convicted in Brazil of lobbying the U.S. government for the sanctions and tariffs. (AP via Al Jazeera)
  • October 2026: Trump said Brazil's election "looked like another rigged election," without giving evidence; Brazil's vice president rejected that. Justice Dino ordered police to look into possible foreign funding of Flávio Bolsonaro's campaign; these are allegations, not findings. A senior State Department official said Flávio's family would receive U.S. visas. (ABC Australia; AP via CBS)

What this case shows

  1. Several bodies checked the count, including one run by the losing president's own government. The Defense Ministry, the TSE, the federal accounts court (which checked 604 machines) and the Brazilian Bar Association each reported no fraud, according to AP. The Defense report did not call the system perfect: it said the tabulation system's protection against malicious code could not be certified. (AP via click2houston; TSE)
  2. A challenge was heard, decided in about a day, and penalized when it offered no proof. The coalition had a legal route; the court answered with reasons and a fine. How fast and how hard a court should answer a losing candidate is argued below. (TSE; Anadolu)
  3. The people accused of planning it, not only the crowd, were tried. The conviction reached a former president, two former defense ministers, a navy commander and a justice minister. Most of the 1,190 people held responsible for January 8 were rank and file. (STF; Congresso em Foco)
  4. The first story is often wrong. In one week, Flávio Bolsonaro was reported in Argentina, then denied entry by Argentina, then confirmed by AP to have passed through Argentina and Ecuador to Miami. A reader needs to know which parts were official at each step. (RTE; ABC Australia; AP via CBS)
  5. A foreign government's response is part of the record. The U.S. called the trial a "witch hunt," sanctioned the lead justice and raised tariffs, then lifted some of both. Brazil then convicted a U.S. resident for lobbying for those measures. Americans can ask what role their own government should have in another country's court case. (NPR; Bloomberg Law via CBS; AP via Al Jazeera)

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: Moraes was a named target of the alleged plot, then judged it, and he had led the earlier electoral case, so the process was not neutral. Our answer: This is a serious objection. NPR reported that part of the plan was to shoot Moraes, and he was the rapporteur. We did not open a full statement of the critics' legal argument or Brazilian legal commentary on whether a target may judge. The conviction was by four of five justices, and the dissent is recorded. We list it as an open question.
  2. Objection: Fux dissented, saying the case belonged before the full eleven-member Court, not a five-member panel. Our answer: NPR reports that Fux urged acquittal on all five counts and said the case should go to the full Court. The STF's own summary says Fux voted to convict two defendants of one crime and to acquit the rest. The two accounts differ on detail; we show both.
  3. Objection: A 27-year sentence is out of proportion. Our answer: The STF found that Bolsonaro led an armed criminal organization and that the plan included killings. Whether those findings are right is what the dissent and the appeals contest. Bolsonaro's lawyers deny the plot. We did not read the evidence.
  4. Objection: Law 15.402/2026 shows Congress thinks the January 8 sentences were too harsh. Our answer: Congress did override Lula's veto by wide margins. Supporters call it correcting excess; opponents call it an indirect amnesty. Moraes suspended it in one case pending the full Court, and we do not know whether the full Court has ruled. (Congresso em Foco; El Mostrador)
  5. Objection: The U.S. president and the Bolsonaro family say the trial and the passport order are political persecution. Our answer: We report those statements as claims by named people. Flávio says the order is persecution; the investigators' stated concern is betting-platform money, which he denies. A suspicion in a news report is not a finding.
  6. Objection: Week-old news cannot be sorted this fast. Our answer: Agreed. Every Flávio item is labeled with who said it, and this section will be rewritten when the STF, the Federal Police, the U.S. government or Flávio himself says more.

Open questions: what would change our minds

  • The STF's full written judgment and each justice's vote on each count. Not read.
  • The Defense Ministry report and its technical annex. Not read; we rely on AP and the TSE.
  • The evidence behind "Green and Yellow Dagger" and the other plot documents the court cited.
  • Exactly how Fux voted on each count, and his reasons for sending the case to the full Court.
  • The Federal Police and prosecutor indictments, and the exact dates and reasons for the November 2025 custody and finality.
  • The text of the passport order against Flávio Bolsonaro, whether he complied, and whether a warrant has been issued.
  • Any U.S. government statement or record on his visa or entry.
  • The legal arguments that Moraes should not have judged the case.
  • Whether the full STF has ruled on Law 15.402/2026.
  • The text of the 2023 TSE ruling that barred Bolsonaro from running (we saw only headlines).
