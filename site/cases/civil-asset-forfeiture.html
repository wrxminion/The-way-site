Civil asset forfeiture (United States, 1984 to 2026)

Federal and state law lets agencies seize property tied to crime without a conviction, and a federal fund took in $2.287 billion in fiscal 2025; courts and legislatures have tightened some rules while law enforcement and reform groups still disagree about incentives, innocent owners and who must prove what.

In brief

In civil forfeiture, the government sues property and does not need to convict its owner. Federal law since 2000 puts the burden on the government to show by a preponderance of the evidence that property is forfeitable, and it adds hardship release and an innocent-owner defense. The Justice Department's Assets Forfeiture Fund took in $2.287 billion in fiscal 2025 and $2.422 billion in 2024. The Supreme Court held in 2019 that states are bound by the Excessive Fines Clause, and held in 2024 that no separate early hearing is required for seized cars. Four states, by one advocacy group's count, have ended civil forfeiture. Sheriffs say forfeiture helps fund enforcement and slow the drug trade. Reformers say agencies profit from seizures that owners cannot afford to contest. The record shows rules that changed because legislators and courts took up the question. It does not yet show how many owners were never charged, because the federal fund total does not break that out.

The record

What civil forfeiture is

  • In civil (in rem) forfeiture the government sues the property, not the owner. Property can be forfeited even when its owner was never charged. Criminal forfeiture, by contrast, follows a conviction of the owner. Justice Thomas described the civil form in 2017 as proceedings that can end in forfeiture "even when the owner is personally innocent," while noting that some statutes, including Texas's, provide an innocent-owner defense. (Leonard v. Texas, statement respecting denial of certiorari, March 6, 2017; opened)
  • Federal law runs mainly through 18 U.S.C. 983, added by the Civil Asset Forfeiture Reform Act of 2000 (Public Law 106-185, enacted April 25, 2000). The government must prove by a preponderance of the evidence that property is subject to forfeiture, and for property used in a crime must show "a substantial connection between the property and the offense." Claimants who say they are innocent owners must prove that defense by a preponderance. Notice of a non-judicial forfeiture is due within 60 days of seizure (90 days when state or local police hand a seizure to federal agencies). A claimant files a sworn claim with no bond, and the government generally has 90 days to file a complaint. A court may appoint counsel for a financially unable claimant who already has appointed counsel in a related criminal case, and for a primary residence must ensure representation by a Legal Services Corporation attorney. A claimant can seek release of seized property for substantial hardship, with exclusions for contraband, currency and evidence. (18 U.S.C. 983, Cornell LII; Public Law 106-185, GovInfo; both opened)
  • Public Law 106-185 section 2 has no statement of congressional findings, so we cannot quote a stated purpose from the enacted text. (GovInfo; opened)

Equitable sharing: how federal and local forfeiture connect

  • Equitable sharing lets federal agencies share forfeited property with state and local agencies that helped. In an "adoption," a local agency seizes property and asks a federal agency to take it over; the request is due within 30 days of seizure. In joint cases, shares track each agency's work. In the DOJ guide we opened, the federal share is generally 20 percent of net proceeds when a local agency did all the pre-seizure work, and shared funds must supplement and not replace budgets. That guide is undated in our copy (it points to a 2008 policy manual and a 1984 start), so current percentages should be checked. (DOJ equitable sharing guide, mirrored by U.S. Attorney's Office, Rhode Island; opened)
  • Attorney General Eric Holder's January 2015 order barred most federal adoptions of local seizures. Attorney General Jeff Sessions reinstated adoptions with some restrictions in July 2017. According to the Institute for Justice (an advocacy group), adoptions fell from 26 percent of equitable-sharing forfeitures in 2014 to 5 percent in 2016, and have stayed near 10 percent since 2019. (Institute for Justice, Policing for Profit 4, equitable sharing chapter; opened; we did not open the Holder or Sessions orders themselves)
  • The National Sheriffs' Association opposed the 2015 order, saying it weakens local ability to fight drug crime, shifts costs to local governments and ends a tool that has run about 30 years. (National Sheriffs' Association release, Jan. 26, 2015; opened)

State law

  • Four states are described by the Institute for Justice as having ended civil forfeiture in favor of criminal forfeiture: New Mexico, Nebraska, North Carolina and Maine. Maine's repeal (LD 1521) took effect in July 2021 without the governor's signature, allows forfeiture without conviction only in narrow cases (death, deportation, flight or abandonment), creates a prompt post-seizure hearing, requires public reporting, and limits use of equitable sharing to seizures of $100,000 or more in cash. IJ reports Maine agencies collected over $14 million through equitable sharing over two decades against just over $3 million forfeited under state law from 2009 to 2019. (Institute for Justice, July 13, 2021; opened)
  • New Mexico's HB 560 passed the Senate 37 to 0 in March 2015 and would require conviction and send proceeds to the general fund instead of police budgets. (Jurist, March 2015; opened; the article does not report the governor's signature)
  • Institute for Justice reports across 24 states that half of currency forfeitures were worth less than $1,678, against an estimated attorney cost near $3,300; that 71 percent of DOJ forfeitures were administrative (no court hearing); and that in three states with data, 62 to 76 percent of seizures were forfeited by default. (Institute for Justice, Policing for Profit 4, March 24, 2026; opened summary page only; advocacy source)

What this case shows

  1. Many owners cannot afford to ask a court. If half of cash forfeitures are under about $1,700 and a lawyer costs about twice that, the process may be too costly to use, which would leave small owners with a right they cannot exercise. The numbers come from an advocacy group's data and should be read as claims to be checked against state records. (IJ, 2026)
  2. The money is public but hard to follow. DOJ posts fund totals, but the page does not say how much came from cases with no conviction. Maine had to pass a law to require public reports, which suggests that the state-level picture was not easy to see. (DOJ; IJ 2021)
  3. Voters in several states changed the rules. New Mexico passed its bill 37 to 0 in the Senate, and Maine's repeal passed as a bipartisan bill. The topic has produced votes across party lines. We do not score any party. (Jurist; IJ 2021)
  4. Courts have set limits, narrowly. Timbs applies the Excessive Fines Clause to states, and Culley leaves states free to adopt retention hearings. Both rulings turned on the Constitution, not on the policy question of whether forfeiture should be civil at all. (Cornell LII)
  5. Incentives are named by justices on different sides. Thomas (2017) and Gorsuch (2024) both pointed to financial incentives and to the poor bearing the heaviest burden, while Sotomayor's dissent in Culley named seizures held for long periods. The majority in Culley did not decide the incentive question. (Cornell LII)

The strongest objections, and our answers

  1. Objection: Forfeiture takes the profits and tools of drug trafficking and funds enforcement. The National Sheriffs' Association says forfeiture revenue pays for equipment, training and officer safety and that a few isolated abuses should not end a 30-year program. Mitigation: We state this position in full. Evidence on how often forfeiture reduces crime is not in the sources we opened, so we make no claim either way.
  2. Objection: CAFRA and state laws already protect innocent owners. The 2000 Act put the burden of proof on the government, allows hardship release, and has an innocent-owner defense. Mitigation: These are real changes, and we quote them. Whether claimants can use them is the open question: the owner still has the burden on the innocent-owner defense, and the numbers on default and administrative forfeiture come from an advocacy group.
  3. Objection: The reform figures come from the Institute for Justice, which litigates against forfeiture. Mitigation: We label the source, use DOJ and court texts for core facts, and mark IJ numbers as secondary. A Heritage Foundation piece (2017) also argues for reform; we opened only that one non-IJ reform source and do not rely on it for numbers.
  4. Objection: The DOJ fund total is not "forfeitures of innocent people." Mitigation: Correct. The fund includes proceeds from convictions and large criminal cases. We say so. The figure shows scale and public posting, not the number or share of owners who were never charged.
  5. Objection: Culley shows the Court sees no due process problem. Mitigation: The Court held that a separate preliminary hearing is not required. It did not say current practice is ideal, and three justices dissented while two concurring justices raised doubts.
