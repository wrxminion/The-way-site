Justices on the retention ballot

Justices of California's Supreme Court and Courts of Appeal don't run against opponents. Voters decide whether each one is elected to a new term (yes or no).

What each court does

  • Supreme Court: The state's highest court, with seven justices. It has the final word on what California law and the state constitution mean. On the ballot you vote yes or no on keeping each justice; no one runs against them. A full term is 12 years. source: sos.ca.gov (Official Voter Information Guide)
  • Court of Appeal: The middle level of California's courts, in six districts. Its justices hear appeals from the trial (superior) courts, and its rulings can be reviewed by the Supreme Court. You vote yes or no on keeping each justice. A full term is 12 years. source: sos.ca.gov (Official Voter Information Guide)

Judges on your ballot

The California Supreme Court has one open seat. Horvitz & Levy, a law firm that follows the court, reported it had been vacant for more than eight months and that a third justice could be added to this ballot only if a new appointee were confirmed and sworn in within about four weeks, which it called unlikely. source: Horvitz & Levy

  • Associate Justice of the Supreme Court
    Kelli M. Evans source: sos.ca.gov (Secretary of State) source: Governor press release, Aug. 10, 2022 source: CalMatters, Oct. 31, 2026 (campaign against Evans) source: CalMatters source: GrowSF source: Horvitz & Levy    On the ballot in every county. Retention vote: yes or no. Named to the Supreme Court by Gov. Gavin Newsom, who announced the pick on August 10, 2022; the Commission on Judicial Appointments held its confirmation hearing in November 2022. This is her first time on the ballot; a yes vote keeps her for a 12-year term. CalMatters lists her earlier work as an Alameda County Superior Court judge, chief deputy legal affairs secretary to the governor, civil rights attorney, public defender and State Bar senior director. GrowSF, a voter guide that recommends yes votes on both justices (updated September 10, 2026), reports she does not appear in the Commission on Judicial Performance public discipline record. Opposition: the Peace Officers Research Association of California, a law enforcement union, is running a late campaign urging voters to remove her. CalMatters reports the union argues she is moving to reopen criminal cases it believes should stay closed, and that its website cites a blog's count of her dissents when the court declined to review criminal cases. The union's leaders told CalMatters the effort likely will not persuade enough voters. Evans declined to comment. Legal scholars quoted by CalMatters call such a campaign a threat to judicial independence, and one said it had not gotten traction. Only once, in 1986, have California voters removed Supreme Court justices in a retention vote.

Retention versus contested. Supreme Court and Court of Appeal justices are retention votes (yes or no). Superior Court seats here are contested races between two named candidates. Check your sample ballot for your county.

Los Angeles County: Superior Court

Four seats are on the November ballot. Each has two candidates, nonpartisan, six-year term, vote for one. These are runoffs between the top two finishers of the June 2 primary, which is how California fills a Superior Court seat when no one wins a majority. The county list does not mark incumbents.
