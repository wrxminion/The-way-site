Proposition 40: Billionaire taxOne-time 5% tax on the wealth of California billionaires, mainly for health care

Citizen initiative (constitutional amendment and statute): , placed on the ballot by voters who signed petitions (Initiative constitutional amendment and statute). Passes with a simple majority of the votes cast on it.

Read the full text of the measure: Official Voter Information Guide (PDF) ↗

In plain words

"Net worth" means what you own (stocks, businesses, investments) minus what you owe. This charges billionaires who lived in California on Jan. 1, 2026 a one-time tax of up to 5% of their covered assets, such as businesses, stocks, art and collectibles. Real estate and some pensions and retirement accounts are not counted. It is due in 2027, or can be spread over five years at a higher total. 90% must go to public health care; 10% to education, food aid and running the tax. The money would not count under the state spending limit or the school funding formula. If Prop. 41 or Prop. 42 gets more yes votes, a court could block Prop. 40 even if it passes. (lao.ca.gov (Legislative Analyst's Office) ↗)

Example

Biggest change

  • One-time tax of 5% of net worth on billionaires who were California residents on Jan. 1, 2026; 90% for public health care. source: lao.ca.gov (Legislative Analyst's Office)

Who it affects most: California's billionaires, who would pay it, and people who rely on Medi-Cal and other public health care programs.

Cost to the state: Temporary revenue increase of tens of billions of dollars over several years; possible ongoing state income tax losses, likely under $1 billion a year, if billionaires leave or change their income; administration costs of tens of millions a year paid from the tax (Legislative Analyst).

Why people want it, and why others object

Why this is on the ballot

Voters put this on the ballot by signing a petition. It changes the state constitution, so only another statewide vote can change it back. It passes with a simple majority.

  • SEIU-United Healthcare Workers West put it on the ballot. It projects about $100 billion over five years and says federal Medi-Cal cuts will close hospitals and clinics. source: CalMatters

Touches the state's tax-limit rules: California's spending limit and local tax votes, in plain words, explained.

Conflicts with another measure

  • If Proposition 41 or Proposition 42 receives more yes votes than Proposition 40, courts could find a conflict that blocks Proposition 40 even if it wins a majority (Legislative Analyst). source: lao.ca.gov (Legislative Analyst's Office)

Why supporters want it

  • Official argument in favor: "billionaires pay much lower rates than working families"; it would raise about $100 billion to replace federal health care cuts. Signed by Suzanne Jimenez (SEIU-United Healthcare Workers West), Victoria Barron (Planned Parenthood of Pacific Southwest) and Denise Robb (AFT Local 1521). source: voterguide.sos.ca.gov (Secretary of State)

Why opponents object

  • Official argument against: "This measure is a one-time tax that only provides temporary funds." Opponents cite a Stanford economists' study projecting a $25 billion revenue loss as wealthy residents leave. Signed by René Bravo, M.D. (California Medical Association), Francisco Silva (California Primary Care Association) and Dr. Debra Schade (California School Boards Association). source: voterguide.sos.ca.gov (Secretary of State)

Who backs each side

  • For: SEIU-UHW, U.S. Sen. Bernie Sanders, Teamsters California, AFSCME California, California Labor Federation, California Democratic Party. Against: California Business Roundtable, California Medical Association, California Teachers Association, Planned Parenthood, California Professional Firefighters, State Building and Construction Trades, Chris Larsen, Ron Conway. The official argument against says Gov. Gavin Newsom and both candidates for governor oppose it. source: CalMatters
  • Money (Secretary of State, through Oct. 6, 2026): committees in support reported $32,213,823 (mostly SEIU-UHW's "Yes on 40" committee, $31,510,329). Committees in opposition reported $99,596,279 (largest: "No on Prop 40 - Doctors, Teachers, Small Businesses..." $88,721,279; "Golden State Promise" $10,450,000). source: sos.ca.gov (Secretary of State)

Official summary by the Attorney General (tap to open)
Imposes one-time tax of up to 5% on certain taxpayers with covered assets valued over $1 billion; covered assets include businesses, securities, art, collectibles, and intellectual property, but exclude real property and some pensions and retirement accounts. Allocates 90% of these tax revenues for health care, 10% for food assistance or education-related programs; prohibits using revenues to replace existing funding for these purposes. Exempts such tax revenues from constitutional requirements for school funding, budget reserves, and state spending limit.
(sos.ca.gov (Official Voter Information Guide) ↗)
